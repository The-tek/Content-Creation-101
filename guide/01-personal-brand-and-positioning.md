# Module 1: Personal brand and positioning

*Decide what you want to be known for, prove it, repeat it, and give it time.*

This module teaches the foundation of a personal brand. A brand is a link in people's heads between you and one thing, and trust is what turns that link into money. You will learn how to pick a destination, build real proof, choose a narrow niche and a clear position, stand out with a belief most people disagree with, and build a world your audience wants to live in. You will also learn how to last in the age of AI with taste and trust. Everyone who has done it agrees on one thing: it takes years, and the people who keep going win.

## 1.1 Why personal brands win now

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** People trust people faster than they trust companies, and trust is what turns attention into money. A personal brand is not your business. It is the way people find you and the reason they believe you.

### Trust is the whole strategy

- A personal brand is a reputation people carry in their heads. If you share good ideas for long enough, people start to trust you. That trust is the whole strategy, because people buy from someone they trust. Money follows trust.
- Think of a personal brand as a way to get people to your door, not as the business itself. A founder uses one to find users for a startup. A coach uses one to find clients. Nearly anything can be sold through a person people trust.
- Without that trust, a business has to win people over fast with hard-sell tactics like countdown timers and pushy pages. That pushes people toward quick money and shady tricks. With trust, your content has already done most of the selling, so you can stop obsessing over landing pages and headlines.
- If you are a beginner, do not spend weeks on your niche, bio and banner. They matter later, but plenty of people with a blank profile picture grow just fine. Good content, posted for a long time, matters more.
- For a founder, the most valuable thing you build is not views or cash flow. It is the emotional connection between you and your customers. Nobody understands that connection better than the founder.
- Some creators go further and say a strong personal brand is the best marketing investment you can make right now. Treat that as an opinion, not a proven fact.

### People follow people, not companies

- On social media, people follow Elon Musk and Steve Jobs. They do not follow Tesla or Apple the same way. The accounts people actually care about belong to humans.
- Big companies have noticed. One creator says the CEO of PayPal hired a head of content, for a job paying around \$180,000 a year, to build his personal brand and point that attention at PayPal. That is one person's claim, but it shows how seriously CEOs now take this.
- Tony Robbins runs many programs, but the brand people know is Tony Robbins. A CEO who builds a personal brand early gets ahead because they own a way to reach people without paying for ads.
- Some creators used to say you should build a company brand and a personal brand together. Now they say to put almost all your effort into the personal brand and then point it at your companies.
- To decide between a personal account and a company account, ask two questions. First: does the buyer need to be taught how your product solves their problem? Candy, clothes or a photo filter app need no teaching, so entertainment that creates a feeling works. Tax help, coaching, agencies and complex software do need teaching, so you educate. Second: what is the fastest way to earn their trust? Almost always it is a person, because people trust people faster than faceless brands.
- A company account wins mostly in consumer goods and retail, where a shopper has to remember a brand name in a store. Even there, founders often host the content themselves, like the founder of the snack brand Midday Squares.
- Early-stage companies are mostly built on founder-led content. People prefer to hear from a person than from a brand page, unless the product is truly groundbreaking.
- The same goes for things like courses, communities and creator products. They usually sell through long posts on X and long videos on YouTube first, because people buy from someone they already trust.

### Founder-led, brand-led and the whole team

- Founder-led content is written in the first person: opinions, stories, "how I'm building this", behind the scenes and lessons learned. It builds trust fastest because people follow people. The risk is that the founder's personal opinions can sometimes hurt the company.
- Brand-led content is written in the third person: launches, campaigns and announcements. It scales better and is worth more if you ever sell the business, but it is harder for people to relate to.
- Most companies should run both. Say the founder account shares why you are building the product and what you are learning, while the company account handles launches and updates.
- Employees can build their own brands too. Operators who compared notes call this a massive advantage for companies with about 20 or more people. Teams on X every day also find talent and useful connections, because X works as a huge networking place.
- The account itself is not what drives growth anymore. The world around it does: the founder, the team, creators and customers all reach more people than a brand account or an ad. Marketers at several well-known software companies say that around 30% or more of their Meta ads should run through real people's accounts instead of the company's own.
- Personal branding has also changed. The first version was about attention: look at my life. The newer version is about teaching. Creators now replace the mentors and teachers people once relied on for careers, money and skills.

### The content CEO

- An old-style CEO is stuck firefighting in meetings, and the business depends on them like a house of cards. A "content CEO" spends time building trust, reach and a team that can do more than one person can alone. As one line puts it, you can't run a business today with a playbook from ten years ago.
- A founder who wants to do this needs answers to four worries. "No one will listen to me": build an authority engine that proves you know your field. "I don't have time": make one piece of content and cut it into many smaller ones. "How will this make money": connect content to sales in a simple way. "I'll have to do it all alone": get a team that multiplies your effort.
- Every business today is partly a media company, because content is how people find you. You still should not hand it all off straight away. The founder has to understand content deeply first, or they cannot judge anyone else's work.
- Who your audience is matters more than how big it is. One investor never built an audience of other investors. His followers are real people who want to learn and grow, and they are also real customers of the companies he backs. That makes his reach much more useful.
- The opposite mistake is common. Junior staff at investment firms are told to build an audience and end up followed by other junior staff who like each other's posts. Announcements then only reach their own small circle. A firm's content needs a real reason to exist for people outside the industry.
- One view says investing is splitting into two sides: a few huge firms, and people with a clear extra value, such as an audience that gives founders access to customers. Money alone is no longer a reason for founders to pick you. Example: one investor with a platform posted a portfolio announcement video that got 50,000 views in 24 hours, and ran a live audio chat with several hundred people. That gave the founder reach, which is why small checks from him could still get into good deals.

### A personal brand is not the same as being a creator

- A personal brand is what people think of you when you are not in the room. You build it to help your career.
- There are levels. Level 0 is everyone who is online. Level 1 is a brand that is an extension of what you do and does not define you, and this is where most people should stop. Level 2 is when you are known for it and earn some money from it. Level 3 is when the brand is bigger than you, like Tim Ferriss or GaryVee.
- One community of brand builders shares results from its members: one raised his first investment cheque for an AI software company at about 6,000 followers, another worked with Bill Gates's daughter after 185 or more posts, another made \$80,000 in a month from his agency with many leads coming from his brand, and another landed a \$20,000 sale to a business. These are the community's own claims. The point is that a brand improves a little with each post and the gains stack up.

### Frameworks

**The trust triangle**

1. Growth: do what works to attract people.
2. Authenticity: share your real beliefs, so you attract the right people.
3. Authority: show you know your field. Teaching in a way that changes how people act is arguably the most important of the three.

**Personal account or company account?**

1. Question 1: does the buyer need to be taught how your product works? If yes, educate. If no, entertain and create a feeling.
2. Question 2: what is the fastest way to earn their trust? Almost always it is a person.
3. Pick your money game: advertising (brand deals, which need a personal brand) or conversion (selling your own product, which can work on any kind of account).
4. Then study only accounts in your own category and niche. Find one, look at who they follow, list 10 to 20 similar accounts, and sort them by their best-performing posts.
5. Check whether an account really teaches: after reading its last 10 posts, could you apply something yourself? If not, it is entertainment dressed up as education.

### Examples

- **The PayPal job post:** One creator says the CEO of PayPal hired a head of content, paid about \$180,000 a year, to build his own personal brand and send that attention to PayPal.
  <br>*Why it works:* If true, it shows that even huge companies see a founder's personal brand as a way to reach people. The number is that creator's own claim.
- **The investors with the wrong audience:** Junior staff at investment firms are told to build an audience. They end up followed by other investors, so their announcements only reach their own small circle. One investor built an audience of real people instead, and those people are customers of the companies he backs.
  <br>*Why it works:* Who is in your audience matters more than how many. A crowd of peers cannot buy from the businesses you care about.

### Common mistakes

- Building only a company account when your buyer needs to be taught and needs to trust a person.
- Building an audience of peers who like each other's posts instead of real customers.
- Handing content off before the founder understands it deeply.
- Using pushy hard-sell tactics because you never built trust first.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say to put all your effort into the personal brand and point it at the company. Others say that in 2026 companies should run a founder account and a brand account side by side, with the brand account handling launches and updates. A third group adds that a company account can win in consumer goods and retail, usually with founders still hosting it.

### Try it

Pick any business you know: yours, a client's or one you admire. Write answers to the two questions: does the buyer need to be taught how it works, and what is the fastest route to their trust? Then list the people in that business (the founder, team members) who could each run a personal account, and one topic each could post about.

## 1.2 Start from the end: what you want to be known for

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Before you make any content, decide where your brand is going: what you want to be known for, and what that reputation should get you. Then you can judge every post by one question: does it move me closer or further away?

### Foundation before tactics

- Beginners are flooded with advice like "copy these formats, steal this hook". People who have built personal brands for years say none of it works without a strong foundation. So before your next post, or your first one, know where you are going.
- Think of getting in a car with no destination. You can't pick a route, and you can't tell whether you are getting closer or further away. A brand with no destination works the same way.
- Building a brand is closer to a formula than to an art. After studying hundreds of top personal brands, including Tony Robbins and Martha Stewart, the same formula shows up in almost any field.
- Most people jump straight to posting and growing without answering the basics: what does this brand stand for, what makes it different, why is this person worth listening to, and what world are they inviting the audience into?
- That is why most personal brands fall apart. They are built on mud, so they sink after a few months.

### Known for and paid for

- Start with one question: when you wake up 20 years from now, what do you want to be known for and paid for?
- Draw a grid with how well you are paid on one side and how well you are known on the other. Not known and not paid is where everyone starts. Paid but not known describes experts who get paid to coach or speak but whom nobody has heard of. Known but not paid describes people with millions of followers who can't turn it into money. The goal is to be known and paid for the right thing.
- The biggest mistake is leaning your ladder against the wrong wall. You make content about things you won't want to be known for in 10 years, or you grow an audience in a niche you hate. Then you win, hate your audience, and want to start over.
- Check that your topic fits your goals 25 and even 50 years out. If you are in your 20s, picture yourself at 50. What are your 5, 15 and 30 year visions? If you build a brand around making content and end up hating content, you won't be glad you built it.
- If you only chase the algorithm instead of what you actually want, you get stuck as "that one person" and keep making content you start to hate. Deciding your 10-year answer first is more useful than any tip about hooks.
- A personal brand costs you time, money and privacy. Without a strong reason why, you are far less likely to stick with it when hard times come.

### Keep a north star

- Ask yourself: if I hit my follower goal (10K or 25K), what would actually change in my life? Remember why you make content, so you are not shooting in the dark.
- Your reason can be personal. One creator says content was a self-improvement tool that helped them get past social anxiety, back when they were scared to raise a hand in class.
- Ask "to serve who?" and start from the end goal. Founders who build a brand only as a way to get leads can end up trapped in that format. Founders who share what they are building out of curiosity attract more people.
- Be clear with yourself, and with anyone you hire, about the main goal: views, sales of a small niche product, or simply paying the bills while making what you love. A creator who asks for more views and then dislikes making broader videos ends up like the band that wanted a big audience and then hated being popular.

### Build a mission into the brand

- Most people quit because they can't picture their brand in 6 months, let alone 2 years. They post random topics, get no traction, and decide they have nothing to say.
- A mission fixes this. It is a north star made of two points. Point A is one sentence about the life your reader wants to escape. It is the problem, the thing you are against. Point B is one sentence about the life you help them move toward. Your mission is to help as many people as possible get from A to B, and every idea you post should sit somewhere between the two.
- Share that mission in your content. People follow momentum, so they follow people who are clearly moving somewhere. For example, one business YouTuber's mission is to help people build a business they don't end up hating. His audience can repeat it for him.
- MrBeast's business works the same way. In his content he obsesses over making the perfect video. In his money he has a strong wish to help people in need. Those two missions make each video mean more than a typical creator's.

### Frameworks

**Work backwards from the outcome**

1. What outcome do you want from this brand? Be specific.
2. What would you have to be known for to get that outcome? This is the reputation you need.
3. What do you need to do to be known for that? You are known for doing, not only for talking.
4. What do you need to learn to do those things? These are the skills to build before you start.
5. Then test every action: is it moving me closer to the outcome or further away?

**Mission as Point A to Point B**

1. Point A: one sentence about the life your reader wants to escape.
2. Point B: one sentence about the life you help them reach.
3. Mission: help as many people as possible go from A to B.
4. Every idea and every post should sit somewhere on that path.

### Examples

- **The ladder on the wrong wall:** Say you grow a big audience making videos about stock tips, but in 10 years you don't want to talk about stocks at all. You win, then hate your own audience and want to start over.
  <br>*Why it works:* This is a made up example. Winning on the wrong topic is a loss you only notice after years, which is why the 10-year question comes before every tactic.
- **A Point A and Point B:** Say you help busy parents with money. Point A: "stressed about bills every month and never getting ahead". Point B: "calm about money, with savings growing." A post about a monthly budget sits on the path between them. A post about a celebrity's car does not.
  <br>*Why it works:* This is a made up example. A mission works as a filter: you can check any post idea against it.

### Common mistakes

- Jumping to hooks and formats before you know your destination.
- Picking a topic you won't want to talk about in 10 years.
- Chasing the algorithm until you are stuck making content you hate.
- Not telling yourself, or your team, what the main goal is.

### Try it

Write four lines, one per question in "Work backwards from the outcome": the outcome, what you must be known for, what you must do, what you must learn. Then write Point A and Point B as one sentence each. Finally, look at your last 10 posts (or 10 you plan to make) and mark each one "closer" or "further".

## 1.3 Credibility first: do the thing, then talk about it

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A personal brand stands on what you have actually done. Get real proof first, or be honest that you are still learning, and then talk about it. Faking expertise gets spotted.

### You can't shortcut credibility

- A personal brand rests on what you have really done: your wins, your failures and your experiences. There is no real shortcut around that.
- People online are more skeptical than ever. Within seconds of seeing you, viewers wonder: can I trust this person, are they legit? Many have been burned by fake experts, so a promise alone no longer works. Share the promise and the proof.
- Expertise earns trust, but it takes more than expertise to get long-term loyalty. That comes from building a world around your brand, which lesson 11 covers.
- Most people post to get credibility, and that is backwards. Get proof in real life first, then use it online. One well-known business YouTuber built and sold 3 software companies before his first post.
- A leader has followers. If you are not a leader in real life, you won't get followers online. So answer this plainly: what do you help people with, and why should anyone follow you for it?

### Do cool things first

- Here is a joke recipe for 1 million views in your first week: spend years doing cool things, start posting about it, then blow up. The joke is true. Build real skills, experience, a network and knowledge before you post, and people can tell you are not faking.
- The best growth trick is having interesting things to share. They come from access, lived experience, skills, results and personality. If a brand isn't growing, it is usually missing these, or it has a packaging problem: a good thing explained badly.
- Don't pretend. Only talk about things you have done and are good at. One creator tried to build a personal brand at 16 and it flopped, because there was nothing useful to say, no skills and no value to give.
- Fake gurus are easy to spot. They have no credentials, they never did anything interesting, and their advice is generic. The business YouTuber mentioned above mastered software, coaching and investing before making general business content.
- If you work for someone with a brand, shine a light on the cool things they have already done. One creator ran TikTok for a big entrepreneur's team and built the whole strategy on showing what that entrepreneur and his family had already achieved.
- Many creators only make content about content. A better split is filming about 3 hours a week and spending the rest of your time doing interesting things, because that is where new stories, comparisons and experience come from.
- Switch from "how to" content to "how I" content. As one creator put it, you shouldn't teach anything you haven't done yourself.
- Don't ask "what should I post?". Ask "what have I earned the right to explain?" Try the dinner test: could friends grill you on this topic for 30 minutes with no prep? If yes, you can post about it.

### Student route or expert route

- There are two honest ways to show up, and the difference is how your audience sees you. (Real experts should keep learning too, but that is a different point.)
- An expert has already achieved what the audience wants. They have been there and done that, and they teach what they did and how the audience can apply it.
- A student has not achieved it yet and is on the same journey as the audience. They work like a scientist: run experiments, then share what they guessed and what happened.
- Neither is wrong, as long as it is true. A student who poses as an expert is lying. Students who lean into what they really are tend to get better results and more traction.
- If you have no experience yet, be a documenter, not a teacher. Run experiments and break down what others did, in case studies and breakdowns. A good path over 5 to 15 years: document first, then go do impressive things, then talk about what you did.
- You don't need to be a big authority. You need to be truly interested and one or two steps ahead of your reader. Even a fresh way of thinking about an old idea is valuable.
- Some of the best content comes from people writing about what they are learning right now. "Here's what I'm figuring out" has a raw, honest feel that polished experts can't copy.

### Bank your receipts

- Receipts are the real proof you can turn into content. Keep two lists. If you are an expert, keep a Credibility Bank: your successes and failures, each with the lesson your audience can use. It shows people why you are worth listening to.
- If you are a student, keep an Interest Bank: what you are pursuing and how you are going about it. It is not as strong as credibility, but it shows what you do differently from the average person.
- Become your own case study. One ghostwriter had no testimonials at first, only his own posts that went viral, so he could promise to write similar posts for others. Be so good at what you do that people can't help but pay to learn from you.
- Make work that people can judge. "I understand AI marketing" is weak. A workflow that solves a real marketing problem is strong. Builds, experiments and teardowns show both the result and your thinking.
- Don't only show the polished demo. Show what went wrong, what you rejected, what the tool couldn't do, and why you chose one approach over another. That is where people learn how you think.

### Keep stacking proof

- Proof stacking means adding new proof on top of old proof, and also talking about what you are going to do next, not only what you have done. Pick a public mission people can follow.
- Example from one creator: they grew a well-known business YouTuber's brand (proof 1). Then they started a public mission to grow their own Instagram from 0 to 100K followers using only an iPhone (proof 2). A third will come after that. They can talk about the first win for maybe 5 to 10 more years before it feels stale.
- Another well-known business creator built and sold a gym company (claimed to be worth about \$42 million), then grew his personal brand, then, he says, sold \$106 million of books in a year. Each step stacked on the last.
- Stacking keeps you credible 10 years from now. Many people keep repeating what they did 10 to 20 years ago, and it goes stale.
- Many people have done impressive things but are afraid to talk about them because it feels like bragging. The argument against hiding: when you stay quiet, people with less experience end up giving your audience the advice, and they give it badly.
- Don't take content advice from people who have never done it, like someone with 500 followers. Find people who built real personal brands and ask them.

### Frameworks

**Student or expert: pick the one that is true**

1. Expert: you have done what the audience wants, and you teach it.
2. Student: you are on the same journey, run experiments, and share the results.
3. Posing as an expert when you are a student is lying, so choose the honest one.

**Documenter to doer path**

1. Document: run experiments and break down what others did.
2. Do: go and achieve impressive things yourself.
3. Talk: teach what you did, then keep stacking new proof.

### Examples

- **Proof stacking:** After growing a well-known business YouTuber's brand, one creator started a public mission to take their own Instagram from 0 to 100K followers with only an iPhone. Now they have a second case study for when the first one gets old.
  <br>*Why it works:* New proof on top of old proof keeps you current, and a public mission gives people something to follow.
- **The brand that flopped at 16:** One creator tried to build a personal brand at 16 and it failed. They had no skills and nothing useful to teach.
  <br>*Why it works:* Content can't create substance that isn't there. It only amplifies what you have actually done.
- **A Credibility Bank entry:** Say you run a small bakery. One entry could be: "My first batch of 200 cookies sold out in an hour, then the next 3 batches didn't sell at all. Lesson: people buy what they see someone else buying, so I put a 'sold out yesterday' sign on the shelf."
  <br>*Why it works:* This is a made up example. A good entry is a real event plus a lesson someone else can use.

### Common mistakes

- Posting to get credibility instead of getting credibility first and then posting.
- Positioning as an expert when you are really a student.
- Only making content about content, with no new experiences to draw on.
- Hiding real achievements because it feels like bragging.
- Only showing the polished result and never the decisions and failures behind it.

### Try it

Spend 10 minutes filling a Credibility Bank or an Interest Bank, whichever is true for you. Write 5 lines, each with a real event or pursuit and the lesson someone else could use. Then run the dinner test on each one: could friends grill you on it for 30 minutes with no prep?

## 1.4 What a brand really is: association

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A brand is not a logo or a color palette. It is a link in people's heads between you and one thing. You build that link by showing the same pairing again and again.

### A brand is a pairing

- Most people overcomplicate brand. They think logo, colors and fonts, which gives them nothing to act on. A simpler definition: a brand exists when your audience links two things together. Good branding is choosing a pairing on purpose and repeating it consistently.
- Michael Jordan wore Nikes every game, every night, not just the finals or the All-Star game. Without that consistency, the link never forms. Over time, people felt "greatness" when they saw the shoe, and Nike borrowed that feeling by pairing with him over and over.
- The Nike swoosh means nothing on its own. It got its meaning from Jordan wearing it. So be careful what and who your brand gets linked with.
- This idea is called association, and it may be the most important idea in all of marketing. Tony Robbins is a performance coach. Gary Vee is social media and marketing. Martha Stewart is homemaking. That reflex of joining a person to a category is association. A personal brand is just that link, spread to lots of people.
- The word "branding" comes from cattle ranches. A rancher burned a mark into a cow so anyone who saw it linked the cow to its owner. A personal brand tries to burn your name into a category, so people think of you whenever they think of that category.

### How the link gets built

- The only way to build a link in someone's mind is to show it to them again and again. Content is the best way to do that, which is why content is the engine of a personal brand. It spreads the link to many people at once.
- For example, a banker in the 1800s could have a very strong reputation in finance but could only show it in person. Likewise, you could be the best fashion designer in the world, but if nobody sees you doing or talking about fashion, the link never reaches many people.
- The strength of your brand depends on how many people see the link and how often. You build it by doing the thing and talking about it in front of as many people as you can.
- If you don't repeat a few topics over time, you end up known for everything, which means known for nothing. Gary Vee and Mel Robbins keep coming back to the same 5 to 10 topics. Many people avoid repeating themselves because they think the audience has already heard it. Repeat anyway, until people can't help but connect you with those topics.
- One creator wants to be known for content creation, marketing and branding, and nothing else. They repeat the same core ideas with new comparisons, stories and examples.

### What you want to be linked with, and what you don't

- Decide what you want your audience to link with you. Trust? Real usefulness? Information that is hard to get? Access to rooms your audience can't enter? For example, one creator wants to be linked with the strategies and systems used by the biggest personal brands in the world. Pick 5 links and pair yourself with them over and over.
- One main link for a brand like this is the belief that if you build a personal brand to grow a business, you should aim for trust and not for going viral. It shows up in almost every long piece. The attitude is: I want to wear the Nikes every game, not just sometimes.
- Just as important is what you do not want to be linked with. If you want to be known as trustworthy, but you keep appearing next to scammy characters in your niche, your audience will see you through that filter.
- Watch out for a personal brand that turns into a prison. If you build it around one narrow label, it can trap you when you want to change.
- A trick from one 90-day plan: ask yourself, "if I could buy 2 or 3 words in people's memory, which would I pick?" Then say them constantly. Objects work too, like the same hat and hoodie in every video, or the taped-up sunglasses a famous vlogger wore.

### Amplifiers: other people spreading the link

- If your exposure only comes from content you post yourself, your reach is limited by your own time. Amplifiers are other people who spread your link for you, and they can speed things up a lot.
- David Goggins built a very strong link to discipline. Podcast hosts talk about him, and hundreds of clipping accounts share short clips of those conversations for free, even on episodes he isn't on. Another creator, known for a looks-focused topic, built a similarly strong link in about 3 to 4 years, partly by using a network of clipping accounts posting his content across thousands of pages.
- Some creators say the top personal brands are all doing this, which is why brands now grow bigger and faster than they used to. Treat that as one view, but the idea is useful: make your ideas easy for other people to clip and share.

### Frameworks

**From pairing to brand**

1. A brand is a pairing of two things in someone's mind.
2. Good branding is choosing that pairing on purpose and repeating it consistently.
3. Pair yourself with your 5 chosen links in every piece of content.
4. Write down what you do not want to be linked with and stay away from it.

### Examples

- **Michael Jordan and Nike:** Jordan wore Nikes every game, not only the big ones. Over time people linked greatness with the shoe. Nike borrowed that feeling by pairing with him again and again.
  <br>*Why it works:* Consistency creates the link. A pairing done only sometimes does not stick.
- **Clips of Goggins:** Clips of podcast hosts talking about David Goggins and discipline get shared by hundreds of clipping accounts. Goggins isn't even on many of those episodes.
  <br>*Why it works:* When your link is clear, other people can spread it without you posting more.

### Common mistakes

- Treating a brand as a logo, fonts and colors instead of a repeated pairing.
- Talking about a topic only sometimes, so the link never forms.
- Avoiding repeating your topics because the audience has heard them.
- Appearing next to people or topics you don't want to be linked with.

### Try it

Write the 5 things you want people to link with you, and 3 things you refuse to be linked with. Then check your last 10 posts: how many of them pair you with one of the 5?

## 1.5 Niche: start narrow, then widen

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** It is easier to own a small category than a big one, and easier to own one category than five. Start in a small area where you go deep, then widen as your proof grows.

### Two rules for building the link

- Rule 1: it is easier to be linked with a small category than a big one. Owning "video storytelling for business owners" is far easier than owning "business".
- Picture a spiderweb. To be the person for all of business, you need deep knowledge of every business model, every skill (traffic, funnels, offers, pricing) and every function (marketing, sales, legal, finance). That web is huge, and when you start, your strands are thin and weak. "Video storytelling for business owners" is a much smaller web. You cover fewer areas, so in the same time your strands get thicker.
- This is why many people you have never heard of, with very narrow positioning, make a lot of money.
- Rule 2: it is much easier to own one category than five. Every extra topic splits the time you spend building each link.
- "You are the niche" (talking about whatever you like because the brand is you) is a weaker plan when you are starting out, because your message gets diluted. Lesson 7 explains this.
- Start narrow and then widen. A well-known business creator started with boring small businesses before going to business in general. An interviewer started with CEO conversations before moving to general pop culture. A sports commentator started with basketball, then all sports, then politics.

### Pick the right pond, lake or ocean

- Once you know where you are going, pick the size of water to compete in. Say a founder has one successful e-commerce business and wants to build a personal brand about business. Business as a whole is an ocean. The people already there built many kinds of businesses over many years. With one type of experience you can still help people in your lane, but you can't compete with the big names there, at least not yet.
- How to choose: ask what you have actually accomplished, what your wins and failures are, and how those compare with the ocean, lake or pond you are considering.
- Example of the sizes: brand as a whole is the ocean. Personal brand is the lake. Personal branding for business owners is the pond, and it is where many of the biggest success stories came from.
- Most people should start in a pond. Gary Vee started with wine, moved up to marketing, then to business as a whole (leadership, serving customers), then to personal development.
- You won't be stuck in the pond forever. The danger is the opposite: many people jump from pond to lake to ocean only because they are bored with their topic. Move up only when your experience allows it. If you enter a bigger water with no experience, you just repeat other people's advice and get no traction. Build traction in the pond first, then follow a river into the lake.

### The bullseye and the kind of fame you want

- Before you pick topics, decide which kind of fame you want. Mass fame means everyone knows you, like a huge business creator. Category fame means you are a big deal only inside your category. It is easy to start narrow and widen later, and very hard to go back from broad to narrow.
- Draw a bullseye. Put the narrowest version of your topic in the center (for example, social growth for business owners) and widen the rings out to the broadest version (business as a whole). Start in the center. There, your topics, examples, words and proof all match one clear viewer. Widen only as your proof grows.
- Aim to be the favourite in a narrow category, not just one of many in a big one. People keep folders in their head, like "my golf guy". When a problem hits, they open the folder and go to number 1. Being number 1 in a narrow category beats being number 5 overall, which is why branding around one word works so well.
- Do one thing better than 99% of people, not 99 things. One creator only wants to be known for content creation. Splitting into business, mindset, productivity and AI can work for people at the top who already have a brand. While you are coming up, focus on one thing and expand later.
- Stick to 1 to 3 topics. If you can't say what you stand for in one sentence, people won't know why to follow you, and your follower numbers will stay low.

### A small niche can still be a big market

- A common complaint is "my niche is too small". One team that has worked with thousands of channels says it has yet to find a single niche where it couldn't get some traction. It calls "my niche is too small" a belief that puts a low ceiling on you.
- The team's attitude: any niche can go viral if you have the right topics, tools and direction.
- Small niches can be huge. One claim is that about 30 million people play golf every weekend. Search for knitting on YouTube, sort by most viewed, and you'll find videos with millions of views. Real exceptions do exist: topics so specific that only a few thousand people in the world care.
- Advice for founders building a following: pick a narrow niche early. Find 100 people who love it (for example, people trying to get product design teams and customer support teams working together) and make them love you. Don't try to be everything to everyone. Work out what you are really good at and own it.
- One caution for founders: viral content has a huge market, but most founder businesses sell to a narrow group, like people earning \$50,000 a month or more, or big businesses. Content for them has to be more specific, so it won't have mass appeal.

### Pick the sub-niche you win first

- Case study: a wildlife creator. He was known for podcasts and TV shows but struggled on YouTube, because he was going after everything at once.
- If you want to win a whole niche, don't start with the whole niche. Every big niche splits into smaller areas called sub-niches. Start in one or two, then widen as the channel grows. For the wildlife creator, that meant extinct animals, dangerous animals and reptiles.
- The usual advice is to go as broad as possible. This case says no: pick small battles first. The claimed result is that his long-video views went up 3,900%, from about 30,000 a video to 3 million, and he became the biggest channel in his niche.

### Frameworks

**Pond, lake, ocean**

1. List what you have accomplished, including wins and failures.
2. Compare that with the pond, lake or ocean you want to compete in.
3. Start in the pond where your experience can win.
4. Move up only as your experience grows, never because you are bored.

**The niche bullseye**

1. Draw rings from the narrowest version of your topic to the widest.
2. Start in the center, where your topics, examples, words and proof match one viewer.
3. Widen only as your proof grows.
4. Choose category fame or mass fame early, because going from broad to narrow is hard.

### Examples

- **Gary Vee's path up the ponds:** Gary Vee started with wine, then moved up to marketing, then to business as a whole, then to personal development.
  <br>*Why it works:* His brand followed his experience upward. It did not start at the top.
- **Narrow first, wide later:** A well-known business creator began with boring small businesses and then moved to business in general. An interviewer began with CEO conversations and then moved to pop culture.
  <br>*Why it works:* Narrow first and wide later is a common path.

### Common mistakes

- Starting broad because you want more reach.
- Splitting your content across 4 or 5 topics so every link stays weak.
- Moving up from pond to ocean because you are bored of your topic.
- Using "my niche is too small" as the reason for low views.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say to start narrow and own one small category, doing one thing better than 99% of people. Others warn that "niche down and only talk about one thing" can trap you in a new kind of job you don't enjoy. They say your niche is how you help people change, not a narrow topic, and that people will get interested in almost anything if you present it well. They admit niching down works, but want freedom to talk about anything, so they cast a wide net first and offer specifics to the people inside the audience.

### Try it

Draw your niche bullseye on paper. Write the narrowest version of your topic in the center and 2 wider rings around it. Then answer: what have I accomplished that proves I can win in the center ring?

## 1.6 Size your niche: reach, core, casual and new

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Before you blame your niche, check how big your market really is. Then make every idea work for 3 kinds of viewers: people who love the topic, people who like it a bit, and strangers. And be clear about whether your goal is more views or the right viewers.

### Measure your market the simple way

- The size of your market is the number of people who could realistically watch your content. Don't overcomplicate it. Step 1: search your niche term on YouTube (for example, golf). Step 2: filter out Shorts by choosing a longer video length. Step 3: sort by most viewed. Step 4: ignore the freak outliers at the very top, like a celebrity golf video with about 34 million views, which is not a fair picture. Step 5: scroll further and look at channels fully dedicated to the niche, such as a golf tutorial channel with many videos at 1 million views. That level is roughly the size of your market.
- Now compare. If the best channels get 1 million views a video and you get 100,000, you are at 10% of what is possible, so the niche is not your problem. Most people who say their niche is too small are getting 5,000 to 10,000 views a video.
- Some niches are also not fully used by creators yet, so there may be even more viewers waiting.

### Go upstream: your niche sits inside bigger interests

- Your niche sits inside larger interests, like a small circle inside a bigger one. Make videos that reach the bigger circle while still serving your niche.
- Case study: a business creator with good basics (a team, good editing, decent thumbnails) made very narrow videos, like "6 tools to run a business", "how to use Slack" and "Zoom vs Google Meet". The advice was to take bigger swings, like asking millionaires for advice, and general business videos a total beginner could enjoy.
- Case study: a university lecturer in permaculture. His early videos were narrow, like water harvesting. Then "How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert" got 12 million views in 4 months, and several of his videos now pass 1 million. He turned a small niche into a topic that matters to 12 million or more people.

### Core, casual and new viewers

- Every idea should be clickable and enjoyable for three groups at once. Core viewers are the hardcore fans who really care. Casual viewers dip in and out and care a bit. New viewers aren't into your niche and have never seen your channel, but would click on something interesting.
- The Sahara video works for all three. A permaculture student likes it, a casual gardener likes it, and someone who doesn't care about gardening still clicks.
- There are two ways to fail. One is getting too caught up in the core and making only technical videos for hardcore students. The other is chasing new viewers so hard that you go too broad and lose the audience you built. Jumping on any trend at random won't bring your core along.
- Growth means reaching new people, so broader appeal gives you a better chance of a click from a stranger in recommendations.
- Watch out for the hardcore-fan trap. If you love a niche enough to start a channel about it, you will want to go very specific, which only appeals to people as hardcore as you.
- A test one channel manager uses: the world's biggest creator should be able to watch this, and my mother should be able to watch this.

### Order of operations: monkey bars

- Over a career, it works better to start narrow and focus on your core first, then expand. You can't really go the other way, because starting broad and narrowing alienates the viewers you gathered.
- Think of monkey bars. Start in one sub-niche (a small area inside the bigger niche), then swing to the next, slightly broader one, then the next. Because they overlap, you bring people with you. For example, some music channels started with a very specific sub-genre of electronic music, then moved to European drum and bass, then broader.
- A few creators got millions of views right away with broad videos, but they are the exception, like the few people who win the lottery. Most channels that now get 100,000 to 1 million views a video started more niche.
- Here is an example for short videos. One creator found that shorts aimed at school kids did best. When a video didn't appeal to school kids, views dropped. When he aimed at that core again, views jumped back.

### Maximizing views or reaching the right people

- Some creators try to get as many views as possible. But views aren't everyone's goal. If you sell a niche product to hardcore fans, broad videos may do little for your business. You might aim for 2 to 3 million very targeted views a month instead of 10 million random ones.
- Be careful about the band trap. Creators ask for more views, get them, then dislike making broader videos, like a band that misses its small shows once it gets famous. Be clear with yourself and with anyone you hire about the main goal.
- Who should maximize views: people whose main income is ad revenue, sponsorships or affiliate links, and most channels that don't yet earn a full-time income. One caveat from the head of a YouTube agency: their data shows very broad viral creators often convert sponsorships worse.
- Who should not: people who earn more from a small, specific group, like someone selling a course on using Slack as a manager. A wildly viral Slack video won't bring the right people. But you can borrow formats that went viral across many niches, like "If I had to start over with Slack, here's what I'd do". If you usually get 1,000 views and this gets 30,000, you got more views and still brought the right viewers.
- From the personal brand side, the sweet spot for most is 3,000 to 10,000 views a post, because viral does not equal money. Pick the overlap between a small, high-value audience and how many people care about the topic that fits your goal.

### Frameworks

**Core, casual and new viewers**

1. Core: hardcore viewers who care a lot.
2. Casual: people who dip in and out.
3. New: people outside the niche who would click on something interesting.
4. Test each idea against all three, and avoid both failures: only core, or only new.

**A 5-step market size check**

1. Search your niche term on YouTube.
2. Filter out Shorts and sort by most viewed.
3. Ignore the freak outliers at the very top.
4. Scroll to dedicated channels in the niche. That level is roughly your market size.
5. Compare your own views to that level before blaming the niche.

### Examples

- **The Sahara video:** A permaculture lecturer made narrow videos until "How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert" got 12 million views in 4 months. Several other videos passed 1 million.
  <br>*Why it works:* He kept his niche but picked a topic that core, casual and new viewers could all click.
- **Borrowing a viral format:** Say you sell a course on using Slack. You borrow a format that went viral in other niches, "If I had to start over with Slack, here's what I'd do". A channel that usually gets 1,000 views gets 30,000.
  <br>*Why it works:* Borrow the format, keep the audience. You got more views and still reached the right people.

### Common mistakes

- Blaming a small niche without checking what the best channels in it actually get.
- Making ideas only for hardcore fans.
- Going so broad you lose the audience you built, or jumping on random trends.
- Chasing views when you sell to a small group.

### Try it

Do the 5-step market size check on your niche. Write down what the dedicated channels get. Then work out your own typical views as a percentage of that number.

## 1.7 Positioning: promise, proof, perspective, personality

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Positioning is how you carve out your own lane inside a topic, so people remember you instead of the 50 others posting about it. This lesson gives you three ways to check yours: its four building blocks, a 4 Ps checklist, and a test for whether a specific audience wants what you stand for.

### Topic versus positioning

- Your topic is the category you are linked with, like fitness or marketing. Positioning is how you talk about it so you carve your own lane. Making content about a topic does not give you a personal brand. It becomes a brand only when people remember you without trying.
- Positioning has four building blocks: beliefs, messaging, signal and taste.
- Beliefs are the core ideas you stand for in your topic. Make them contrarian, meaning most people in your space would disagree with them (lesson 8 covers this).
- Messaging is how you deliver those beliefs: with facts or with stories, which format, what rhythm, tone and style. This is mostly your choice, which is why you can win in almost any niche in almost any format. Only two things are not optional: be clear, and keep sticking to your core beliefs.
- One way to pick your messaging is through constraints. Two constraints one YouTuber started with: never leave the office or move the camera, and don't share every detail of his personal life. Then he worked backward to the best format within those limits. Anyone can set their own limits.

### Signal and taste

- Signal is how much of your content is truly valuable. Out of 100 minutes someone spends with your content, how many were useful to them? A higher share means people remember you better.
- Say you cover four topics and you are great at video storytelling, but only 30 of every 100 minutes are about it. Your signal is 30 out of 100. Now someone who spends all 100 minutes on one topic but is useful in only 31 of them has more signal than you, even if they are worse at it. So don't spread yourself across too many topics.
- Keep signal high by making each minute non-obvious (new to the viewer) and easy to act on (they can use it alone and get the result you promised).
- Taste is the consistency of your choices over time, in line with what your viewer thinks is quality. People with great taste keep linking themselves to products, objects, people, places and ideas their audience sees as high quality. Quality is a matter of opinion, so taste is mostly about what you consistently choose. High taste makes your link to your topic feel more valuable. How things look is a separate thing, called aesthetics (lesson 11).
- Your personal brand is expertise plus personality. Beliefs and signal make up your expertise. Messaging and taste make up your personality. Consumer topics, like fashion or food, usually reward personality over expertise. Selling to businesses is the opposite: expertise can cut through even with less personality. You almost always need some of both.
- In one line: the core of positioning is a contrarian view, delivered clearly, consistently and with as much useful content as possible.

### The 4 Ps: promise, proof, perspective, personality

- Promise: give one clear promise of what you offer (teaching or entertainment) and repeat it, not 10 different ones. One creator's promise is personal brand, content creation and marketing. A business YouTuber's is business and AI. Try not to stray, because that is what keeps your positioning strong.
- Proof: the reason anyone should listen to you. Saying better things is not enough. People listen to people who have done what they want to do. For example, one business YouTuber had 3 successful company sales before he made content. Put your proof everywhere: your bio, your video hooks, your short-form hooks.
- Perspective: the thing you say that nobody else does. Contrarian takes, taking the opposite position of a big competitor, and standing out like a purple cow in a field of brown ones are all versions of this. It has to be new and not obvious, and built from your own life, stories and comparisons. Before every piece, ask: what unique view can I share that they haven't heard?
- Personality: share some of yourself. One idea is a monthly post showing what you did that month. For one business YouTuber, that shows his supercars, extreme sports, family, dog and wife.
- If your views and followers don't turn into money, you are probably not positioned correctly, so go back to the 4 Ps.

### Does a specific audience want what you stand for?

- Everyone knows product-market fit, the idea that people want what you sell. Few think about the same thing for a personal brand. It means a specific audience instantly gets what you stand for, wants more of it and can't get it anywhere else. Answer three questions: what do you stand for, who is it for, and why should they care? Most brands stand next to 50 competitors and wonder why nobody notices them.
- The strongest brands often own a category or create a new one. For example, Duolingo owns gamified language learning. There are three ways to do it: combine two worlds (AI with trading, or fitness with finance), be first and do it well, or solve one specific pain. If you can't find what makes you different, the product, or for a creator the skill, needs more work first.
- Decide what emotion people should feel when they see you, such as smart, early, belonging, confident, inspired or rebellious. Make your audience the hero by asking what someone gets for giving you their attention. Say it the same way everywhere. The format changes per platform, but the story, usefulness and emotion don't.
- If you can't say what you stand for in one sentence, you will convert few followers. Have real opinions, because neutral accounts are forgettable.
- Profile basics: get verified, show your face, write a bio of 2 to 3 lines that says what you do and who it's for, use a simple banner, and pin a post that makes people want to follow. One claim is that showing your face kills about 80% of the competition, since most people won't. The bio itself isn't that important as long as it isn't bad.

### The authority statement and content buckets

- Write one sentence: "I help X with Y in Z way." This is your authority statement, and it filters every idea. Without it, you drift: you chase views, your message goes generic, and your audience stops buying.
- Personal branding is subtracting, not adding. Four things to map out: your niche bullseye (lesson 5), a list of your contrarian takes, the things you want to be linked with, and your points of uniqueness. For uniqueness, list 5 to 10 competitors and compare them on topic, depth, audience, format and visuals, then find the white space nobody fills.
- Content buckets are 3 to 4 themes that all support your positioning. Every piece you make should fit one. If an idea doesn't fit any bucket, don't make it. One business YouTuber's buckets were business, AI, mindset and relationships, all built from how he wants to be seen. Pick few buckets, or you blend into the noise.

### Frameworks

**The four building blocks**

1. Beliefs: 3 to 5 contrarian ideas you stand for.
2. Messaging: any style, but clear and consistent.
3. Signal: the share of your content minutes that are valuable, non-obvious and easy to act on.
4. Taste: the consistency of your choices over time.

**The 4 Ps**

1. Promise: one clear promise, repeated.
2. Proof: the reason anyone should listen.
3. Perspective: what you say that nobody else says.
4. Personality: some of yourself, shared on purpose.

### Examples

- **The 30 versus 31 example:** You are brilliant at video storytelling but cover four topics, so only 30 of 100 minutes are about it. Someone who spends all 100 minutes on one topic and is useful in 31 of them has more signal than you, even if they are worse.
  <br>*Why it works:* Focus raises signal. Every topic you add dilutes it.
- **A business YouTuber's buckets:** His buckets were business, AI, mindset and relationships. They all back up one image: the AI business guy who cares about mindset and has a great relationship with his family.
  <br>*Why it works:* Buckets come from working backward from how you want to be seen.

### Common mistakes

- Covering many topics so your signal gets diluted.
- Making 10 promises instead of one.
- Saying better things instead of giving people a reason to listen.
- Chasing views until your message goes generic.
- Staying neutral with no real opinions.

### Try it

Write your one-sentence authority statement ("I help X with Y in Z way"). Then list 3 to 4 content buckets that all support it, and check your last 10 posts: how many fit a bucket?

## 1.8 Contrarian takes and a point of view

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** The strongest way to stand out is a belief most people in your field disagree with. A topic gives you things to post about. A point of view gives those posts somewhere to go.

### Why a contrarian take is the strongest lever

- Many creators think the biggest brands blow up because of titles, thumbnails and hooks. Those help, but the biggest brands also approach their niche in a fundamentally different way from most people around them. A contrarian take is the strongest way to stand out, by a wide margin.
- A contrarian belief is something you think is true that most of your space would disagree with. Some people call it counterpositioning (taking the opposite position of the big players). Others call it picking the hill you are willing to die on, or being the purple cow, the one odd cow in a field that people notice. A real perspective attracts the right people and pushes away the ones you didn't want anyway.
- Gary Vee came on stage in a hoodie and beanie and told big companies to take Facebook and social media seriously, at a time when the industry called social media, and Gary, a fad. A well-known business creator said you can buy boring businesses, when society thought the only way to get rich was to be a tech founder. One claim is that this take was a main reason she blew up.
- A contrarian action counts too, not only a belief. Most business creators sell information, consulting or agency services. One of them also sells an energy drink you can buy at Target and drink while watching his YouTube videos. That puts him in a different place in the customer's mind, heart and hand.

### Contrarian, not controversial

- A contrarian take is a belief you feel you have to share because it helps people. A controversial take is said only to trigger a reaction. Never make things up for a reaction.
- Don't go against the grain on everything. A brand made of nothing but disagreement is tiring and not believable.
- There are three ways to position yourself against others. Counter-position: be the opposite, like a young guy in a basement against polished gurus. Mirror with a twist: do the same content with a new delivery, like a playbook on a whiteboard, or agency tips told through cult metaphors. Pick a standout audience: for example, sales training for women in a niche full of men.
- Example of a take: if you build a personal brand to grow a business, aim your content at trust, not at going viral. One creator shares this over and over and says it is a big reason for over 400,000 combined followers across platforms in 18 to 19 months. They also say creators who preached virality a couple of years ago now talk about trust.
- Another example: everyone in content said lighting, gear and editing tricks matter most. One creator said topic matters more, and filmed everything on an iPhone to prove it. They say the market started repeating it, and credit a few contrarian takes for growing from under 10,000 to 60,000 followers.

### How to find your takes

- Use two columns. On the left, write everything people say or do in your niche that you fundamentally disagree with. On the right, write the opposite: your belief or action. Then test those takes in your content, see which ones get the strongest reaction and double down. The winner becomes your most important link to pair yourself with.
- You won't disagree with everything. List the common beliefs in your space and write your counterarguments. The 3 to 5 things you are sure everyone else is wrong about are your core contrarian beliefs. Once they are written down, content gets simple, because you know what you are proving.
- Keep a take vault. Every time you strongly disagree with someone's post, write down what you believe instead. Most big brands run 3 to 5 big contrarian ideas, with smaller takes under each.
- Ask: what are people in my niche saying that I disagree with, and would stand by until the end? Make a video about that.
- One approach is to not watch anyone else in your category, so most ideas come from scratch and end up more contrarian. Sometimes this means you are wrong, confusing, or too far out there, but contrarian thinking comes from there.
- Your job is to create new ideas, formats, angles and hooks. Take inspiration, but aim to become a real thought leader. Be careful about copying others, and about chasing views by jumping on every trend.

### Point of view and narrative

- A topic gives you things to post about. A point of view gives those posts somewhere to go. Build around a belief about what is changing in your market, then keep proving it, challenging it and explaining it. That turns your positioning into a story people follow.
- Salesforce didn't position itself as a better CRM (a tool companies use to track customers). It attacked the whole way software was sold with the message "The End of Software". That changed the buyer's question from "is this a better CRM?" to "do I still want to buy software this way at all?" It repeated the message everywhere: messaging, launch, a staged protest outside a rival's conference, and events.
- Another software company, Gong, ran on "Goodbye opinions. Hello, reality." The enemy was managing sales by gut feel. It published research from real sales calls, like how much each side talks, pricing and competitor mentions. A salesperson could value the research without caring about Gong, but each study made the bigger argument more believable.
- The goal of content is to help your audience with something real while strengthening the worldview behind your brand. The formats change, but the conclusion keeps getting stronger.
- When there is news, don't ask "how do I post about this too?" Ask what it gives you a chance to prove, question or explain about the world you already talk about. That is the difference between covering news and building a story.
- Start with the conclusion you want people to reach. Edward Bernays, an early pioneer of public relations, sold pianos by making people want a music room. Architects designed music rooms, there were exhibitions, and people with taste talked about them. Then the piano felt like the obvious missing piece. If you shape how people understand a problem, you shape what they look for in a solution.

### Frameworks

**The two-column method**

1. Left column: what people in your niche say or do that you disagree with.
2. Right column: the opposite, which is your belief or action.
3. Test the takes in your content and see which ones get the strongest reaction.
4. Double down on the winner and pair yourself with it again and again.

**The belief path**

1. Ask: what do I want people to believe before they get to my offer?
2. A post makes them notice the problem.
3. A teardown shows why the current approach breaks.
4. Research shows it is happening widely.
5. A build or demo shows the better version.
6. A customer story proves it works outside your own bubble.

### Examples

- **Gary Vee in a hoodie:** Gary came on stage in a hoodie and beanie and told big companies to take social media seriously, while the industry called it a fad.
  <br>*Why it works:* It looks obvious now. It was wildly contrarian then. What counts is that it was a real belief.
- **Salesforce and "The End of Software":** Instead of claiming a better product, Salesforce attacked the whole way of buying software and kept repeating that story in its messaging, launches and events.
  <br>*Why it works:* A point of view changes the buyer's question. A topic does not.

### Common mistakes

- Making up a take for a reaction, which is controversial, not contrarian.
- Going against the grain on everything.
- Naming a specific person or company as the enemy (lesson 11).
- Covering news just to post about it, instead of using it to prove your worldview.

### Try it

Do the two-column exercise. Write 5 things people in your niche say that you disagree with, and the opposite that you believe. Circle the one you would keep sharing even if nobody liked it.

## 1.9 Finding your gap and your market edge

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Your best spot is where something you can offer meets something people want. This lesson gives you three tools to find it: your unfair advantages, the layer underneath a hot conversation, and a content map that keeps every post on one path.

### Your market edge

- Ask yourself: what am I genuinely exceptional at? What would me, a few years ago, want to read? (That person is your audience.) Where do my skills overlap with a hot niche? What can people get from me that they can't get anywhere else? Who else can say this? If many people can, your angle isn't unique.
- Turn your unfair advantages into content. They come from five places: your experience, your access to people or places, your results, your point of view and your taste. A job, a past and a network all count.
- World-class content comes from where your unfair advantages meet what the market wants. Most people do only one side. They post what they know, which nobody cares about. Or they chase trends and sound like everyone else.
- Both mistakes end in copy-and-paste brands, like crypto profiles that all look alike. Copy-paste brands don't grow.
- Pick a pocket of the market where a hot niche overlaps with your skills. On X, expect your brand to keep evolving, because the platform moves in real time.

### Find the gap you have a right to own

- Example: AI marketing content was exploding, with workflow tools, agents and demos. But it showed what the tools could do, not how a real marketer would use them to make money. One creator came from marketing and growth, and asked what happens when somebody who understands marketing gets access to all of this. They didn't need to be the best automation expert.
- Method: find a conversation that already gets attention and look one layer underneath. What do people keep asking? Where does content stop just before it becomes useful? Then ask if you have something unusual to add: experience, access, data, taste, a technical skill or a useful mix of backgrounds.
- Nearby subjects that support the same link are fine. This creator later expanded into AI and distribution, go-to-market and the marketer's future.
- A good content strategy is partly deciding what you refuse to be known for. Leave some good ideas alone.
- Test before you commit: can you already think of ten pieces of original work around it, things to build, test, explain, measure or argue from your own experience? If all you have is a nice sentence for your bio, you have a slogan, not a position.
- Answer these 8 questions and use them as a filter for what you publish. What does my audience already care about? What are they missing in the content they see? What do I have to add: experience, access, data, skills or taste? What do I believe is changing? What does that mean for the audience? What beliefs stop them seeing it the same way, and what evidence could change them? What words do I want them repeating? Once they agree, what should they do next? You can also give your answers to an AI tool and ask for a positioning statement, a 3 to 4 theme strategy and 20 ideas, and ask it to flag anything too broad or generic.

### The content map

- Make your map with pen and paper, not a notes app. Handwriting forces you to slow down. Most people quit because they can't picture their brand in 6 months. They post random topics, get no traction and think they have nothing to say.
- Stop treating posts as darts thrown at a board. Treat them as puzzle pieces that add up to a bigger picture. As the map grows, people have more reason to binge your work, and you stop depending on one viral post.
- People follow a brand for three reasons: your post entertained, taught or inspired them; you talk about things they are interested in; and they believe you can help them change their life. The first two come from consistency and quality. The third is the most powerful.
- Step 1, your mission: write two points. Point A, in one sentence, is the life your reader is escaping. Point B, in one sentence, is the life you help them reach. Your mission is to help as many people as possible go from A to B (lesson 2).
- Step 2, pick 2 to 3 broad topics. Make them broader than you think, because people scrolling are not searching for specific knowledge. Social media is for attention, not for deep study. Too narrow and you are invisible to 95% of the people who could benefit. For example, "lead generation for service businesses" becomes online business, "AI automation workflows" becomes artificial intelligence, and "carnivore diet" becomes health. Start with 1 to 2 if you need to. The best topics sit where what you deeply care about meets what helps others.
- Step 3, pick 3 to 5 subtopics under each broad topic, like a mind map. This is where your unique angle shows. Two people can write about business and still be different brands: one covers freelancing, sales and client work, and another covers digital products, content strategy and audience building. AI can suggest subtopics, but write your own list first.
- Step 4, write 3 to 5 specific content ideas per subtopic. Don't judge them yet.
- This also fixes the "I have too many interests" problem. Philosophy, fitness, business and psychology can look scattered until you see they all move someone from scattered focus to self-mastery.

### Authority without being the best

- Why should anyone learn from you when many people teach the same thing? Teach through a new lens: your personal story, your core beliefs or a fresh idea you found while researching. One example lens is the line "ideas are the new oil", used to introduce personal branding.
- You don't need to be a big authority. You need to be genuinely interested and one or two steps ahead. You don't even have to be ahead. A different way of thinking about an idea is valuable on its own.
- If you are leaving a secure job, follow your energy, and not in a fluffy way. Find where you play your own game. For example, one investor was good at finance but would never be exceptional because he played by someone else's rules. Work out what you can be truly exceptional at, because that is where you will have the energy to keep going.
- A good reintroduction post ends with a list of what you talk about on your page (6 topics in one example) and a one-line plan. A new visitor then knows right away what to expect.

### Your advantage as a moat

- Everyone has some advantage. Sometimes it is obvious. An entrepreneur who grew to 100,000 YouTube subscribers fast had good packaging, but there are probably under 10 people in the world who could write that title. He spent a decade building the proof.
- Often it is less obvious. What do you have access to that most people don't? What do you know more about than others? Which life events and experiences are only yours? Even being a beginner is an advantage, because you have less to lose.
- It can be a billionaire giving life advice, or a fishing channel run by someone who lives beside the best trout river in America.
- Once you find a channel's advantage, run every idea through one filter: could everyone just say this? If yes, it is not your idea.

### Frameworks

**Find the gap**

1. Find a conversation that already gets attention.
2. Look one layer underneath: what do people keep asking, and where does content stop just before it becomes useful?
3. Ask what you can add: experience, access, data, taste, a skill or a mix.
4. Test it: can you list ten pieces of original work around it?

**The content map**

1. Mission: Point A (the life they escape) to Point B (the life you help them reach).
2. 2 to 3 broad topics.
3. 3 to 5 subtopics under each.
4. 3 to 5 specific ideas under each subtopic.

### Examples

- **The AI marketing gap:** AI content was full of demos of what tools can do. One creator came from marketing and asked what happens when a marketer gets all of this. They didn't have to be the best automation expert.
  <br>*Why it works:* The gap was one layer underneath the hot conversation.
- **Two business brands:** Two people can both write about business. One covers freelancing, sales and client work. The other covers digital products, content strategy and audience building. They are two different brands.
  <br>*Why it works:* Your subtopics are where your angle shows.

### Common mistakes

- Posting only what you know, or only chasing trends.
- Staying so narrow that you are invisible to 95% of the people who could benefit.
- Having a nice bio sentence but no original work behind it.
- Throwing random topics like darts instead of building a map.

### Try it

Write down 10 pieces of original work (things to build, test, explain, measure or argue) around your idea. If you can't get to 10, your position isn't ready yet.

## 1.10 Authenticity, story and identity

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** People follow people, not ideas. Your story, your beliefs and what makes you different are what let the same idea land differently when you say it.

### People follow people, not ideas

- Take two people who both post "habits are good for you". One is James Clear, the author of a famous habits book. The other is a random account. Millions know Clear's story and book, so they like and repost. The random account gets ignored, or gets replies like "yeah, no kidding". Same idea, very different result.
- Three things explain the difference. First, time under attention. The more attention people give you, especially through long content, the more they read each new post through everything else you have said. A creator with two books, years of videos and newsletter articles with 100,000 or more views can post something simple, and people read it as part of a philosophy they already know. Most beginners quit before people have spent enough time with them.
- Second, shared values. When people know who you are, where you came from and what you stand for, they relate to your ideas as a whole.
- Third, honest polarization. If everybody likes you, nobody loves you. Give people reasons to strongly disagree, and you also give others reasons to strongly agree.
- This is why personal branding is hard to saturate. People who dislike one creator will love another one who says the same idea through a different story and different beliefs. It is the resonance that counts, not the idea, so it doesn't matter if someone has already said everything you want to say.
- It works for products too. If you can buy the same product from a random brand or from a creator you love, you buy from the creator because you trust them more.

### Using your story and beliefs

- You can use your story and your beliefs in two ways: as a topic, or as a way to reframe a topic that already works.
- Story as a topic: "how I failed at seven different business models". Story as a reframe: take a topic that is proven to work, like how to build a personal brand, and start it with your own story. Belief as a topic: you believe digital products are the best way for beginners to start, and you explain why. Belief as a reframe: take classic advice, like how Steve Jobs built products, and look at it through your own lens.
- Exercise for your story: write where you were, what sparked a change, and where you are now. Exercise for your beliefs: for each topic you cover, list the core principles you hold with conviction.
- Beliefs don't come when you stare at a blank screen. They sit in the back of your mind. Think about it for about a week, consume content, and let it trigger ideas you write down. An AI tool can also help pull your story and beliefs into one document.
- Building a brand is telling chapters of one bigger story, with every post a step on the journey. Get clear on 7 things: who you are, where you started, what you believe, who you are doing it for, why you, where you are going and what the payoff is.
- You can also start from the end goal and work back to the beliefs people need. Tesla's 2006 plan is a classic example: first build an expensive sports car, then a cheaper car, then an affordable car, then clean energy. For a personal brand, your story is either your own growth, or you have already reached the goal and show others the road.

### Identity, character and being different

- People follow identity, not information. If anyone else could have said what you said, you won't build a big brand. Your identity is built from your stories, your contrarian takes and your personality. The gap between you on camera and you talking to a friend should be tiny. This is also why it is safe to share all your ideas: people can copy your frameworks, but not who you are.
- People follow characters, not creators. Stop creating content and start creating a character, the way Disney and Pixar do. For one entrepreneur's brand, the team shows what makes him human: he is a family man, he does action sports, he loves peanut butter. If you only teach how and never show who you are, people won't connect.
- Being different beats being better. Trying to make better content than everyone else loses to making content that is different. We don't need more copies of the big names, we need more of you. Focus on what you contribute, not on competing. Mine your own experience instead of your competitors: ideas from your own stories, comparisons and frameworks can't be copied.
- Lean into what makes you you: barefoot, outside, coffee in hand, drawing little diagrams. One creator started shooting in new places and says the first video was a 10x outlier (about ten times their normal views). If you don't reflect who you are, you build an audience of people you don't like.
- Be yourself, because content isn't hard because you don't know how. It's hard because people build it in a way they can't keep up. Playing a character you aren't is usually insecurity. If you aren't being yourself, you won't last more than 6 months. Don't make content that makes you want to quit, like asking the CEO of a company making \$600 million a year to film "best networking hacks".
- Voice means choosing how you want to be seen (smart, funny, blunt) and making your voice match it. Talk in your normal voice and don't let AI flatten it. A brand is intentional choices repeated over time. A laid-back person building a brand around flashy cars won't feel real, and viewers are skeptics.

### The character diamond

- A diamond has four points, and you can fill them in to describe yourself as a character. Top: your north star, what you want to be known for, your main skill or trait. Bottom: your weakness, something you are bad at, which makes you human and which strong brands keep sharing. Left: your real interests, because the more you share, the more people get pulled in. Right: your non-negotiables, the hills you would die on.
- A second version has the same shape. Top: your purpose, the mission or movement you are on. Bottom: your pain, the worst thing that ever happened to you, so people connect with who you are. Left: your weakness, like Superman's kryptonite. Right: your worldview, your unique beliefs, which attract the right people and push away the wrong ones.

### Crowded niches, personality and story

- In a crowded niche like wildlife photography, good photos aren't enough, because there are a million photographers. People connect when they understand your personality, your reason why, or the emotion behind the work.
- Case study: a photographer who started from zero posted "my photos 10 years ago vs now", with himself in the frame in crazy gear in a crazy place. A story unfolds, the viewer feels with him, and he shows emotion by smiling. Viewers then ask how to learn, which opens the door to making money. In contrast, another photographer's beautiful city photos had no emotional connection, so nobody cared.
- Check your profile too. When someone finds you through a reel, your profile has to pass the "this is exactly what I'm looking for" test. The second photographer's photo grid didn't match his reels, which instantly kills trust.
- Story can also be your positioning, because most people are forgettable. One writer hid his past, including running a \$50 million a year e-commerce business with his brother and a battle with mental illness, to climb the corporate ladder. In another story, a migrant with a strong accent who faced racism gave a 7-minute speech at a public speaking club that made his differences instantly forgotten. He became a major figure in tech and opportunities came to him, and his secret was basic storytelling.
- If you master storytelling, you stop selling and start persuading, and opportunities come to you. In a world full of AI, storytelling is a survival skill. As a well-known money writer puts it, a good storyteller with a decent idea will always have more influence than someone with a great idea who hopes the facts will speak for themselves.

### Frameworks

**Why the same idea lands differently**

1. Time under attention: the more time people spend with you, the more your posts are read through your whole philosophy.
2. Shared values: people know who you are and what you stand for.
3. Honest polarization: you give people reasons to disagree strongly, and so to agree strongly.

**Four ways to use your story and beliefs**

1. Story as a topic: tell a story directly.
2. Story as a reframe: start a proven topic with your story.
3. Belief as a topic: share a belief and your reasons.
4. Belief as a reframe: look at classic advice through your lens.

### Examples

- **The same sentence from two accounts:** A famous habits author and a random account both post "habits are good for you". The author's post gets thousands of likes because millions know his story and book. The random account is ignored.
  <br>*Why it works:* Same idea, but different story and different time under attention.
- **Two photographers:** One photographer showed his photos from 10 years ago next to now, with himself in crazy gear and smiling. Another posted beautiful city photos with no person or emotion behind them.
  <br>*Why it works:* In a crowded niche, people connect with the person behind the work.

### Common mistakes

- Acting like a character you are not, which you won't keep up past 6 months.
- Hiding your story and your edge because it feels embarrassing.
- Teaching only how, and never showing who you are.
- Trying to be liked by everybody, which means loved by nobody.
- Having reels and a profile that don't match.

### Try it

Write where you were, what sparked a change, and where you are now. Then list 3 beliefs you hold with conviction about your topic.

## 1.11 Worldbuilding: a world your audience lives in

*11 min read*

> **The big idea:** The strongest personal brands feel like a world people live in, not just a feed they scroll. Seven elements make an audience feel like citizens of that world, and six parts make up the world itself.

### Why build a world

- Fiction franchises like Lord of the Rings, Star Wars, Dune and Marvel build worlds. Tolkien's has characters (Frodo, Gandalf, Aragorn), places (the Shire, Mordor) and magic (wizards, the one ring). When you re-enter the world, you arrive with context, so you aren't just watching a film, you are living in it.
- A brand that teaches can do the same thing. It means having your own language and concepts, shared definitions, recurring characters and places, and honesty about your weaknesses. Done well, the audience starts thinking in your frameworks, uses your terms in their own content, and feels like a citizen of your world rather than a casual viewer. That is loyalty not only to your content, but to how you see the world. One brand strategist with 17 years of experience says almost nobody talks about this.
- Most strategists skip it. They rush to content plans without knowing what the brand stands for, what makes it different, why the person is worth listening to, or what world they are inviting people into.
- Useful information alone leaves a lot on the table. Shared language, shared ways of seeing the world and named concepts people can take with them are what create loyalty.

### The 7 elements of a world

- 1\. Name your concepts. Take something you already teach and give it a name. Don't make up a name on the spot for something you haven't used. Good names are easy to remember, easy to repeat and easy to reference. A name is easier to use in conversation and in other people's content, so they spread your message for you. Don't be lazy and just walk through the framework without naming it.
- 2\. Redefine vague terms. Every niche has terms nobody really understands. Give them a clear definition that tells people what to do, and you create a lens tied back to your brand. For example, the lesson on association defined branding as a pairing of things. That kind of definition is a big advantage, because it puts you in a different category from your peers.
- 3\. Share weaknesses. People don't trust someone who pretends everything is perfect. There are three types. A flaw is something you can change, like anxiety on camera. A limitation is something you can't change but can work with, like ADHD used as a strength. A cost is what your choices make you give up, like an Olympic athlete with no time for friends and family. Costs are the strongest type. It must be real, not like faking tears on camera. One way to show it is filming without your hat, if that scares you.
- 4\. Characters and settings. Gary Vee's daily video series had a recurring cast: his assistants and his brother AJ. Audiences attach to side characters, like in The Office. One creator keeps mentioning their content director, and people message them about him and joke in the comments about him holding a camera for 11 to 12 hours through London. A world with only you in it is flat, so add characters and places on purpose.
- 5\. Interest stacking. Share hobbies, interests and values outside your expertise. An acquaintance shares one thing with you, a friend shares several, and a best friend shares many. An audience works the same way. One creator's list: Harley-Davidsons, dancing at hardcore and metal shows, the Seahawks and trash TV. You have to actually share these things, or nobody knows what you have in common. Each one gives the audience another chance to connect with you.
- 6\. Lore and legends. These are stories of your wins and failures, with the lessons. For example, a client chased views, went broader, got more views and subscribers but fewer sales. It teaches twice: the lesson, and the experience behind it. Scars often become your most strongly held points. Share your highs and lows, not just the highlight reel, and store them in your Credibility Bank (lesson 3).
- 7\. A common enemy, built from your contrarian take. People often bond more over shared dislike than over shared love. This falls apart when the enemy is a specific person or company, because that makes you a call-out creator, not a contrarian one. Instead, frame your take as the thing standing between your audience and their goal, such as treating going viral as the only measure of success. People who share your frustration will join in. A contrarian take is your belief. A common enemy is something you and your audience rally around, which turns a belief into a movement.

### The 6 parts of a world

- Surfaces are the places where you show up. They are digital (social media, YouTube, X, email, product pages, landing pages, even Spotify playlists, anything you communicate through) and physical. Keep your beliefs and messaging consistent across surfaces. The formatting doesn't have to match. You don't need every surface on day one.
- Objects are items or products you show up with, like a famous vlogger's taped sunglasses or Steve Jobs's black turtleneck. Not every brand needs one, but objects stick in memory more than abstract ideas.
- Characters are other people in your content, especially in a world with several people in it. MrBeast has his friends. When several people share the same interest, they all broadcast the same link.
- Citizens are audience members who become part of your world: they follow, then become fans who share your work. The more similar and tightly packed your citizens are, the faster your audience compounds.
- Aesthetics are your design language: colors, surfaces and textures. They make your taste visible, and we live in a visual world.
- Interoperability means the paths that carry someone from one surface to the next, like from a short video to YouTube to email to a community. Without it, your surfaces are a pile of random islands. With it, attention flows through one world.

### Shared language and a creative system

- Some creators plan their look like a film set: a recurring set, fixed fonts and colors, named series with their own title style, styling on purpose, a collaboration every month, and launches run as campaigns (tease, reveal, launch, sustain). A tip on a visual signature is to own one color or pattern, like orange for Nerf.
- A content meeting, twice a month, can work like this. First, look at news in your niche: do you like it, and is it an opinion or a tactic? Then look at your own history: the camera roll from last week, then 1, 3 and 5 years ago. Then look at the posts in your topic that did unusually well. Talk through each idea out loud with someone, asking "would this make a good video, and what's the hook?" Record it, and sort ideas into priority, middle and backburner.
- Your audience examines your words closely. Coin your own phrases and repeat them for years. One business YouTuber owns "exhaust the body, tame the mind", which he has posted for 3 or more years, and people quote it and tag him. Another creator uses the word "moonshot" again and again. They say they went from about 0 to 200,000 on Instagram and 0 to 100,000 on YouTube in about 6 months.
- Try this language test: what would someone say if they recommended you to a friend? Most positioning happens when you aren't in the room. Build a small vocabulary and use it everywhere: content, homepage, sales deck, podcasts and launches. You will get bored of your own phrases much faster than the market does. Show someone five pieces of your content and ask what you talk about. That answer measures your positioning better than your bio does.

### Frameworks

**The 7 elements of a world**

1. Name your concepts so they are easy to remember, repeat and reference.
2. Redefine vague terms with definitions that tell people what to do.
3. Share weaknesses: a flaw, a limitation or a cost.
4. Add characters and places.
5. Stack interests outside your expertise.
6. Tell the stories of your wins and failures.
7. Build a common enemy from your contrarian take.

**The 6 parts of a world**

1. Surfaces: where you show up.
2. Objects: items you show up with.
3. Characters: other people in your world.
4. Citizens: audience members who become fans who share your work.
5. Aesthetics: your taste made visible.
6. Interoperability: paths between your surfaces.

### Examples

- **A content director as a recurring character:** One creator keeps mentioning their content director. Fans now message them about him and joke about him holding a camera for 11 to 12 hours through London.
  <br>*Why it works:* A side character adds familiarity and a feeling of belonging.
- **An owned phrase:** One business YouTuber owns the phrase "exhaust the body, tame the mind", which he has posted for 3 or more years. People quote it and tag him.
  <br>*Why it works:* Owning a phrase takes years of repetition.

### Common mistakes

- Naming a concept you invented on the spot instead of something you already teach.
- Faking a weakness, or making a specific person or company your enemy.
- Showing up on many surfaces with no paths between them.
- Getting bored of your own phrases before the market does.
- A world with only you in it.

### Try it

Pick one thing you already teach and give it a name that is easy to remember, repeat and reference. Then pick one vague word in your niche and write a definition for it that tells people what to do.

## 1.12 Trust: how it is built and how to protect it

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Trust means people believe you will meet or beat their expectations, because you did before. People also trust conclusions they reach themselves. Together these show how to earn trust on purpose, and why chasing virality can damage it.

### What trust is and how to build it

- Trust is how strongly someone believes you will meet or exceed their expectations, based on what you did in the past.
- So you build trust by setting an expectation and then meeting it, again and again. For teaching content, set up a painful problem your audience can't solve alone, then give radically clear steps, as obvious as possible, from the problem to the solution. Do this on repeat.
- Think of a free sample at a big store. If you like the sample, you are more likely to buy the full pack. In the same way, when you later mention an offer, people are more likely to believe you will deliver, because you met their expectations before.
- To do this, identify the painful problems of your ideal customer (the person you want to serve, who isn't necessarily your current audience), and identify your unique solutions. Then across all your content, keep setting up one of those problems and walking through the clear solution. Done consistently, your audience would have to fight to not believe you.
- The chain looks like this: obsession leads to skills, skills to results, results to proof, proof to trust, and trust to a brand that scales. Trust comes through a lot of content.

### Optimize for trust, not virality

- Going viral can help. The problem is optimizing your content for virality, because it makes you pick topics that don't lead to the business results you want.
- Say someone who teaches business principles posts a random video about their fast food order. It goes viral, but the audience it brings doesn't care about what drives the business. Attention around something is not the same as people believing you can help them.
- Think of a country singer who has all the attention in country music. If she launched a course on how young musicians can break through, you wouldn't believe she could teach it, because she never showed she could teach.
- Here is a real pattern: a person one creator worked with chased views and made much broader content to go viral every time. Views and subscribers went up, while sales went down.
- Your reputation is what people say when you are not there, not your bio. If a video would get views but hurt your brand, don't post it. Lots of views from people who then have a bad experience with you is the worst outcome. Post videos that get views, never videos made only for views. Views should turn into followers, and followers into buyers. Don't build a rage-bait brand.
- Don't associate with negativity. A bad brand closes doors behind your back that you didn't know were open. Online, stay out of drama, stay in your lane and keep serving your audience.

### Let people reach the conclusion themselves

- Some words plant ideas in a viewer's mind without them noticing. Direct pressure hits the viewer's defences. Suggestion goes around them.
- The direct version is bad: "I'm the best in the content space. You need my help. Your system is broken. Here's mine, you should buy it." It sounds brash and salesy, so people dismiss it.
- The suggestion version is better: "These are the five best content strategies I used to grow these businesses to over \$20 million a year last year." After the explanation, viewers may conclude on their own that you are one of the best. When people reach a conclusion on their own, they trust you much faster. This is where obsessive fandom comes from. Think of the film Inception, where an idea is planted so the person believes it came from them.
- Check every piece against five inputs. 1) Is it relevant to your ideal viewer? Narrow it down. The more specific it is, the more it feels like you are reading their mind. 2) Is it a small effort for a big reward? Viewers leave when things get complex, and simple things get action. 3) Does it use a frame that isn't obvious? Non-obvious ideas create obsessive audiences. To find them, spend time outside your niche and combine two things that don't usually go together. 4) Is it backed by proof? Without proof, you are just another person with an opinion. With proof, you are a source of truth. Get the proof first, then make the content. 5) Are the steps something the viewer can actually do?

### Three levels of obsession

- Level 1 is the signal layer. It's the easiest, and some say anyone can get a few viewers within 30 days this way. Make content with higher signal than everyone else in your category. Signal is how often a viewer leaves thinking it was worth their time. If 30 out of 100 pieces are valuable, that is 30%. Three things raise it: it's relevant to the viewer, not obvious and backed by proof. Example: a video about "dopamine ladders" was on target (storytelling), not obvious (a framework invented for it) and backed by proof (examples and numbers for each step).
- Level 2 is the belief layer. Change what the viewer believes, not by telling them they are wrong, but by showing a way of thinking they can't unsee. Three things help: a contrasting frame (then versus now, A versus B), a character they relate to (someone who looks like them and started where they started), and a small cost for a large reward. Example: "0 to 100,000 subscribers on YouTube in 5 months".
- Level 3 is the action layer, and the strongest. The viewer takes action and gets a real result. The trap is telling them what to do, which feels like homework. Instead, show proof so strong that if they simply copy it, they might get the same result. It needs two things: numbers that prove it (for example, how this strategy took someone from 2 to 15,000 followers in 6 weeks), and steps broken down into the smallest pieces someone can follow.
- One creator claims that people at level 3 will most likely watch and buy almost anything you offer. The chain is: proof suggests action, action creates results, and results drive obsession. They point to comments like "I grew from 2 to 20,000 subscribers just by watching your videos" as evidence, which is their own claim.

### Frameworks

**Build trust, step by step**

1. Identify the painful problems of your ideal customer.
2. Identify your unique solutions.
3. In each piece, set up one of those problems.
4. Solve it with radically clear steps, and repeat.

**Three levels of obsession**

1. Level 1, signal: relevant, not obvious, backed by proof.
2. Level 2, belief: a contrasting frame, a character they relate to, and a small cost for a large reward.
3. Level 3, action: numbers that prove it, and steps broken into the smallest pieces.

### Examples

- **The fast food order video:** A business teacher posts a random video about a fast food order. It goes viral, but the audience it brings doesn't care about what drives the business.
  <br>*Why it works:* This is a made up example. Attention is not the same as people believing you can help.
- **Direct versus suggestion:** Direct: "I'm the best, your system is broken, buy mine." Suggestion: "These are the five best content strategies I used to grow these businesses to over \$20 million a year last year."
  <br>*Why it works:* People trust conclusions they reach themselves.

### Common mistakes

- Optimizing for views on topics that don't lead to your goal.
- Telling viewers what to do instead of showing proof.
- Sharing frameworks with no numbers behind them.
- Making things complex because complex feels valuable.
- Posting for views that hurt your brand.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say to optimize for trust, not virality, because viral topics can bring people who don't care about what you sell. Others, who grow channels from 1 million to 100 million monthly views, favor maximizing views, though they agree that creators selling to a small group should aim for fewer, more targeted views. Another view says the sweet spot for most personal brands is 3,000 to 10,000 views a post, because viral does not equal money.

### Try it

Write the one painful problem your ideal customer has, and the clear steps from problem to solution. Then check your last 10 posts: how many set up a problem and then solve it with clear steps?

## 1.13 Obsession, stickiness and fandom

*11 min read*

> **The big idea:** A strong brand link comes from three things: obsession (the time you put in), positioning (how well your content sticks) and worldbuilding (how far it spreads). The goal is a fan whose brain releases a little dopamine just from seeing your name.

### Obsession: the oxygen of a personal brand

- Brands at their peak are genuinely obsessed with their field: Dana White with MMA, Steve Jobs with product design, Warren Buffett with investing, Kylie Jenner with cosmetics. If there is one thing you can control, it is how obsessed you are.
- In practice, obsession means time spent doing the thing: how much of your waking hours you spend doing, talking about, learning and teaching it. The biggest reason most brands lack a solid foundation is that they haven't spent enough time doing the thing.
- The chain is: obsession leads to skills, skills to results, results to proof, proof to trust, and trust to a brand that spreads.
- Fake obsession means talking about something you don't actually do. People can sniff that out, and the brand won't grow.
- Some people get extremely obsessed with any field they focus on. Joe Rogan is one, and it's why he can cover comedy, MMA, podcasting, archery and pool. He is the exception. If you aren't naturally obsessed and can't force it, it will be very hard to spend enough time to build a brand.
- A test for choosing a niche: pick one you are already obsessed with, or are willing to become obsessed with, enough that you would spend 40 hours a week on it for the next decade.
- One creator describes their own case. They set out to build one of the strongest brands in content strategy, studied the competition and went all in. They say that after 3 to 4 years of content, 91 YouTube videos and about 18 to 24 months in the content strategy niche, they had 1.2 million hours of watch time. That is their own claim.

### A formula for the value of a personal brand

- One way to think about it: brand value equals viewers, times content minutes, times stickiness.
- Viewers: the number of people who see you linked with your topic. The best wide format is short video, either made by you or clips of you made by others.
- Content minutes: the minutes someone spends seeing or hearing you about your topic. Meeting people in person doesn't scale, but content does, for the same effort. You raise this number with more volume, and with more minutes that are on target, which means narrower topics.
- Stickiness: a multiplier for which minutes build trust and which are junk food. It is the most important variable, and most people ignore it.
- Length correlates with stickiness. One minute of short video is less sticky than one minute of long video, which is less sticky than one minute of a livestream, because stickiness compounds when someone watches many minutes in a row.
- Five things raise stickiness: longer formats (more depth), more places where people see you (repetition), more contrarian beliefs, higher signal and clearer messaging.
- Putting it together: viewers come from worldbuilding, minutes come from obsession, and stickiness comes from positioning. Obsession creates the minutes, positioning makes them stick, and worldbuilding spreads them out.
- A simple setup: one short-video platform for reach (7 posts a week), one long-form platform for depth (1 to 2 posts a week) and one place to nurture fans (email or a community). It takes hundreds and hundreds of posts.

### The goal: a Pavlovian response and cult fans

- The ultimate goal is that a viewer's brain releases dopamine just from seeing your name or face, before they watch anything. It is named after Pavlov: the bell rang, the dog got food, and eventually the bell alone made the dog's mouth water. Some creators call this king status in attention land, and say that if you can do it on command you can build a business as big as you want.
- You get there by taking viewers up a six-step ladder of reactions. The first four steps are about your message, getting the viewer to care about what you are saying. Steps 5 and 6 are about you, the messenger, and this is where cult fandom is built.
- Step 5, affection: the viewer starts liking and trusting the person telling the story and gives you a longer leash. Four things raise likability: attractiveness (a subconscious effect), your overall vibe (how you look, dress and sit), smiling (joy comes through the lens) and actually solving a problem for them. The last is the strongest and works whatever you look like. Don't force likability. Be authentically passionate.
- Step 6, revelation: the viewer realizes you will keep being a source of value over time. Teaching reaches this step much more easily than entertainment, because entertainment competes with Netflix, the NFL and the biggest streamers. If viewers struggle badly with the problem you cover, it may take only a couple of pieces to go from step 1 to step 6. If your topic is a nice-to-have, it takes many more.
- Faceless content can reach steps 1 to 4 but struggles to reach the top, because there is no personality to attach taste, trust and liking to.
- Cult fans act differently from casual fans in three ways. Casual fans let posts come to them, while cult fans go looking and consume everything. Casual fans watch and leave, while cult fans share you. Casual fans see your offer and think about it, while cult fans buy.
- Aim to be the favorite of a narrow category, not just one of many in a broad one. Being number 1 in a narrow category beats being number 5 overall.
- Four things decide how good your content is, with rough weights: direction 30% (useful, non-obvious, on-target topics), comprehension 40% (shorter words, simpler sentences, bite-sized chunks), effectiveness 20% (does the tip work when they try it) and likability 10%.
- Think of a watering hole. An elephant returns to the hole that had water 9 out of 10 times, not the one that had water 1 time in 3. Every useful post is water.

### Signature habits and the kind of video you make

- One creator starts videos with "Check this out really quick", in about the last 30 videos. It is a signature pattern people recognize by ear. Unique visuals, a color palette, motion and a clear look help you cut through, because copied patterns make people tune out.
- There are two main types of video, and both solve a pain point. Entertainment solves boredom. Teaching solves a specific problem. Teaching reaches trust, likability and the Pavlovian response faster. Entertainment rarely needs proof but struggles to get past step 5.
- Your hook style is part of your positioning. A teacher hook builds authority so you can sell expertise later. An investigator hook suits people who want to be at the leading edge, showing they find what most people don't know. A contrarian hook suits a smart expert or someone who frames themselves as the contrarian in a crowded space. A fortune-teller hook suits anyone building authority on where a topic is heading.
- A clear topic also helps people sort themselves. Don't trick people who aren't your target into watching. If someone doesn't care about investing, an investing video should lose them quickly.

### Frameworks

**The brand value formula**

1. Brand value = viewers x content minutes x stickiness.
2. Viewers come from worldbuilding, minutes from obsession and stickiness from positioning.
3. Together they create the link between you and your topic.

**Cult fan versus casual fan**

1. Some versus all: how much of your content they consume.
2. Watch versus share: whether they spread you.
3. Think versus buy: whether they pay.

### Examples

- **Joe Rogan and the obsession gene:** Rogan covers comedy, MMA, podcasting, archery and pool, only because he is hyper-obsessive about each, and his show has enough content minutes to build each link.
  <br>*Why it works:* He is the exception. Most people should own one category.
- **The watering hole:** An elephant goes back to the hole that had water 9 out of 10 times, not the one that had it 1 in 3.
  <br>*Why it works:* Consistent usefulness makes people come back.

### Common mistakes

- Talking about something you don't actually do, which people sniff out.
- Ignoring stickiness and counting only views.
- Forcing likability instead of being authentically passionate.
- Spreading your effort over many topics so no minutes stack up.

### Try it

Run the niche test: could you spend 40 hours a week on this topic for the next 10 years? If not, write down which part of it you would still be obsessed with.

## 1.14 A brand that lasts in the AI era

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** AI made it cheap to make things, but not to be trusted. Taste, distribution and high agency are the three things AI can't give you, and a brand needs a position nobody else holds to survive.

### The three moats

- One claim is that about 90% of people using AI do the same thing: prompt, accept, ship, with no judgment and no taste. Building even one of three strengths makes you stand out. Taste is knowing what good looks like. Distribution is getting your work in front of people who care. High agency is the willingness to figure things out when nobody shows you how.
- AI can't replace them. Judgment only comes from experience, trust only comes from consistency, and drive doesn't quit when the path gets unclear.
- A claim about timing: in about 12 months, taste won't be rare but expected, and distribution will be harder because everyone will try. Right now, these strengths put you ahead of 95% of people using the same tools.
- AI doesn't level the playing field, it tilts it. AI mirrors how much the user understands. AI is a multiplier, not an equalizer. Curiosity plus AI is a big gain, while passivity plus AI is nothing, because zero times ten is still zero. A speed boost on bad judgment just gets you to the wrong place faster.
- High agency in practice means hitting a wall and finding a way around it, combining tools nobody told you to combine, and opening the docs and trying four approaches before asking for help. Ask "what if I tried this?" instead of "how do I do this?". The CEO of Replit has said you don't need development experience, you need grit and the ability to learn fast. A Coinbase CEO is also reported to have said his best hires were often unqualified on paper but high agency.
- Patience is a strategic asset. When the market is flooded with fast and shallow work, slow and deep becomes the advantage. High agency is the hardest to fake and is the foundation of the other two. High-agency people study the best builders on X, Reddit and in communities, learn the frameworks instead of copy-pasting prompts, and ask AI to critique their work.

### Taste: how it shows up

- Taste shows up in specific decisions, at three levels. Small: you notice your AI-built site looks like every other template, so you spend 20 minutes on icons, a color palette based on real design research and a font that fits. Editorial: you rewrite the first two sentences of an AI draft because it opened with something generic. The default hook is almost always wrong. Obsessive: you study websites that convert, looking at whitespace, button placement, signup flow and error messages.
- Taste is also knowing what to reject. It means having an opinion about the defaults and overriding them.
- You can't shortcut taste. It comes from seeing thousands of examples until you build an internal sense of what works, and from reading enough great writing to feel when a sentence earns its place.
- Example: one builder spent a year coding with AI and built more in 3 months than in the previous 2 years. Then they looked back honestly. Half was mediocre, functional and technically correct but forgettable. The trap was confusing output with quality. They stopped building for a few weeks and spent hundreds of hours studying design, typography, spacing, hierarchy and storytelling.
- As one creator put it, you can't prompt your way into having taste. To build it, study people at the frontier where great taste meets new technology. Save work that makes you stop scrolling and work out why. Test one decision in your own work, and build a reference library. Good enough is free now and available in endless supply, so it no longer helps you. Functional is free. Remarkable still costs something.

### Trust is the premium asset

- AI made building cheap but not trust. Making things now costs almost nothing. Being trusted is as hard as ever, maybe harder, because AI content made people more skeptical. When everything could be AI, trust in the humans behind the work becomes the premium.
- Some data cited by creators (check it before you reuse it): "AI slop" was named a word of the year for 2025, with mentions up 9 times. Consumer trust drops by about half when people can tell content was made by AI. In one university study, AI ads beat human ones by 19% on clicks, but the likelihood of buying dropped by 33% once people learned the ads were AI-made.
- Trust comes from consistency, a recognizable voice and a long record of showing your work for months or years. AI cuts creation down to minutes, but trust still takes months or years. You can't generate trust with a prompt.
- Followers are vanity. People who reply, share without being asked and come back daily are the real moat. A passive audience is a commodity, and an active community is a moat.
- People prefer to hear from a personal page than a brand page unless the product is groundbreaking, and early-stage companies mostly grow with founder-led content. Courses, communities and creator products sell through X and YouTube first, because people buy from someone they trust.
- Your traits are the moat too. Work ethic, coachability, grit and the ability to adapt decide who wins as AI improves. Every role is becoming an ownership role.

### 5 things a brand needs in the AI era

- A position nobody else holds. AI can copy any post within hours, so owning information is worth less and less. Own a lane through at least one of five things: premium packaging, originality, practical usefulness, a rare mix of skills or a strong personal presence. The best brands stack two or three. The algorithm doesn't punish you for being bad, it punishes you for being average.
- Data for the topic, you for the take. Pick topics the market has already shown it wants (study the best posts from top accounts). Then add your own view by asking: what do I believe about this that everyone else gets wrong?
- A lot of volume on one platform until you own it. One creator stayed only on YouTube for 18 months before touching Instagram or TikTok. If you spread across 5 platforms, each one gets a fifth of your effort.
- A high quality bar. When supply is limited, speed wins. When supply is endless, quality wins. Ask: was this the best I could do with my time and skill today? If this were the only post people ever saw from you, would you be proud?
- Real obsession. AI can copy your style, but it can't copy real interest. Gut check: are you excited to make the next 100 posts on this topic?
- A good split: let AI do about 80% of the work (research, drafts, structure) and put your own 20% into taste, which means the hook, what stays and what gets cut. Most people do the opposite.

### Frameworks

**The three moats**

1. Taste: knowing what good looks like and what to reject.
2. Distribution: getting your work in front of people who care.
3. High agency: figuring things out when nobody shows you how.

**Five things a brand needs in the AI era**

1. A position nobody else holds.
2. Data for the topic, you for the take.
3. A lot of volume on one platform.
4. A high quality bar.
5. Real obsession.

### Examples

- **A year of building with AI:** One builder made more in 3 months than in the previous 2 years, then admitted half of it was functional but forgettable, because it used the same prompts and defaults as everyone. They paused building to study design and storytelling for hundreds of hours.
  <br>*Why it works:* Output is not quality. Taste fills the gap.
- **AI ads and trust:** In one university study, AI-made ads beat human ones by 19% on clicks, but the likelihood of buying dropped by 33% once people learned the ads were AI-made.
  <br>*Why it works:* This is a stat creators cite, so check it before reusing it. It shows why trust in the human behind the work matters.

### Common mistakes

- Prompt, accept, ship, with no judgment.
- Confusing output with quality.
- Spreading across 5 platforms and giving each a fifth of your effort.
- Being average, because the algorithm punishes average.
- Treating followers as the moat instead of an active community.

### Try it

Take one AI draft you have. Rewrite only the first two sentences by hand, and write down what the default version did wrong. That is your taste at work.

## 1.15 Plans, timelines and staying power

*11 min read*

> **The big idea:** This lesson gives you a concrete plan for the first 90 days and a realistic timeline for the years after. Many creators and investors say the same hard thing: it takes longer than you think, and the people who win keep going.

### A 90-day plan

- Days 1 to 3 are for positioning. Answer three questions: which game do you play (mass fame or category fame), who exactly do you help (your authority statement), and what are your 4 brand pillars (your niche bullseye, your list of contrarian takes, what you want to be linked with, and what makes you unique). Lessons 5, 7 and 8 explain these.
- Days 4 to 90 are for content. A content formula is a topic, plus a format, plus a visual layout. Aim for 3 to 4 topics, 1 to 3 formats and 1 to 3 layouts, which gives you 5 to 10 formulas.
- In days 1 to 60, make about 50 posts: 3 in the first week, 5 in the second and then 7 a week. Test widely and wildly. The only filter is whether the post is valuable to your ideal viewer.
- Then keep a formula only if it passes 4 checks: it performs, it brings the right people (read the comments and the bios of new followers), you enjoy making it and you are proud of how it looks. Double down on what passes, cut what doesn't, and repeat. Formulas burn out, so go back to testing.

### A personal brand playbook

- You have less competition than you think. Say 100 people in your niche announce they will start a personal brand. 80 never start, 10 quit early, 5 chase a shiny new thing and 2 get unlucky. That leaves 3. These numbers are made up to show the point, not from a study.
- What to work on depends on your numbers. If you are stuck at 0 to 500 views, ask why anyone would care: is it entertaining or valuable, does it look good, does it stop the scroll? At 1,000 views and up, work on structure: the idea, the hook and the delivery. At 10,000 to 50,000, find your best posts and repeat them, but run a signature series at most about 3 times a month or it dies. At 100,000, build systems, a team and a process for every post.
- Review your 4 pillars monthly. Look at saves, views and shares, then drop, replace or fix a pillar. Keep testing new things even when old ones work, because it won't work forever.
- To make money, remember that why they watch is why they buy. Start with a contact in your bio, then add an email list and simple landing pages, then services, low-priced teaching and finally done-for-you high-priced work.
- Four formats that work. Free work: one designer made merch for billion-dollar brands unasked, and they replied. Niche-niche playbook: very specific advice, like "3 chest exercises for men over 50". Would and wouldn't: your picks, like "10 things I'd buy at Trader Joe's as a food scientist". Curation: saving people time by sorting the good from the rest.
- A realistic timeline: 30 days in, you realize you can do this. By 100 days it is a habit. By 365 days, expect the first money. In year 2, you know enough to make a real plan. In year 3, stick to it. After 5 years, you can do almost anything.
- Identity marketing is another idea worth knowing. In a lonelier world with fewer places to gather, brands became the way people find their people. People buy brands for five identity reasons: belonging (a sneaker brand), feeling better than others without saying so (a luxury label), being a rebel (a canned water brand with a skull logo), standing out (a loud, unusual item) and pride in a subculture ("if you know, you know"). There is also a sixth, private reason: fixing a weak spot in someone's identity. For example, a company selling electric hydrofoil boards found older men who thought they were done with extreme sports could feel young again. In an ad test, buyers split about 50/50 between those who wanted to see someone like themselves and those who wanted to see the younger person they wish they were.
- Four questions to ask about any audience: who are they, who do they think they are, who do they want to be, and who do they want to hear from?

### Expect it to take time

- A common warning from people who build brands: don't expect any meaningful result for the first 36 months. Plenty of people pop at 3, 6, 9 or 12 months, but not most. It is better to expect nothing for 3 years and be shocked at month 9 than to expect a pop at 3 months and be in year 2 with little to show. That isn't about lowering your ambition. Think of a personal trainer. If you want to lose 60 pounds in 2 months, a good trainer resets you to 4 to 6 pounds. Most people burn out from wrong expectations, not from bad content strategy or positioning.
- People start with huge expectations, especially in the first 6 months. They compare themselves to top creators while not doing the work those results needed. A brand strategist hears "build me what a famous creator has with a whole team behind him" often, and usually turns it down, because most people won't invest the money, time, attention and privacy it takes.
- It takes hundreds and hundreds of posts. There is no version where 3 weeks of posting dominates a category. The formula works in every niche if you have the patience, discipline and obsession to run it for as long as it takes.
- You will not see huge results in under a year. Even if you do, don't expect to.
- One investor's golden rule: create value for people and you receive value back, as their attention. He didn't set out to grow followers, but to create value. His edge is consistency. He says it is really hard to keep someone down if they keep showing up, and that he was the worst player and the "dumbest one" in his class at a top university.
- He also says much of what looks like luck is the combined result of thousands of small actions. For example, he went to the gym at 5 a.m. every day, and six months of daily chats with one regular, who was a major tech CEO, became one of his most important mentor relationships, with no end goal.
- He changed his mind on hard work, from an extreme view (100-hour weeks, 4 hours of sleep) to taking care of your whole self. His definition of high performance is being better than a month ago and much better than two months ago. He also stays even-keeled, a lesson from baseball: never too low, because you are one pitch from getting out of any situation, and never too high, because you are one pitch from getting hit.
- Longevity is the key. Most great investments pay off over the long term, so stay in the game, which is why having a team matters.

### 7 principles for lasting brands

- These are mostly about mindset. 1) Consistency: the vlogger Casey Neistat posted a video every day for 800 days. The math: 100 reps, each 10% better, adds up to about 13,800 times better. That does check out, since 1.1 multiplied by itself 100 times is about 13,800.
- 2) Pain builds resilience: Nvidia nearly died in 1995, and its CEO, Jensen Huang, kept going. 3) Obsession with the craft: MrBeast had about 20 subscribers in 2012. 4) Make what you love and think of the audience last, a view held by the music producer Rick Rubin. 5) Delusional self-belief: Michael Jordan was cut from his high school team. 6) Mastery takes decades: the sushi chef Jiro still says he hasn't perfected rice. 7) Insanely simple: Steve Jobs cut the iPhone down to almost no buttons.
- One specific growth story, as the creator tells it: hand-drawn concept images for founders on LinkedIn brought in 110,000 followers in a month.
- Every business today is partly a media company. Content is essential and you can't hand it off right away, because the founder has to understand it first.
- Focus on improving video by video, because it stacks up. Reasons for starting differ: for one creator, content was a tool for personal growth that pushed them past social anxiety.

### Frameworks

**The 90-day plan**

1. Days 1 to 3: pick your game, your authority statement and your 4 pillars.
2. Days 1 to 60: make about 50 posts and test widely and wildly.
3. Keep a formula only if it performs, brings the right people, you enjoy it and you are proud of it.
4. Double down, cut and repeat.

**What to work on at each stage**

1. 0 to 500 views: why would anyone care?
2. 1,000 and up: structure of idea, hook and delivery.
3. 10,000 to 50,000: find outliers and repeat them, with a signature series at most about 3 times a month.
4. 100,000: systems, team and process.

**The timeline**

1. 30 days: I can do this.
2. 100 days: it is a habit.
3. 365 days: expect the first money.
4. Year 2: make a real plan.
5. Year 3: stick to it.

### Examples

- **100 people down to 3:** Of 100 people who say they will start a personal brand in your niche, 80 never start, 10 quit early, 5 chase a shiny thing and 2 get unlucky. 3 are left.
  <br>*Why it works:* These are made up numbers, not a study. The point is that staying power is rare.
- **The trainer's reset:** If you tell a personal trainer you want to lose 60 pounds in 2 months, they reset your goal to 4 to 6 pounds.
  <br>*Why it works:* A brand works the same way. Wrong expectations burn people out faster than bad strategy.

### Common mistakes

- Expecting results in 3 months.
- Comparing yourself to top creators in your first 6 months.
- Keeping a formula just because it worked once, since formulas burn out.
- Repeating a signature series more than about 3 times a month.

### Try it

Write your plan for the first 60 days: your 3 to 4 topics, 1 to 3 formats and the number of posts per week. Then write down the four checks for keeping a formula and put a day-60 review in your calendar.

## Cheat sheet: Personal brand and positioning

The whole module on one page.

- Decide what you want to be known for in 20 years before you make content.
- Work backwards: the outcome you want, the reputation it needs, the actions that earn it, and the skills for those actions.
- Do the thing first, then talk about it. Never pretend to be something you are not.
- Show up as an expert or as a student, whichever is true. Never fake being the expert.
- Keep a list of your real wins, failures and lessons. They are your proof.
- A brand is one pairing repeated over and over, like Michael Jordan wearing Nike every game.
- Write down what you do not want to be linked with, and stay away from it.
- Start in a small niche and widen only as your proof grows.
- Own one category before you try to own five.
- Check how big your market really is before you blame your niche.
- Make each idea work for hardcore fans, casual viewers and strangers.
- Be able to say what you stand for in one sentence.
- Keep 3 to 5 beliefs most people in your field disagree with, and repeat them.
- Disagree with ideas, not people. Never make a person your enemy.
- If you sell something, aim for trust, not for going viral.
- Name the things you teach, and give vague words in your niche a clear meaning.
- Share real weaknesses. Never fake them.
- Build a world around your brand: places, characters, your own language and paths between them.
- Show strong proof and let people reach the conclusion themselves.
- Obsession is time spent doing the thing, and fake obsession gets noticed.
- In the AI era, taste and trust are what protect your brand.
- Expect about 36 months. Staying power wins.
