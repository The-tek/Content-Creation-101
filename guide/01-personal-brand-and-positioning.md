# Module 1: Personal brand and positioning

*Decide what you want to be known for, prove it, repeat it, and give it time.*

This module covers the foundation of a personal brand. Trust is the strategy, and a brand is an association in people's heads between you and one thing. The lessons teach how to pick a destination, prove it with real credibility, choose a niche and position, stand out with contrarian takes and a world your audience lives in, and last in the AI era with taste and trust. They agree it takes a long time and that staying power is the real edge.

## 1.1 Why personal brands win now

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** People trust people faster than they trust companies, and trust is what turns attention into money. A personal brand is not the business itself, it is the traffic source and the trust engine behind it.

### Trust is the whole strategy

- Your content and the quality of your ideas, over a long enough time, build a brand people "can't help but trust". That is the entire business strategy. "Money is a measure of trust."
- A personal brand is not a business, it is a traffic source. Founders use one to get users for a startup, e-commerce brands use UGC to sell products, and you can sell nearly anything with a trusted personal brand.
- Brands without a personal brand must build trust fast or lean on deeper direct response tactics, which opens the door to unethical tactics and a focus on quick money. With a trusted brand "your content has already done the selling", so you don't need to obsess over landing pages and headlines.
- Beginners should forget about niche, bio and banner at first. They matter, but plenty of people with no bio and a blank profile picture do fine. Content quality over time matters more.
- Beyond views and cash flow, the most valuable thing a founder builds is the brand, which is the emotional connection between you and your customers. Nobody knows that connection as well as the founder.
- A common claim, not proven: a top tier personal brand is "the single greatest ROI marketing bet" right now.

### People follow people, not companies

- People follow Elon Musk, Steve Jobs and Sam Altman, not Tesla, Apple or OpenAI. The accounts people actually follow are people.
- One creator's claim: the CEO of PayPal hired a head of content (a job post of about \$180,000 a year) to build the CEO's personal brand and point that attention at PayPal. CEOs of huge companies are hiring their own creative directors.
- Tony Robbins has many programs but the brand is Tony Robbins. CEOs who build a personal brand in the next 5 years will get ahead because they understand distribution.
- One creator changed their advice: they used to say build both a company brand and a personal brand. Now they say put all the effort into the personal brand, then point it at your companies.
- A 2x2 asks two questions. 1) Does the buyer need to be taught how your solution solves their problem? Candy, clothes, a filter app: no, so entertainment that creates a feeling. Tax help, coaching, agencies, complex software: yes, so education. 2) What is the fastest way to their trust? Almost always a person: "people trust people faster than amorphous brands".
- A brand account wins only in consumer goods and retail, where buyers must remember a brand name in a store, and even then the founders often host it (example: Midday Squares).
- People prefer to hear from a personal page rather than a brand page unless the product is groundbreaking. Early stage companies are dominated by founder-led content.
- EXM7777: courses, communities and creator products sell through X and YouTube long-form first "because people buy from someone they trust".

### Founder-led, brand-led and the whole team

- Founder-led content is first person (opinions, stories, "how I'm building X", behind the scenes, lessons). It builds trust fastest because people follow people. Risk: the founder's personal opinions can hurt the brand.
- Brand-led content is third person (launches, campaigns). It scales better and is worth more if you sell the business, but it is harder to relate to.
- In 2026 companies should run both. The founder account says why we're building it and what I'm learning; the brand account does launches and updates.
- Employee-led content is a working format for businesses with 20+ employees, and operators who compared notes called employees building their own brands "a massive advantage". Companies and employees should both be on X every day finding talent and connections, because X is "the best networking platform in the world".
- "The actual account itself is not the driver, the world is". Founder, team, creators and customers now push more than brand accounts and ads. At a Cannes panel, marketers from Wispr Flow, ManyChat and Motion all recommended running 30%+ of Meta ads from people's accounts (whitelisting).
- Personal brand 1.0 was attention (look at my life). 2.0 is teaching: creators now replace mentors and teachers for career, money and skills.

### The content CEO and founder positioning

- "You can't run a 2025 business with a 2015 playbook." The old CEO is stuck firefighting in meetings on a business that is a house of cards relying on them. The content CEO focuses on building trust, distribution and leverage.
- Four systems move a founder up two axes, brand and leverage: an authority engine (answers "no one will listen to me"), a content waterfall system ("I don't have time"), a content to revenue bridge ("how will this make money") and a team multiplication system ("I'll have to do it all alone").
- "Every business these days is a media company." Content is essential, and it can't be delegated straight away: the founder must deeply understand it first.
- One investor never built his audience as a venture investor. His audience is "real people looking to learn, grow, explore", which he says is more powerful because they are real customers of the companies he invests in.
- VC associates told to "build an audience" end up with an audience of other VC associates liking each other's posts, so company announcements only reach their own little ecosystem. A firm's content needs a real reason for being (Andreessen's Future is named as the best example).
- One view: VC is now a barbell. Big brands on one side, and on the other people with a clear value lever such as an audience that gives founders access to customers. Capital alone as a value proposition "is completely dead". With a platform, small checks get into good deals because the founder gets distribution (example: a portfolio announcement video with 50,000 views in 24 hours plus a Twitter Space with several hundred people).

### Personal brand is not the same as being a creator

- A personal brand is "the perception others have of you when you're not in the room", built to help your career.
- The levels: level 0 = everyone online. Level 1 = the brand is an extension of what you do and does not define you (where most people should stop). Level 2 = you are known for it and make some money from it. Level 3 = the brand is bigger than you (GaryVee, Tim Ferriss).
- One personal brand community claims these outcomes from members' brands: one member raised his first check for an AI SaaS at about 6,000 followers; another collaborated with Bill Gates's daughter after 185+ posts; another made \$80,000 in a month from his agency with many leads from his brand; another made a \$20,000 B2B sale. The point: improve video by video, it stacks up.

### Frameworks

**The trust matrix**

1. Growth: doing what works to attract people
2. Authenticity: expressing your core beliefs, so you attract the right people
3. Authority: displaying expertise; framed as "persuasive education changes behavior" and arguably the most important

**Personal brand or brand account (2x2)**

1. Question 1: does the buyer need to be taught how the solution works? Yes = education, no = entertainment that creates a feeling
2. Question 2: what is the fastest way to their trust? Almost always a person
3. Two money games: advertising (brand deals, needs a personal brand) or conversion (sell your own thing, all four boxes can work)
4. Then study only accounts in your own box and niche: find one, go through who they follow, list 10 to 20, sort by outliers
5. Test for an education account: after their last 10 posts, could you apply something yourself? If not, it is entertainment dressed as education

**The content CEO's four systems**

1. Authority engine: answers "no one will listen to me"
2. Content waterfall system: answers "I don't have time"
3. Content to revenue bridge: answers "how will this make money"
4. Team multiplication system: answers "I'll have to do it all alone"

### Examples

- **The PayPal job post:** One creator says the CEO of PayPal hired a head of content, about \$180,000 a year, to build the CEO's own personal brand and send that attention to PayPal.
  <br>*Why it works:* It is used as a sign that even huge companies now see the founder's personal brand as distribution. The number is the creator's own claim.
- **The VC echo chamber:** Investors told to build an audience ended up followed by other investors, so their portfolio announcements only reached their own small circle. One investor built an audience of real people instead, who are customers of the companies he backs.
  <br>*Why it works:* Who the audience is matters more than its size: an audience of peers gives no distribution to the businesses you care about.

### Common mistakes

- Building only a company account when the buyer needs to be taught and trust a person.
- Building an audience of peers who like each other's posts instead of real customers.
- Delegating content before the founder deeply understands it.
- Leaning on aggressive direct response tactics because no trust was built first.

### Where the sources disagree

One view: put all effort into the personal brand and point it at the company. Another: companies in 2026 should run both a founder account and a brand account, with the brand account handling launches and updates. A third adds that a brand account can win in consumer goods and retail, usually with founders still hosting it.

### Try it

Take any business you know (yours, a client's, one you admire). Answer the two questions from the 2x2 in writing: does the buyer need to be taught how it works, and what is the fastest route to their trust? Then list which people in that business (founder, team members) could each run a personal account, and one topic each would post about.

## 1.2 Start from the end: what you want to be known for

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Before any content, decide where the brand is going: the outcome you want and the reputation that gets you there. Every post can then be judged by whether it moves you closer or further away.

### Foundation before tactics

- Most people building a personal brand are flooded with "copy these formats, steal this hook" advice. The claim, after 17 years of building personal brands: none of it matters without a strong foundation. Know where you are going before your next (or first) piece of content.
- The car analogy: if you get in a car with no destination you cannot pick a route, and you cannot measure whether you are getting closer or further away.
- Personal branding is "not an art, it's a formula". After studying hundreds of top personal brands (Codie Sanchez, Tony Robbins, Martha Stewart), the formula applies in pretty much any category.
- Most strategists sprint straight to content strategy and amplification without knowing what the brand stands for, what makes it different, why the person is worth listening to, or what world they are inviting the audience into.
- Most personal brands fall apart because they skip this step and build "on a foundation of mud and dirt".

### Known for and paid for

- Start with one question: when I wake up in 20 years, what do I want to be known for and paid for?
- The 2x2 has one axis for how well you're paid and one for how well you're known. Not known and not paid is where everyone starts. Paid but not known: experts paid to coach or speak whom nobody knows. Known but not paid: people with millions of followers who can't make money. The goal is known for and paid for the right thing.
- The biggest mistake is "leaning your ladder against the wrong wall": making content about things you don't want to be known for in 10 years, or growing an audience in a niche you hate. You win, then hate your audience and want to start over.
- Check the topic fits your 25 and 50 year goals. Think about yourself at 50 while you are in your 20s: what are your 5, 15 and 30 year visions? If you build a brand around content and end up hating content, you won't be glad you built it.
- Optimize for the algorithm instead of what you want and you get stuck as "this one person", making content you start to hate. Decide the 10-year answer first: it will be more helpful than any of the other tips.
- You invest time, money and privacy in a personal brand. Without a strong why, when "the storms come" you are far less likely to stick with it.

### Keep a north star

- If you hit your follower goal (10K, 25K), what would it tangibly change in your life? Remember why you do content so you are not shooting in the dark.
- For one creator, content was a personal development tool: it pushed past social anxiety (scared to raise a hand in class in high school).
- A good question to ask is "to serve who?": start with the end goal. Founders who build a brand only as a lead channel become "a slave to that format", while founders who document out of curiosity attract more people.
- Be clear with yourself, and with anyone you hire, what the main objective is (views, sales of a niche product, or simply paying bills while making what you love). Creators who ask for more views and then dislike making broader videos fall into the "alt band" trope.

### Build the mission into the brand

- Most people quit because they have no clear picture of what their brand looks like in 6 months, let alone 2 years. They post random topics, get no traction and assume they have nothing to say.
- Your mission is a north star made of two dots. Point A (one sentence): the life your reader is trying to escape, your anti-vision and "the enemy". Point B (one sentence): the life you help them move toward, your vision. Mission = help as many people as possible go from A to B, and every idea must live between the two points.
- Movement marketing: share your mission and message in your content. "People follow momentum. People follow people that are moving." One business YouTuber's mission is helping entrepreneurs build a business they don't grow to hate, and people repeat it for him.
- On MrBeast: his business is mission-driven. In content he obsesses over making the perfect video, in money he has a deep desire to help people in need, and these two missions make every video "mean more than your average creator".

### Frameworks

**The Brand Journey Framework**

1. What is your desired outcome? What do you want from this personal brand?
2. What would you have to be known for to get that outcome? This names the reputation you need
3. What do you need to do to be known for that? You are known for doing, not only talking
4. What do you need to learn to do those things? The skills to build before you can start
5. Then test every action: is it moving me closer to the desired outcome or further away?

**Known for and paid for 2x2**

1. Not known, not paid: where everyone starts
2. Paid, not known: experts nobody has heard of
3. Known, not paid: big followings that earn little
4. Known and paid for the right thing: the goal

**Mission as Point A to Point B**

1. Point A: one sentence on the life your reader wants to escape (the enemy)
2. Point B: one sentence on the life you help them reach (the vision)
3. Mission: help as many people as possible go from A to B
4. Every idea and every post must sit somewhere on that path

### Examples

- **The ladder on the wrong wall:** Creators grow an audience on a topic they don't want to be known for in 10 years, win, and then hate their own audience and want to start over.
  <br>*Why it works:* Winning on the wrong topic is a loss that takes years to notice, so the 10-year question comes before every tactic.

### Common mistakes

- Jumping to hooks and formats before knowing the destination.
- Picking a topic you won't want to talk about in 10 years.
- Chasing the algorithm until you are stuck as a person making content you hate.
- Not telling yourself or your team what the main objective is.

### Try it

Write four lines, one per question of the Brand Journey Framework: outcome, what you must be known for, what you must do, what you must learn. Then write Point A and Point B as one sentence each. Look at your last 10 posts (or 10 posts you plan) and mark each one closer or further.

## 1.3 Credibility first: do the thing, then talk about it

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A personal brand stands on what you have actually done. Get real proof (or be honest that you are still learning), then talk about it. Faking expertise gets spotted.

### You cannot shortcut credibility

- The "painful truth": the success of a personal brand rests on what you have actually done, your accomplishments, successes, failures and experiences. You cannot really shortcut it.
- Skepticism online is at an all time high. Viewers immediately wonder "can I actually trust this person? Are they legit?"
- Expertise earns trust, but it takes more than expertise to create long term loyalty (that comes from worldbuilding, lesson 11).
- Most people post to GET credibility. That is backwards: get proof in real life first, then use it on social media. One well-known business YouTuber built and sold 3 software companies before his first post.
- A leader has followers. If you are not a leader in real life, you won't get followers online. Boil down why anyone should follow you: what do you help people with?
- Proof over promise. Skepticism is high because people got burned by frauds. Promises alone used to work; now share the promise AND the proof.

### Do cool stuff first

- A joke recipe: "How to get 1M views in your first week: 1. spend years doing cool [stuff] 2. start posting about it 3. blow up." Build real skills, experience, network and knowledge before posting; people can tell when you are not faking it.
- The best growth hack is having genuinely interesting things to share, which come from access, lived experience, skills, results and personality. If a brand isn't growing, 90% of the time it is missing these, or it has a packaging problem.
- Don't larp (pretend). Only talk about things you've done and are good at. One creator tried a personal brand at 16 and it flopped because there were "no insights to give, no skills, no value".
- Fake gurus are easy to spot: no credentials, never did anything interesting, generic advice. The same business YouTuber mastered the software, coaching and investor niches before making general business content.
- One creator who ran TikTok for a big entrepreneur's team built his whole strategy on shining a light on the cool things that entrepreneur and his family had already done.
- Lots of creators only make content about content. A better split is filming 3 hours a week and spending the rest "doing cool stuff", which is where new stories, analogies and experience come from.
- One creator switched from "how to" content to "how I" content, because "I shouldn't be teaching anything I haven't done myself".
- "Don't ask what should I post, ask what have I earned the right to explain." The dinner test: could friends grill you on the topic for 30 minutes with zero prep?

### Student route or expert route

- The fork in the road is about how the audience sees you (experts should always keep learning; that is not the point here).
- Expert: has accomplished what the audience wants, has "been there and done that", and teaches what they did and how the audience can apply it.
- Student: has not accomplished it yet and is on the journey toward the same goal as the audience. Works "like a scientist": runs experiments and shares hypotheses and results along the way.
- There is no right or wrong "other than the truth". A student who positions as an expert "is really just lying". The claim: students get better results and more traction leaning into what they actually are.
- If you have no experience yet, be a documenter, not an educator. Run experiments and break down what others did (case studies, documentaries, breakdowns). An ideal path over 5 to 15 years: documenter, then go do impressive things, then talk about what you did.
- You don't need to be an authority. You need to be genuinely interested and one or two steps ahead of the reader, and even a different way of thinking about an idea is valuable on its own.
- Some of the most compelling content comes from people writing about what they are actively learning. "Here's what I'm figuring out" has a rawness polished experts can't copy.

### Bank your receipts

- The Credibility Bank (for experts): list your credibility stories, successes and failures, each with the takeaway your audience can use. It tells people why you are worth listening to.
- The Interest Bank (for students): list what you are pursuing and how you are pursuing it. It doesn't have the same effect as credibility, but you can show what you do differently from "your average Jill or Joe".
- These are your "receipts": the substance you then turn into a brand.
- Become your own case study. One ghostwriter started with no testimonials, only his own posts that went viral, so he could promise similar posts. "Be so good at what you do that people can't help but pay to learn from you."
- Produce work people can judge. "I understand AI marketing" is weak; a workflow that solves a real marketing problem is strong. Builds, experiments and teardowns show the result and the judgment behind it.
- Don't only show the polished demo. Show what went wrong, what you rejected, what the tool couldn't handle and why you chose one approach. That is where people learn how you think.

### Keep stacking proof

- Proof stacking: keep adding new proof on top of old proof, and talk about what you are GOING to do as well as what you've done. Pick a public mission.
- One creator's example: they grew a well-known business YouTuber's brand (proof 1), then started a public mission to grow their own Instagram from 0 to 100K with just an iPhone (proof 2), then will move to a third. They can talk about that first growth for maybe 5 to 10 more years before it gets stale.
- A well known business creator built and sold a gym company (the claim is about \$42M), then grew his personal brand, then (the claim) sold \$106M of books last year.
- Proof stacking keeps you an expert 10 years from now. Many people settle on what they did 10 to 20 years ago.
- Many people have done impressive things but are scared to talk about it because it feels like bragging. The view: by not sharing it you aren't helping, because people with less experience end up giving your audience the advice, the wrong way.
- Stop taking content advice from people who haven't done it, like someone with 500 followers. Find people who built real personal brands and ask them.

### Frameworks

**Student route vs expert route**

1. Expert: has done what the audience wants and teaches it
2. Student: on the same journey, runs experiments like a scientist and shares results
3. Pick the one that is true; posing as an expert when you are a student is lying

**Credibility Bank and Interest Bank**

1. Credibility Bank: each success and failure plus the lesson the audience can use
2. Interest Bank: what you are pursuing, how, and what you do differently
3. Use both as the raw material (your receipts) for content

**Documenter to doer path**

1. Document: experiments and breakdowns of what others did
2. Do: go and achieve impressive things yourself
3. Talk: teach what you did, then keep stacking new proof

### Examples

- **Proof stacking:** After growing a well-known business YouTuber's brand, one creator started a public mission to take their own Instagram from 0 to 100K followers with only an iPhone, so there is a second case study when the first gets old.
  <br>*Why it works:* New proof on top of old proof keeps you current, and a public mission gives people something to follow.
- **The flop at 16:** One creator tried to build a personal brand at 16 and it flopped because there were no insights, no skills and no value to give.
  <br>*Why it works:* Content can't create substance that isn't there; it amplifies what you have done.

### Common mistakes

- Posting to get credibility instead of getting credibility and then posting.
- Positioning as an expert when you are really a student.
- Only making content about content, with no new experiences to draw on.
- Hiding real achievements because it feels like bragging.
- Only showing the polished result and never the decisions and failures behind it.

### Try it

Spend 10 minutes filling a Credibility Bank or an Interest Bank (pick the one that is true for you). Write 5 lines, each with a real event or pursuit and the lesson someone else could use. Then run Codie Sanchez's dinner test on each: could friends grill you on it for 30 minutes with no prep?

## 1.4 What a brand really is: association

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A brand is not a logo or a color palette. It is an association in people's heads between you and one thing, built by repeating that pairing again and again.

### Brand is a pairing of things

- Most people overcomplicate brand (logo, colors, fonts) in ways that give no actions. The operational definitions: branding is a pairing of things. Good branding is an intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently. A brand exists when your audience associates two things together.
- Michael Jordan wore Nikes every game, every night, not just the finals or the All-Star game. Without that consistency the association never forms. Nike borrowed what people felt about Jordan ('greatness personified') by pairing with him over and over, and those feelings moved to Nike.
- The Nike swoosh means nothing on its own. It got its meaning from Jordan wearing it. Be deliberate about what and who your brand gets linked with.
- Association is 'probably the most important concept to understand in all of marketing'. Tony Robbins is a performance coach, Gary Vee is social media and marketing, Martha Stewart is homemaking. That reflex of joining a person to a category is association. The line: 'personal brands are just associations at scale.'
- The word branding comes from cattle ranches, where ranchers burned a mark into a cow so anyone seeing it linked the cow to its owner. A personal brand tries to burn your name into a category, so people think of you when they think of that category.

### How the association gets built

- The only way to build an association in someone's mind is to expose them to it over and over. The best way is content, which is why content is the engine of personal branding. It spreads the association at scale.
- An example: JP Morgan had a strong brand in finance in the 1800s but could only show it in person. You might be the best fashion designer in the world, but if nobody sees you doing or talking about fashion design, the association never scales.
- A four-point summary: 1) personal branding is association at scale. 2) Its strength follows the breadth and depth of how often people see the association. 3) It is built by you doing and talking about the connection with as many people as possible. 4) You can speed it up with personal brand amplifiers.
- If you do not repeat a few topics over time, you are known for everything, not something. Gary Vee and Mel Robbins repeat the same 5 to 7 (or 5 to 10) topics. Many people avoid repeating because they think the audience has heard it. The answer: repeat until people cannot help but connect you with those topics.
- One creator's goal is to be known for content creation, marketing and branding, 'and that's it'. They repeat the same core ideas with new analogies, stories and examples.

### Desired associations and anti-associations

- The exercise: decide what you want your audience to associate with you. Trust? Serious usefulness? Access to information that is hard to get? Access to rooms your audience cannot enter? One example is access to the strategies, systems and principles used with the biggest personal brands in the world. Take your 5 associations and pair yourself with them over and over.
- One main association is the contrarian belief that personal brands built to grow a business should optimize for trust, not virality. Almost every piece that is not a short clip includes it. 'I'm trying to wear the Nikes every game, not just sometimes.'
- Equally important is what you do NOT want to be linked with. A brand is built by what you avoid too. If you want to be known as trustworthy and you keep showing up next to scammy characters in your niche, your audience will see you through that filter.
- Beware of a personal brand that is 'more of a prison than a personal brand'.
- A version from a 90-day plan: ask 'if I could buy 2 or 3 words in people's memory, which ones?' (the Google AdWords of memory), then say them constantly. Objects help too: the same hat and hoodie in every video, Casey Neistat's taped sunglasses.

### Personal brand amplifiers

- Amplifiers are a cheat code. David Goggins (discipline) and Clavicular (looksmaxing) built near bulletproof associations in a few years (about 3 to 4 years at most for Clavicular).
- If exposure came only from content they posted themselves, reach would be limited by their time. Instead thousands of people spread the association through podcast reactions and viral clips. Rogan has clips about Goggins and discipline without Goggins on the episode, and hundreds of clipping accounts share them for free.
- Clavicular makes a lot himself and has 'weaponized a clipper army' posting across thousands of accounts. The claim: all top personal brands are tripling down on this, which is why personal brands now grow bigger and faster than before.

### Frameworks

**The definition ladder**

1. Branding = a pairing of things
2. Good branding = an intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently
3. Brand = your audience associates you and the thing you paired yourself with
4. Pair yourself with your 5 desired associations in every piece, and list what you do not want to be linked with

**The four points of association**

1. Personal branding is association at scale
2. Strength = breadth and depth of how often people see the association
3. Built by doing and talking about the connection with as many people as possible
4. Sped up by amplifiers: other people sharing clips and reactions

### Examples

- **Michael Jordan and Nike:** Jordan wore Nikes every game, not only the big ones. Over time people linked greatness with the shoe. Nike then borrowed that feeling by pairing itself with him again and again.
  <br>*Why it works:* Consistency is what creates the link. A pairing done sometimes does not stick.
- **Rogan and Goggins clips:** Clips of Rogan talking about Goggins and discipline get shared by hundreds of clipper accounts. Goggins is not even on those episodes.
  <br>*Why it works:* Other people can raise your exposure without you posting more, if the association is clear.

### Common mistakes

- Treating brand as a logo, fonts and colors instead of a repeated pairing
- Pairing yourself with a topic only sometimes, so the link never forms (the Nike point)
- Avoiding repeating your topics because the audience has heard them
- Showing up next to people or topics you do not want to be linked with

Write your 5 desired associations (what you want people to link with you) and 3 anti-associations (what you refuse to be linked with). Then check your last 10 posts: how many pair you with one of the 5?

## 1.5 Niche: start narrow, then widen

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** It is easier to own a small category than a big one, and easier to own one than five. Start in a small pocket where you are deep, then expand as proof grows.

### The two rules of association

- Rule 1: it is easier to be associated with a small category than a big one. Owning 'video storytelling for business owners' is far easier than owning 'business'.
- The spiderweb picture: being the person for business (like a big business creator now) needs deep expertise across every business model, every skill (traffic, funnels, offers, pricing) and every function (marketing, sales, legal, finance). That is a huge web, and starting out your strands would be thin and weak. 'Video storytelling for business owners' is traffic, then video, then storytelling, then business owners: 'the micro of the micro of the micro'. Fewer areas, thicker strands in the same time.
- A claim: this is why many people most have never heard of, with narrow positioning, do multiple seven figures.
- Rule 2: it is much easier to own one category than five. Every extra topic splits the content minutes that build each association.
- 'You are the niche' is an inferior strategy because of signal dilution (see lesson 7).
- Start narrow then broaden. Examples: Codie Sanchez (boring businesses, then general business), Steven Bartlett (CEO conversations, then general pop culture), Stephen A. Smith (basketball, then all sports, then politics).

### Pick the right pond, lake or ocean

- Once you know where you are going, pick the size of water to compete in. Example: a founder with one successful e-commerce business enters personal branding and picks the ocean of business as a whole. Competitors there built businesses for a long time, across many types. With one type of experience you can give value in your lane, but you do not stand a chance against the big dogs, 'at least not yet'.
- How to pick, not a 5-step exercise: what have you accomplished, what are your successes and failures, and how do they stack up against the ocean, lake or pond you are considering.
- Another example: the ocean is brand as a whole. Personal brand is the lake. The pond is personal branding for business owners, because that is where the biggest successes came from.
- Most people should start in a pond. Gary Vee started with wine, moved up to marketing, then business as a whole (leadership, customer fulfillment), then personal development.
- You will not be stuck there forever. The kicker: too many people jump from pond to lake to ocean only because they are bored of their topic. Only move up as your experience allows. Entering a lake or ocean with no experience means you repeat others' advice and get no traction. Build traction in the pond first, then 'hit a river into the lake'.

### The niche bullseye and the game you play

- A 90-day plan, days 1 to 3, asks three positioning questions. First: which game? Mass fame (a big business creator, Codie) or category fame (cult famous only inside your category). It is easy to start narrow and widen later and very hard to go back.
- Niche bullseye: draw rings from narrowest (for example social growth for business owners) to widest (business as a whole). Start in the center and widen as proof grows. In the center your topics, examples, words and proof all match the viewer.
- The cult fans lesson: aim for category fandom, not peak fandom. People keep folders in their head ('my favourite golf guy'). When a pain hits they open the folder and go to number 1. Being number 1 in a narrow category beats being number 5 overall, which is why branding around one word works.
- Do one thing better than 99% of people, not 99 things. One creator only wants to be known for content creation. Splitting into business, mindset, productivity and AI works for people at the top who already have a brand. Coming up, focus on one thing first, then expand.
- Stick to 1 to 3 topics, and if you cannot say what you stand for in one sentence, 'your follower conversion will be trash'.

### Narrow in practice: small niche, big market

- A common pet peeve is creators saying 'my niche is too small'. After working with hundreds of channels (the claim: thousands of channels, over 10 billion views) one team has 'yet to find a single niche where I can't get some traction'. It calls this a disempowering belief that sets a low ceiling.
- The team mantra: 'we can make literally any niche viral. Just give me the topics, the tools and the right direction.'
- Examples of small niches that are big: golf (the claim: about 30 million people play golf every weekend) and knitting (search it, sort by most viewed, and you find videos with millions of views). Real exceptions exist: something so hyper niche that only a few thousand people in the world care.
- Advice to founders building a following: focus on a niche early. Find 100 people who love it (for example, building cohesion between product design teams and customer support teams) and make them love you. Do not try to be everything to everyone. Figure out what you are really good at and own it.
- The founder angle: viral content has a huge market and appeals to beginners, but most founder businesses are niche (people making 50K or more, or big businesses). Content for them is more specific and will not have mass appeal.

### Pick the sub-niche you conquer first (the category winner playbook)

- The case: a wildlife creator. Signed in 2023, known for podcasts and TV shows, struggling on YouTube because he was going after everything.
- If you want to conquer a whole niche, you don't start with the whole niche. Every big niche splits into sub-niches. Start in one or two and widen as the channel grows. For the wildlife creator: extinct animals, dangerous animals and reptiles.
- Against the usual 'go as broad as possible' advice, the answer is 'No. Pick small battles first.' The claimed result: long-form views up 3,900%, from 30k a video to 3M, the biggest channel in the niche.

### Frameworks

**Pond, lake, ocean**

1. Ask what you have accomplished, and your successes and failures
2. Compare that to the pond, lake or ocean you want to compete in
3. Start in the pond where your experience can win
4. Move up only as your experience grows, never because you are bored

**Niche bullseye**

1. Draw rings from the narrowest version of your topic to the widest
2. Start in the center, where topics, examples, words and proof match one viewer
3. Widen only as proof grows
4. Pick category fame or mass fame first, it is hard to go back from broad to narrow

**The spiderweb**

1. A broad category needs a huge web of expertise
2. A narrow category is a small web you can cover fast
3. Same time spent gives thicker strands in the small web
4. Thick strands in a small web build association faster

### Examples

- **Gary Vee's path up the ponds:** Gary Vee started with wine, moved up to marketing, then to business as a whole (leadership, customer fulfillment), then to personal development.
  <br>*Why it works:* The brand followed his experience upward. It did not start at the top.
- **Codie Sanchez and Steven Bartlett:** Codie Sanchez started with boring businesses and then went to general business. Steven Bartlett started with CEO conversations and then went to general pop culture.
  <br>*Why it works:* Narrow first, wide later is the common path in these examples.

### Common mistakes

- Starting broad because you want more reach
- Splitting your content across 4 or 5 topics so every association stays thin (rule 2)
- Moving up from pond to ocean because you are bored of your topic
- Using 'my niche is too small' as the reason for low views

### Where the sources disagree

How narrow to stay. Many creators say start narrow and own one small category: one thing better than 99%, or find 100 people who love it. Another view says 'niche down and only talk about one thing' can land you in another 9 to 5. It says your niche is how you help people transform, not a narrow topic, and that a person can get interested in anything if it is presented well. It admits niching down works, but wants freedom to talk about anything, so it draws a broad net and then offers specifics to people inside the audience.

## 1.6 Size your niche: reach, core, casual and new

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A way to think about niche size: check how big your market really is, make every idea work for core, casual and new viewers, and be clear whether your goal is views or the right viewers.

### Measure your market the simple way (TAM)

- Do not overcomplicate total addressable market. Steps: 1) search your niche term on YouTube (for example golf). 2) Filter out Shorts using video length. 3) Sort by most viewed. 4) Ignore the freak outliers at the very top (a Sidemen golf video at about 34 million views is not a fair picture). 5) Scroll further and look at dedicated channels in the niche, such as a tutorial channel with many 1M-view videos. That level is roughly your TAM.
- Then compare. If the best channels get 1M views a video and you get 100,000, you are at 10% of what is possible before you blame the niche. Most people who call their niche too small get 5,000 to 10,000 views a video.
- Some niches are not yet fully capitalized by creators, so there may be even more viewership there.

### Go upstream: the niche sits inside bigger interests

- Your niche sits inside larger, cascading interests. Make videos that reach the broader category above it while still serving the niche.
- Case study, a business creator: good foundations (team, editing, decent thumbnails) but very niche topics like 6 tools to run a business, how to use Slack, Zoom vs Google Meet. The advice was bigger swings, such as asking millionaires for advice and general business videos a total beginner could enjoy.
- Case study, a horticulture lecturer in permaculture. His early videos were narrow (water harvesting). Then 'How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert' got 12 million views in 4 months, and several videos now pass 1M. He turned a small niche into a topic relevant to 12M or more people.

### CCN: core, casual, new

- The main framework: every idea should be clickable and enjoyable for three groups at once. Core: hardcore viewers who really care. Casual: dip in and out, care a bit. New: not into the niche, never seen your channel, but would click something interesting.
- The Sahara video works for all three: a permaculture student, a casual gardener, and someone who does not care about horticulture.
- Failure mode 1: too caught up in the core, making only technical videos for hardcore students. Failure mode 2: too caught up in the new viewer, going so broad you lose the audience you built. Randomly jumping on any trend does not bring your core with you.
- Growth means attracting new viewers, so broader appeal gives a better chance of a click from a stranger in recommendations.
- The hardcore-fan trap: if you are fan enough to start a channel about a niche, you will want to go very specific, which only appeals to people as hardcore as you.
- One test used when running a channel: 'the world's biggest creator needs to be able to watch this, and my mother needs to be able to watch this'.

### Order of operations: monkey bars

- It works better throughout a career to focus narrow, on the core, first, then expand. You cannot go the other way, because starting broad and narrowing alienates viewers.
- Monkey bars metaphor: start in one sub-niche, then because sub-niches overlap, swing to the next broader one, then the next. Music channels, for example, started with a very specific sub-genre (French deep bass and drum and bass), then European drum and bass, then broader.
- Creators who hit big at once with broad videos (one friend got millions of views on his first videos) are the exception, which is survivorship bias. Most channels at 100K to 1M views a video started more niche.
- Shorts version: know your core audience. In one example creator, shorts aimed at school kids did best. When videos did not appeal to school kids, views dropped, and when he targeted his core again, views jumped back.

### Views maximizer vs your actual goal

- Some creators call themselves view maximizers. But creators have other objectives. If you sell a niche product to hardcore viewers, broad videos may do little for the business, so aim for something like 2 to 3 million really targeted views a month instead of 10 million.
- The alt band trope: creators ask for more views, get them, then dislike making broader videos, like a band that misses small shows once famous. Be clear with yourself and anyone you hire about the main goal.
- Who should maximize views: people whose main revenue is ad revenue (AdSense, sponsorships, affiliates) and most channels below a full-time income. Caveat from the head of a YouTube agency: data shows very broad viral creators often convert sponsorships worse.
- Who should not: people who earn more from a small specific group, like someone selling a course on using Slack as a manager. A wild viral Slack video will not bring the right people. But you can borrow formats that went viral across niches, for example 'If I had to start over with Slack, here's what I'd do'. If you usually get 1,000 views and it gets 30,000, you maximized views and brought the right viewers.
- The same from the personal brand side: the sweet spot for most personal brands is 3 to 10K views a post, because viral does not equal money. Niche maxing (a small high-value audience) vs content TAM (how many people care): pick the overlap that serves your goal.

### Frameworks

**CCN: core, casual, new**

1. Core: hardcore viewers who care a lot
2. Casual: people who dip in and out
3. New: people outside the niche who would click something interesting
4. Test each idea against all three, and avoid both failure modes (only core, or only new)

**Simple TAM check**

1. Search your niche term on YouTube
2. Filter out Shorts, sort by most viewed
3. Ignore the freak outliers at the very top
4. Scroll to dedicated channels in the niche, that level is roughly your TAM
5. Compare your views to that level before blaming the niche

### Examples

- **The Sahara video:** A permaculture lecturer made narrow videos until 'How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert' got 12 million views in 4 months. Several others passed 1M.
  <br>*Why it works:* He kept the niche but picked a topic that core, casual and new viewers could all click.
- **If I had to start over with Slack:** An example for a niche seller: borrow a format that went viral across many niches. A 1,000-view channel getting 30,000 has maximized views and still brought the right viewers.
  <br>*Why it works:* Borrow the format, keep the audience.

### Common mistakes

- Blaming a small niche without checking what the best channels in it actually get
- Making ideas only for hardcore fans (the hardcore-fan trap)
- Going so broad you lose the audience you built, or jumping on trends
- Chasing views when you sell to a small group

### Try it

Do the 5-step TAM check on your niche. Write down what the dedicated channels get. Then compare your own typical views as a percentage of that number.

## 1.7 Positioning: promise, proof, perspective, personality

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Positioning is how you message so your association cuts through. It breaks into beliefs, messaging, signal and taste. A second view uses four Ps. A third asks if you have brand-market fit.

### Association vs positioning

- Association is which category you connect to. Positioning is how you message in content to carve your own lane so the association cuts through. Making content in a category does not give you a personal brand unless the association is sticky and people remember you reflexively.
- The four parts of positioning: beliefs, messaging, signal and taste.
- Beliefs: the core ideas, principles or takes you stand for in your category. Make them contrarian (lesson 8).
- Messaging: how you deliver the beliefs: fact-based or story-based, format, rhythm, tone, style. Mostly dealer's choice, which is why you can win in any niche in almost any format. Only two things are not optional: clarity, and consistency in sticking to your core contrarian beliefs.
- Pick messaging through constraints. Two starting constraints on YouTube were never leaving the office or moving the camera to make content, and not sharing every detail of the personal life. Work backward to the best format within them. Anyone can set their own constraints.

### Signal and taste

- Signal is the density or frequency of something interesting or valuable in your content. Out of 100 minutes someone spent with your content, how many were valuable to them? A higher percentage means stickier messaging and a deeper association.
- An example: you cover four topics and are incredible at video storytelling, but only 30 of 100 minutes aim at it, so your signal is 30/100. Someone who spends all 100 minutes on it with a 31% hit rate has more signal than you, even if they are 'horrible'. So do not fracture topic coverage.
- Keep signal high by making minutes non-obvious (new to the viewer) and tactically implementable (they can apply it alone and get the promised result). 'I say those two words so much, but they are the most important thing.'
- Taste is 'the consistency of your choices over time aligned with what the viewer thinks is quality'. People with great taste consistently link themselves with products, objects, people, settings and messages you see as high quality. Quality is subjective, so taste is the consistent curation of choice. High perceived taste puts a premium on the weight of your association. Taste is the choosing. How it looks is aesthetics (lesson 11).
- Personal brand = expertise + personality. Beliefs and signal roll up to expertise. Messaging and taste roll up to personality. Consumer niches almost always prioritize personality over expertise. B2B is the reverse, where more expertise than personality can cut through. You almost always need some of both.
- Summary line: the essence of positioning is contrarian framing, delivered clearly, consistently and as high signal as possible.

### The 4 Ps of positioning

- Promise: one clear promise of what you give the market (education or entertainment), again and again, not 10. One creator's is personal brand, content creation, marketing. A business YouTuber's is business and AI. Try not to stray, which is what keeps positioning strong.
- Proof: the reason anyone should listen to you. 'It's not about saying better things', it is giving people a reason to listen. Put it everywhere: bio, YouTube hooks, short-form hooks. People listen to people who have done what they want to do. Example: one business YouTuber had 3 exits before he made content.
- Perspective: people follow you for what you say that nobody else can. Contrarian takes, counter positioning and the Purple Cow idea are versions of it. It has to be novel, non-obvious, net new, built from your own life, stories and analogies. Before every piece ask: what unique perspective can I share that they have not heard?
- Personality: share some of yourself. One idea: a monthly carousel of what you did that month. For one business YouTuber they show his supercars, extreme sports, family, dog and wife.
- If views and followers do not turn into money, 'you're not positioned correctly', so go back to the 4 Ps. (The same list without personality is the 3 Ps.)

### Brand-market fit, category and emotion

- Everyone knows product-market fit, few think about brand-market fit. It means a specific audience instantly gets what you stand for, wants more of it and cannot get it anywhere else. Three questions: what do you stand for, who is it for, why should they care (what they get from you that they cannot get elsewhere)? Most brands stand next to 50 competitors and wonder why nobody notices them.
- Own a category, or create one. The strongest brands are often the only one in their category (examples: Fomo, Polymarket and Kalshi, Duolingo for gamified learning). Three ways: combine two worlds (AI with trading, fitness with finance), be first and execute well, or solve one specific pain point. If you cannot find what makes you different, the product (or for a creator, the skill) needs work first.
- Decide the emotion people should feel when they see you: smart, early, belonging, confident, inspired or rebellious. Make the audience the hero: what does someone get for giving you their attention? Say it the same way everywhere: the format changes per channel, the story, usefulness and emotion do not.
- One sentence test: if you cannot say what you stand for in one sentence, your follower conversion will be bad. Have real opinions, because neutral accounts feel forgettable.
- Profile basics: get verified, show your face, bio = what you do plus who it is for (2 to 3 lines), a simple banner, and a pinned post that converts. A claim: showing your face instantly kills about 80% of the competition that will not. The bio is also 'really not that important as long as it's not bad'.

### The authority statement and content buckets

- The second positioning question (90-day plan): who exactly? Write a target authority statement: 'I help X with Y in Z way'. It filters every idea. Without it you get viewer drift: you chase views, the message goes generic, and the audience stops buying.
- Four brand pillars, and 'personal branding is subtracting, not adding': niche bullseye, contrarian take vault, associations and objects, and points of uniqueness. For points of uniqueness, map 5 to 10 competitors on topic, depth, audience, format and visuals, then find the white space.
- Content buckets: first map what the person stands for. Then map 3 to 4 buckets that all back up the positioning and make every piece hit one. If a video does not hit one, do not make it. One business YouTuber's buckets were business, AI, mindset, relationships (or business, AI, productivity), all built from how he wants to be seen. Pick few buckets or you blend into the noise.

### Frameworks

**The four parts of positioning**

1. Beliefs: 3 to 5 contrarian frames you stand for
2. Messaging: any style, but clear and consistent
3. Signal: the share of your content minutes that are valuable (non-obvious and tactically doable)
4. Taste: the consistency of your choices over time

**The 4 Ps of positioning**

1. Promise: one clear promise, repeated
2. Proof: the reason anyone should listen
3. Perspective: what you say that nobody else can
4. Personality: some of yourself, shared on purpose

**Brand-market fit**

1. What do you stand for?
2. Who is it for?
3. Why should they care and why can they not get it elsewhere?
4. Pick the emotion they should feel

- **A business YouTuber's buckets:** His buckets were business, AI, mindset and relationships, all backing up one image: the AI business guy who is into mindset and has a great relationship with his family.
  <br>*Why it works:* Buckets come from working backwards from how the person wants to be seen.

- Covering many topics so signal is diluted
- Giving 10 promises instead of one
- Saying better things instead of giving a reason to listen (proof)
- Chasing views until the message goes generic, viewer drift
- Staying neutral with no real opinions

## 1.8 Contrarian takes and a point of view

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Your contrarian take is the strongest lever to stand out, 'not even close'. It is called the god tier tactic. A topic gives you things to post about, but a point of view gives those posts somewhere to go.

### Why the contrarian take is the strongest lever

- Of all the levers to stand out, the strongest 'by and large, it's not even close' is your contrarian take. The biggest personal brands do not blow up only because of titles, thumbnails and hooks. They have a fundamentally different way of approaching their niche than most in their pond, lake or ocean.
- Contrarian beliefs are 'the god tier tactic' for winning positioning. These are things you believe are true that most of your space would disagree with. It is called counterpositioning by some and contrarian framing by others.
- Some call it picking the hill you are willing to die on, and also name counterpositioning, unique mechanisms and the Purple Cow idea (be the cow in the field that stands out). A real perspective attracts the right people and repels the ones you do not want anyway.
- Examples: Gary Vee came on stage in a hoodie and beanie telling Fortune 500 brands to take Facebook and social media seriously, when the industry called social media and Gary a fad. Codie Sanchez said you can buy boring businesses when society thought the only way to get rich was being a tech founder. The claim: that was one of the main reasons she blew up.
- A contrarian action counts too, not only a belief. Example: most business creators sit in info, consulting or agency, but one also has an energy drink you can buy at Target and drink while watching his YouTube video. That holds a different place 'in the consumer's mind, heart, and hand'.

### Contrarian, not controversial

- Contrarian is a core belief you are 'incapable of not sharing' to help people. Controversial is said purely to get an emotion or trigger people. Do not make things up for a reaction.
- A warning: do not go 'contrarian take maxing', against the grain on everything.
- Three positioning moves: counter-position (the young guy in a basement vs polished gurus), mirror with a twist (same content, new delivery, such as a playbook on a whiteboard or agency tips through cult metaphors) and standout demographic (sales training for women in a bro niche).
- One creator's take: if you build your personal brand to grow a business, optimize your content for trust, not virality. They share it over and over. Their claim: a big reason for over 400,000 collective followers across platforms in the first 18 to 19 months, and creators who preached virality a couple of years ago have reoriented to trust.
- One creator's hill: everyone in content said lighting, gear and editing tricks matter most. They said topic matters more and filmed everything on an iPhone to prove it. They say the market started repeating it, and credit a few contrarian takes for growing from under 10K to 60K followers.

### How to find your takes

- The Two Column Approach: left column is everything you hear people say or see them do in your niche that you fundamentally disagree with. Right column is the opposite: your belief or action. Then test the takes in content, see which resonates most and double down. That winner becomes your most important association to pair yourself with.
- Another version: list the common beliefs in your space and write your counterarguments. You will not disagree with everything. The 3 to 5 things you staunchly believe everyone is wrong on become your core contrarian beliefs. Once documented, content gets simple (see lesson M5).
- A 90-day plan calls it a contrarian take vault: every time you strongly disagree with someone's post, write down what you believe instead. Most big brands run 3 to 5 macro contrarian frames with small takes under each.
- Pick 3 to 5 takes and repeat them again and again. Ask 'what are people in my niche saying that I don't agree with, that I'd go to the grave with?' and make a video about that.
- One approach is not watching anyone else in your category, so most ideas come from scratch and come out more contrarian. Sometimes this makes you wrong, too confusing or 'super out there', but that is where contrarian insight comes from.
- Your job is to create new, original concepts, formats, angles and hooks. Take inspiration but aim to become a real thought leader. Tread very carefully when copying others and be careful chasing views through pure trend-jacking.

### Point of view and narrative

- 'A topic gives you things to post about. A point of view gives those posts somewhere to go.' Build around a belief about what is changing in the market, then keep proving, challenging and explaining it. That turns positioning into narrative. (Another creator has a letter with the same idea: point of view over niche.)
- Salesforce did not position as a better CRM. It attacked the delivery model with 'The End of Software'. That changed the buyer's question from 'is this a better CRM?' to 'do I still want to buy software this way at all?' It was reinforced everywhere: messaging, launch, mock protests outside a Siebel conference, events.
- Gong: 'Goodbye opinions. Hello, reality.' The enemy was managing sales by gut feel. Gong Labs published research from real sales calls (talk ratios, pricing, competitor mentions). A salesperson could value the research without caring about Gong, but each study made the underlying argument more believable.
- Goal of content: help the audience with something real while strengthening the worldview behind the brand. Formats change, the conclusion keeps getting stronger.
- On news: do not ask 'how do I post about this too?' Ask 'what does this give me a chance to prove, question, or explain about the world I already talk about?' That is the difference between covering news and building a narrative.
- Start with the conclusion you want people to reach. Example: Bernays (Propaganda) sold pianos by making people want the music room. Architects designed music rooms, there were exhibitions, and people with taste talked about them. Then the piano felt like the obvious missing thing. Shape how people understand the problem and you shape what they look for in a solution.

### Frameworks

**The Two Column Approach**

1. Left column: what people in your niche say or do that you disagree with
2. Right column: the opposite, your belief or action
3. Test the takes in content and see which resonates most
4. Double down on the winner and pair yourself with it consistently

**Contrarian take vault**

1. Every time you strongly disagree with a post, write down what you believe instead
2. Keep 3 to 5 macro contrarian frames with small takes under each
3. Use the vault to make content decisions simple

**The belief path**

1. Ask: what do I want people to believe before they get to the offer?
2. A post makes them notice the problem
3. A teardown shows why the current approach breaks
4. Research proves it is happening broadly
5. A build or demo shows the better version
6. A customer story proves it works outside your bubble

### Examples

- **Gary Vee in a hoodie:** Gary came on stage in a hoodie and beanie telling Fortune 500 brands to take social media seriously while the industry called it a fad. It is obvious now and was wildly contrarian then.
  <br>*Why it works:* A contrarian take can look obvious later. What counts is that it was a real belief.
- **Salesforce and the End of Software:** Instead of claiming a better CRM, Salesforce attacked the whole way of buying software and kept repeating that story in messaging, launches and events.
  <br>*Why it works:* A point of view changes the buyer's question. A topic does not.

### Common mistakes

- Making up a take for a reaction, which is controversial, not contrarian
- Going against the grain on everything
- Calling out a specific person or company as the enemy (lesson 11)
- Covering news just to post about it, instead of using it to prove your worldview

## 1.9 Finding your gap and your market edge

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Three tools help you find the spot where what you can offer meets what the market wants: your unfair advantages, the layer under a hot conversation, and a content map that keeps every post on one path.

### Your market edge

- Ask: what am I genuinely exceptional at? What would me, a few years ago, want to read (that person becomes your audience)? Where do my skills overlap with a hot niche? What can people get from me that they cannot get anywhere else? 'Who else can say this?' If many people can, the angle is not unique.
- Turn your unfair advantages into content: your job, access, past and network. The five sources of unfair advantage: experience, access, results, perspective and taste.
- The line: 'you create world-class content by tapping into your unfair advantages that overlap with market demand'. Most people do only one side: post what they know (nobody cares) or chase trends (sound like everyone).
- The two starting mistakes: people either post whatever they know, or copy what is trending in a niche they like. Both end as copy and paste brands (example: crypto anon profiles that all look alike). Copy-paste brands do not grow.
- Pick a pocket in the market: overlap a hot niche with your skills. On X the brand should keep evolving because the platform is real-time.

### Find the gap you have a right to own

- The wedge: AI marketing content was exploding (n8n workflows, agents, demos), but it showed what tools could do, not how a real marketer would use them to make money. This creator came from marketing and growth, so asked 'what happens when somebody who understands marketing gets access to all of this?' They did not need to be the best automation expert.
- Method: find the conversation already getting attention and look one layer underneath. What keeps getting asked? Where does content stop right before it becomes useful? Then ask if you have something unusual to add: experience, access, data, taste, technical skill, or a useful mix of backgrounds.
- Adjacent subjects that reinforce the same association are fine (this creator expanded into AI and distribution, go-to-market, the marketer's future).
- 'A good content strategy is partly deciding what you refuse to become known for.' Leave some good ideas alone.
- Test before committing: can you already think of ten pieces of original work around it (things to build, test, explain, measure or argue from experience)? 'If all you have is a nice sentence for the bio, you probably have branding, not a position.'
- An 8-question worksheet: what does my audience already care about? What are they missing from the content they see? What do I have (experience, access, data, skills or taste) to add? What do I believe is changing? What does that mean for the audience? Which beliefs stop them seeing it the same way, and what evidence could change them? What language do I want them repeating? Once they agree, what should they do next? Use the answers as a filter for what you publish. You can also hand the answers to an AI agent to get a positioning statement, a 3 to 4 pillar strategy and 20 ideas, and flag anything too broad or generic.

### The content map

- Do it with pen and paper, not a notes app. Handwriting forces your brain to slow down. Most people quit because they have no clear picture of what the brand looks like in 6 months, so they post random topics, get no traction and think they have nothing to say.
- Stop treating posts as darts. Treat them as puzzle pieces that add to a bigger picture. As the map grows people have more reason to binge your work and you stop relying on one viral piece.
- Three reasons people follow a transformation-first brand: your post entertained, educated or inspired them; you talk about things they are interested in; they believe you can help them change their life. The first two come from consistency and quality. The third is the most impactful.
- Step 1, mission: two dots. Point A in one sentence is the life your reader is escaping (the enemy). Point B in one sentence is the life you help them reach. Mission is helping as many people as possible go from A to B (see lesson 2).
- Step 2, 2 to 3 broad topics, called territories. Broader than you think, because people scrolling are not searching for specific knowledge. Social media is for attention, not value. Too narrow and you are invisible to 95% of people who would benefit. Examples: lead gen for service businesses becomes online business, AI automation workflows becomes artificial intelligence, carnivore diet becomes health. Start with 1 to 2 topics if needed. Best topics sit where what you deeply care about meets what can help others.
- Step 3, 3 to 5 subtopics per broad topic, called cities, like a mind map. This is where your unique angle appears. Two people writing about business can be different brands: one on freelancing, sales and client work, another on digital products, content strategy and audience building. AI can list subtopics, but generate your own first.
- Step 4, 3 to 5 specific content ideas per subtopic (see M5). Do not judge them yet.
- This solves 'I have too many interests': philosophy, fitness, business and psychology look scattered until you see they all move someone from scattered focus to self-mastery.

### Authority without being the best

- Why should anyone learn from you when many teach the same thing? Teach through a new lens (your personal story, core beliefs, or a novel idea you found while researching), or persuade the non-interested (M4). An example lens: the 'ideas are the new oil' line, used to introduce personal branding.
- You do not need to be an authority. You need to be genuinely interested and one or two steps ahead. Actually you do not even need to be ahead: a different way of thinking about the idea is valuable alone.
- The zone of genius: for people leaving secure jobs, follow your energy, not in a fluffy way. Find where you play your own game. In finance one investor was good but would never be exceptional because he played by someone else's rules. Figure out what you can be truly exceptional at, because that is where you will have the energy to grind.
- A reintroduction post ends with a 'What I talk about on this page' list (6 topics in one example) and a one-line strategy, so a new visitor knows right away.

### Competitive advantage as a moat

- Everyone has some competitive advantage. Sometimes it's obvious: an entrepreneur who grew to 100k subscribers fast had good packaging, but 'there's probably under 10 people in the world who can write that title'. He spent a decade building the proof.
- Often it's less obvious: what do you have access to that most people don't? What do you know more about than others? What life events and experiences are only yours? Even being a beginner is one: less risk and less to lose.
- An example: it can be a billionaire giving life advice, or the fishing channel that lives beside the best trout river in America.
- Once you find a channel's advantage, every idea goes through one filter: 'could everyone just say this?'

### Frameworks

**Market edge check**

1. What am I genuinely exceptional at?
2. Where do my skills overlap with a hot niche?
3. Who else can say this? If many can, the angle is not unique

**The content map**

2. 2 to 3 broad topics (territories)
3. 3 to 5 subtopics each (cities)
4. 3 to 5 specific ideas per subtopic

### Examples

- **The AI marketing wedge:** AI content was full of demos of what tools can do. One creator came from marketing and asked what happens when a marketer gets all of this. They did not have to be the best automation expert.
  <br>*Why it works:* The gap was one layer under the hot conversation.
- **Two business brands:** Two people can both write about business. One covers freelancing, sales and client work. The other covers digital products, content strategy and audience building. They are two different brands.
  <br>*Why it works:* The subtopics are where your angle shows.

### Common mistakes

- Posting only what you know, or only chasing trends
- Staying so narrow that you are invisible to 95% of people who would benefit
- Having a nice bio sentence but no original work behind it
- Throwing random topics like darts instead of building a map

## 1.10 Authenticity, story and identity

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** People follow people, not ideas. Your story, your beliefs and what makes you different are what make the same idea land differently when you say it.

### People follow people, not ideas

- The idea that opens the section: artists and entrepreneurs are authentic by nature, and the businesses they build are authentic to their desires and means.
- An example: James Clear and a random guy both tweet 'habits are good for you'. Millions know Clear's story and book, so they like and repost. The random guy gets ignored or 'yeah, no shit' replies.
- Three drivers. Time under attention: the more attention people give you, especially in long form, the more they read your posts through the rest of your ideas. With two books, years of YouTube and 100K+ views per newsletter article, a creator can post simple things and people read them through 'I know their whole philosophy'. Most beginners quit before people have spent enough time with them. Alignment of values: when people know who you are, where you came from and what you stand for, they relate to your ideas as a whole. Authentic polarization: 'if you're liked by everybody, you're liked by nobody'. Give people reasons to heavily disagree and therefore heavily agree.
- Why personal branding is hard to saturate: people who hate one creator will love another saying the same idea through a different story and beliefs. It is resonance, not the idea, so it does not matter if someone already said everything you want to say.
- Given the same product from a random brand and from a creator you love, you buy from the creator because you trust them more.

### Story and beliefs: topics and reframes

- Use story and beliefs both as content topics and as ways of reframing ideas. Story as topic: 'how I failed at seven different business models'. Story as reframe: take a validated topic (how to build a personal brand) and start with your story. Belief as topic: the belief that digital products are the best way for beginners to start, with reasons. Belief as reframe: take classic advice (how Steve Jobs builds a product) and frame it through your lens.
- Exercise: write where you were, what sparked change, and where you are now. For each topic list the core principles you hold with conviction.
- Beliefs do not come at a blank screen. They sit in your unconscious. Think about it for about a week, consume content, and let it trigger ideas to write down. AI prompts can help extract story and beliefs into one document.
- Building a brand is telling chapters of one bigger story, with every post a step in the journey. Get clear on 7 things: who are you, where did you start, what do you believe, who are you doing it for, why you, where are you going, what is the payoff.
- Start from the end goal and work back to the beliefs people need. Examples: Wispr Flow (end goal a real-life Jarvis, so typing is slow, voice is natural, you will be faster with Wispr) and Tesla's 2006 Secret Master Plan (expensive sports car, cheaper car, affordable car, clean energy). For a personal brand, your own development is the story, or you have reached the goal and show others the road.

### Identity, character and being different

- People follow identity, not information. If anyone else could have said the same thing, you will not build a big brand. Identity is built from your stories, your contrarian takes and your personality. The gap between you on camera and you talking to a friend should be tiny. This is also why it is safe to give everything away (M10): people can copy your frameworks, not who you are.
- People follow characters, not creators ('stop creating content and start creating a character'). Disney and Pixar build characters. For one entrepreneur's brand, the team shines a light on what makes him human: family guy, action sports, peanut butter. If you only teach how and never show who you are, people will not connect.
- Different is better than better: trying to make better content than everyone loses to being different. 'We don't need more copies of the big names... we need more yous.' Focus on contribution, not competition. Mine your experience, not your competitors: ideas from your own stories, analogies and frameworks cannot be copied.
- Lean into what makes you you: barefoot, outside, coffee, drawing little diagrams. One creator started shooting in new places and claims the first one was a 10x outlier. If you do not reflect who you are, you build 'an audience of people that you hate'.
- On being yourself: 'Content isn't hard because you don't know how to do it. It's hard because people build it in a way that they can't sustain.' Portraying a character you are not is often from insecurity. 'If I'm not being myself, I'm not going to do it for more than 6 months.' Do not talk about things that make you want to quit, like asking a CEO of a \$600 million a year company to film 'best networking hacks'.
- Voice means choosing your perception (smart, funny, blunt) and making your voice match it. For personal brands, talk in your normal voice and avoid AI. Brand is intentional choices repeated over time. A laid-back person building a brand on flashy cars will not feel real. 'Viewers are skeptics.'

### The character diamond

- The character diamond has two versions with the same shape. Cult Brand version: top is North Star (what you want to be known for, your expertise, your main trait), bottom is kryptonite (something you are bad at, which makes you human), left is your real interests (the more you share, the more people get pulled in), right is your non-negotiables (the hills you will die on).
- Second version: top is Purpose (your mission, the movement you are on), bottom is Pain (the worst thing that ever happened to you, so people connect with who you are), left is Weakness (like Superman's kryptonite, strong brands keep sharing theirs), right is Worldview (your unique beliefs, which attract the right people and repel the wrong ones).

### Saturated niches, personality and story

- In saturated niches like wildlife photography, good photos are not enough because 'there's a million photographers'. People connect when they understand your personality, your why or the emotion behind the work.
- A case study, a former client who started from zero: 'my photos 10 years ago vs now' with him in the frame in crazy gear in a crazy setting. A story unfolds, the viewer is 'with him', and he shows emotion by smiling. Viewers then ask how to learn, which opens monetization. In contrast, another photographer's beautiful city photos had no emotional connection, so nobody cared.
- Profile test: people who find you through a reel land on your profile, and it must pass the 'this is exactly what I'm looking for' test. The other photographer's profile had a mismatch between the photo grid and his reels, which 'instantly takes away all the credibility'.
- Story as positioning: 'Most people are forgettable.' One writer hid his past (a \$50M a year eCommerce business with his brother, a battle with mental illness) to climb the corporate ladder. Another story: a migrant with a strong accent who faced racism gave a 7 minute Toastmasters speech that made his differences instantly forgotten. He became 'a titan of the tech world' with inbound opportunities, and his secret was basic storytelling.
- Mastering storytelling means you stop selling and start persuading, and opportunities come to you. In an AI world storytelling is 'a survival skill'. A well known money writer is quoted: a good storyteller with a decent idea will always have more influence than someone with a great idea who hopes the facts will speak for themselves.

### Frameworks

**Three drivers of authenticity**

1. Time under attention: more time with you means your posts are read through your whole philosophy
2. Alignment of values: people know who you are and what you stand for
3. Authentic polarization: reasons to heavily disagree and therefore heavily agree

**Story and belief as topic and reframe**

1. Story as topic: a story you tell directly
2. Story as reframe: start a validated topic with your story
3. Belief as topic: a belief with your reasons
4. Belief as reframe: classic advice through your lens

**The character diamond**

1. Top: North Star or Purpose
2. Bottom: kryptonite or Pain
3. Left: real interests or Weakness
4. Right: non-negotiables or Worldview

  <br>*Why it works:* Same idea, different story and time under attention.
- **Two photographers:** One photographer showed his photos from 10 years ago next to now, with himself in crazy gear and a visible smile. Another's beautiful photos of a city had no person or emotion behind them.
  <br>*Why it works:* In a crowded niche people connect to the person behind the work.

- Acting like a character you are not, which you will not sustain past 6 months
- Hiding your story and your edge because it feels embarrassing
- Teaching only how, and never showing who you are
- Trying to be liked by everybody, which means liked by nobody
- Having reels and a profile that do not match

## 1.11 Worldbuilding: a world your audience lives in

*11 min read*

> **The big idea:** A strong personal brand is a world, not just a feed. There are 7 elements that make an audience feel like citizens, and 6 components of a world. The world around a brand also drives more than the account itself.

### Why build a world

- Fiction franchises like Lord of the Rings, Star Wars, Dune and Marvel build worlds. Tolkien's has characters (Frodo, Sam, Aragorn), settings (the Shire, Mordor) and magic (wizards, the one ring). When you re-enter the world you come with context. 'We're not just watching these movies. We are living in a world.'
- In educational brands, worldbuilding means your own language and concepts, shared definitions, recurring characters and settings, and openness about weaknesses. Done well, the audience thinks in your frameworks, uses your terms in their own content, and feels like citizens of your world rather than casual viewers. That is loyalty not just to your content but to how you see the world. The claim: in 17 years of building personal brands, almost nobody talks about it.
- Why most strategists skip it: they sprint to content strategy without knowing what the brand stands for, what makes it different, why the person is worth listening to, or what world they invite people into.
- Useful information alone leaves a lot on the table. Shared language, shared ways of viewing the world and named concepts people can take with them create loyalty.

### The 7 elements

- 1\. Name your concepts. Take something you already teach and give it a name, not something made up on the spot. Examples: the Brand Journey Framework, the Credibility Bank, the 70/20/10 framework. Pass the three Rs: easy to remember, repeat and reference. A name is easier to use in conversation and in other people's content, so they spread your message for you. 'Don't just be lazy about it and just declare or walk through the framework.'
- 2\. Redefine terms. Every niche has vague terms nobody really understands. Give a clear, useful, operational definition (one that gives actions) and you create a lens tied back to your brand. Example: branding is a pairing of things. The claim: a massive competitive moat that puts you in a different category from peers.
- 3\. Share weaknesses. People do not trust someone posturing that everything is perfect. Three types: a flaw (something you can change, like anxiety on camera), a limitation (something you cannot change but can adapt to, like ADHD harnessed as a strength), and a cost (what your choices make you give up, the favourite of the three, like an Olympic athlete with no time for friends and family). It must be real, 'not when you fake cry on camera'. One way to show it is filming without a hat, which scares the creator who does it.
- 4\. Characters and settings. Gary Vee's DailyVee had a recurring cast (D-Rock, Babin, Mike Boyd, his brother AJ and others). Audiences attach to side characters like in The Office. One creator keeps referring to their content director, and people DM about him and joke in comments about him holding a camera 11 to 12 hours through London. A world with only you is flat. Add characters and settings on purpose.
- 5\. Interest stacking: hobbies, interests and values outside your expertise. An acquaintance shares one thing with you, a friend several, a best friend many. Same with an audience. One creator's: Harley-Davidsons, dancing at hardcore and metal shows, the Seahawks, trash TV. You must actually share these things or nobody knows what you have in common. Each gives the audience 'more at bats' to connect.
- 6\. Lore and legends. Stories of your successes and failures with the lessons. Example: a client who chased views, went wider, got more views and subscribers but fewer sales. It teaches twice: the lesson, and your experience. Scars often become your most heavily convicted points. Highs and lows, not just the highlight reel. Store them in your Credibility Bank.
- 7\. Common enemy, built from your contrarian take. Humans tend to bond more over shared hatred than love (no moral claim is made). It falls apart when the enemy is a specific person or company. For '99.9%' avoid that, because it makes you a call-out creator, not a contrarian creator. Instead frame your take as the thing standing between the audience and their outcome. Example: optimizing for virality as the only metric. People who share the frustration self-select in. A contrarian take is your belief. A common enemy is something you and the audience rally around, turning a belief into a movement.

### The 6 components of a world

- Worldbuilding is a personal brand category that has been thought about for about 5 years. Surfaces: where you play. Digital (social, YouTube, X, email, product pages, landing pages, even Spotify playlists, 'any pixel that you communicate through') and physical. Consistency of beliefs and messaging across surfaces matters, not identical formatting. You do not need every surface on day one.
- Objects: items or products you show up with, like Casey Neistat's glasses, Peter McKinnon's coffee or Steve Jobs's black turtleneck. Not every brand needs one, but objects stick more than amorphous ideas.
- Characters: other people in the content, especially in a multiplayer world: MrBeast has the boys, Colin has Samir and Samir has Colin. Aligned interest across people broadcasts the same association.
- Citizens: audience members who become part of your world, follow, then become advocates and amplifiers. The more similar and densely packed your citizen profile, the faster the audience compounds.
- Aesthetics: design language, colors, surfaces, textures. It makes taste visible, and we live in a visual world.
- Interoperability: the rails that carry someone from one surface to the next (short form video, YouTube, email, community). Without it surfaces are 'a pile of random islands'. With it, attention flows through one world.

### The world and shared language

- Creative direction for a person: a recurring set, fixed fonts and colors, named series with their own title look, styling on purpose, a collab every month, and launches run like campaigns (tease, reveal, launch, sustain). A visual signature tip: own one color or pattern (Nerf is orange).
- A content meeting, twice a month: news in the niche (do we like it? an opinion or a tactic?), then your own history (camera roll from last week, then 1, 3 and 5 years ago), then outliers in your topic. Debate each one out loud with someone ('would this make a good video? what's the hook?'), record it, and sort ideas into priority, middle and backburner.
- On shared language: your audience examines your words 'through a microscope'. Coin your own phrases and repeat them for years. One business YouTuber owns 'exhaust the body, tame the mind', posted for 3 or more years, and people quote it and tag him. Another creator uses 'moonshot' again and again. The claim: they went from about 0 to 200K on Instagram and 0 to 100K on YouTube in about 6 months.
- A language test: what would someone say if they recommended you to a friend? Most positioning happens when you are not in the room. Build a small vocabulary and use it everywhere (content, homepage, sales deck, podcasts, launches). 'You will get bored of your own phrases much faster than the market will.' Show someone five pieces of your content and ask what you talk about, and that answer measures positioning better than your bio.
- Movement marketing: share your mission and message in your content. 'People follow momentum. People follow people that are moving.' One business YouTuber's mission is helping entrepreneurs build a business they do not grow to hate, and people repeat it for him.

### Frameworks

**The 7 elements of worldbuilding**

1. Name your concepts (pass the 3 Rs: remember, repeat, reference)
2. Redefine terms with operational definitions
3. Share weaknesses: a flaw, a limitation or a cost
4. Characters and settings
5. Interest stacking
6. Lore and legends
7. Common enemy built from your contrarian take

**The 6 components of a world**

1. Surfaces: where you play
2. Objects: items you show up with
3. Characters: other people in the world
4. Citizens: audience who become advocates
5. Aesthetics: taste made visible
6. Interoperability: rails between surfaces

### Examples

- **A content director as a recurring character:** One creator keeps referring to their content director. Fans now DM about him and joke about him holding a camera for 11 to 12 hours through London.
  <br>*Why it works:* A side character adds familiarity and almost a nostalgic feeling.
- **An owned phrase:** One business YouTuber owns the phrase 'exhaust the body, tame the mind', posted for 3 or more years. People quote it and tag him.
  <br>*Why it works:* Own language needs years of repetition.

### Common mistakes

- Naming a concept you made up on the spot instead of something you already teach
- Faking weakness, or making the enemy a specific person or company
- Showing up on many surfaces with no rails between them
- Getting bored of your own phrases before the market does
- A world with only you in it

## 1.12 Trust: how it is built and how to protect it

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Trust is believing you will meet or exceed expectations based on what you did before. People also trust conclusions they reach themselves. Together these explain how a brand earns trust on purpose, and why optimizing for virality can hurt it.

### Trust, operationalized

- A definition: 'trust is a measurement in which somebody believes that you will meet or exceed expectations based on what you have done in the past.'
- So you build trust by setting an expectation and continuing to meet it. In educational content: set the expectation of a painful problem the audience cannot overcome alone, then give 'radically clear steps', as obvious as possible, from problem to solution. Do it on repeat.
- Costco sample analogy: if I like the sample I am more likely to buy the full pack. When you later mention an offer, people are more likely to believe you will deliver because you met past expectations.
- The steps: identify the painful problems of the ideal customer (the customer you want to serve, not necessarily your current audience), identify your unique solutions, and across all your content keep setting an expectation of one of those problems and walking through the clear solution. Done consistently, 'your audience would have to fight to not believe' you will meet expectations.
- The chain: obsession leads to skills, results, proof, trust and then a scaled association. Trust comes through a lot of content.

### Optimize for trust, not virality

- One creator says they are 'not the antiviral guy'. Going viral can help. The problem is optimizing content for virality, because it makes you pick topics that do not lead to the business outcomes you want.
- Example: someone teaching business principles posts a random video about their fast food order. It goes viral, and the audience it brings by and large does not care about the main thing that drives the business. Attention around something does not mean the audience believes you can help them.
- Ella Langley example: she has all the attention in country music, but if she launched teaching young musicians to break through, you would not believe she could teach it, because she never showed the ability to teach.
- From one creator's lore: a person they worked with chased views and made much wider content to go viral on every piece. Views and subscribers went up while sales went down.
- On reputation: reputation is what people say when you are not there, not your bio. If a video would get views but hurt your brand, do not post it. Lots of views from people who then had a bad experience with you is the worst outcome. Post videos that get views, never videos made only for views. Views should become followers, then buyers. Do not build a rage-bait brand.
- Do not associate with negativity. A bad brand 'will close doors behind your back' you did not know were open. Online, stay out of drama ('negative energy attracts other negative energy'), stay in your lane and keep serving the audience.

### The power of suggestion: build trust without claiming it

- Certain words implant thoughts, feelings and beliefs in viewers' heads without them noticing. A persuasion bank shot: direct influence hits the viewer's guard, suggestion goes around it.
- Direct version (bad): 'I'm the best in the content space. You need my help. Your system is broken. Here's mine, you should buy it.' It sounds brash and salesy, so people dismiss it.
- Suggestion version: 'These are the five best content strategies I used to grow these businesses to over 20 million a year last year.' After the explanation, viewers may conclude on their own that you are one of the best. When people reach conclusions on their own, they trust you much faster. 'This is where obsessive fandom actually comes from.' The proxy is the movie Inception.
- The five universal inputs for every piece: 1) relevant and relatable to the ideal viewer (narrow it, the more specific it is the more it feels like reading their mind). 2) Small effort, big reward (viewers check out at complexity, simplicity gets action). 3) Non-obvious frame ('Non-obvious ideas create obsessive audiences. Tattoo that to your head.' To get them, spend time outside your niche and collide two things). 4) Validated with proof ('Without proof, you're just another person with an opinion, but with proof, you're a source of truth.' Get the proof first, then make the content). 5) Tactically implementable steps.

### The three levels of obsession

- Level 1, the signal layer (easiest, the claim is that anyone can get some viewers within 30 days): produce content that is higher signal than everyone else in your category. Signal-to-noise ratio is how often a viewer leaves thinking it was worth their time. 100 pieces with 30 valuable is 30%. Three factors: relevant to the viewer, non-obvious, and validated. Example: a 'dopamine ladders' video was on target (storytelling), non-obvious (a framework invented for it) and validated (examples and metrics for each step).
- Level 2, the belief layer: change what the viewer believes, not by telling them they are wrong but by introducing a way of thinking they cannot unsee. Three factors: a contrasting frame (then vs now, A vs B), a relatable character (someone who looks like the viewer, started where they started), and small cost, large reward. Example: '0 to 100k on YouTube in 5 months'.
- Level 3, the action layer (strongest): the viewer takes action and gets a real result. The trap is telling them what to do, which feels like homework. Instead show proof so strong that if they just replicate it they might get the same result. Two factors: quantitative proof (for example how this strategy took someone from 2 to 15,000 followers in 6 weeks) and tactically implementable steps broken to 'the atomic unit'.
- The claim: level 3 viewers 'will most likely watch and buy literally anything you offer'. The line: 'The proof suggests action, the action creates results, and the results drives obsession.' The roadmap is given on purpose, and the evidence is comments like 'I grew from 2 to 20k subscribers just by watching your videos' (the creator's own claim).

### Frameworks

**Trust, step by step**

1. Identify the painful problems of your ideal customer
2. Identify your unique solutions
3. Set an expectation of one problem in each piece
4. Meet it with radically clear steps, on repeat

**Three levels of obsession**

1. Level 1 signal: relevant, non-obvious, validated
2. Level 2 belief: contrasting frame, relatable character, small cost with large reward
3. Level 3 action: quantitative proof and atomic steps

**Five universal inputs**

1. Relevant and relatable to the ideal viewer
2. Small effort, big reward
3. Non-obvious frame
4. Validated with proof
5. Tactically implementable steps

### Examples

- **The fast food order video:** A business teacher posts a random video about a fast food order. It goes viral, but the audience it brings does not care about the main thing that drives the business.
  <br>*Why it works:* Attention is not the same as belief you can help.
- **Direct vs suggestion:** Direct: 'I'm the best, your system is broken, buy mine.' Suggestion: 'These are the five best content strategies I used to grow these businesses to over 20 million a year last year.'
  <br>*Why it works:* People trust conclusions they reach themselves.

### Common mistakes

- Optimizing for views on topics that do not lead to your outcome
- Telling viewers what to do instead of showing proof
- Sharing frameworks with no data
- Making things complex because complex feels valuable
- Posting for views that hurt your brand

### Where the sources disagree

Is virality good for a brand? One view: optimize for trust, not virality, because viral topics can bring people who do not care about what you sell. Another comes from a view maximizer who takes channels from 1M to 100M monthly views, while saying creators who sell to a small group should aim for fewer, more targeted views. A third says the sweet spot for most personal brands is 3 to 10K views a post because viral does not equal money.

## 1.13 Obsession, stickiness and fandom

*11 min read*

> **The big idea:** Association comes from three things: obsession (time spent), positioning (stickiness) and worldbuilding (spread). The goal is a fan whose brain releases dopamine at your name, called the Pavlovian response.

### Obsession: the oxygen of personal branding

- 'Obsession really is the oxygen of personal branding.' Brands at peak association are genuinely obsessed with their domain: Dana White (MMA), Steve Jobs (product design), Warren Buffett (investing), Kylie Jenner (cosmetics). If there is one controllable dial, it is obsession.
- Practically, obsession is time spent doing the thing: how much of your waking hours you spend doing, talking about, learning and teaching it. The biggest reason most brands lack a solid foundation is that they have not spent enough time doing the thing.
- The chain: obsession leads to skills, results, proof, trust and a scaled association.
- Fake obsession is talking about a thing you do not actually do. 'People can sniff that out' and the brand will not scale.
- The obsession gene: some people get extremely obsessed with any domain they focus on (some creators say they have it, Joe Rogan too). Rogan spans comedy, MMA, podcasting, archery and pool only because he is hyper obsessive about each. He is the exception. If you are not naturally obsessed and cannot force it, it will be very hard to spend enough time to build a brand.
- A test for a niche: one you are already obsessed with or willing to become obsessed with 'such that you would spend 40 hours per week for the next decade on it'.
- One creator's own story: 18 months before the video they set out to build one of the strongest brands in content strategy, studied the players, decided to build a stronger association and were fully obsessed. Their numbers at the time (their own claims): about 18 to 24 months into the content strategy brand, 3 to 4 years into content overall, 91 YouTube videos, 1.2 million hours watched, 72 million watch minutes on long form, and 'probably another billion minutes or two' on short form (an estimate).

### The personal brand formula

- Personal brand value = viewers x content minutes x stickiness.
- Viewers: the number of people exposed to you with respect to the association. The best wide format is short form video, made by you or clips of you by others.
- Content minutes: minutes someone spends seeing or hearing you tied to the association. In person does not scale. Content scales for the same effort. Increase by more minutes (volume) and more minutes on target (narrower topics).
- Stickiness: a multiplier for which minutes build trust and which are junk food. It is the most important variable most people ignore.
- The law: length of a piece of content correlates with how sticky its minutes are. 1 minute of short form is less sticky than 1 minute of long form, which is less sticky than 1 minute of a live stream, because stickiness compounds when someone watches many minutes in a row.
- Five stickiness drivers: longer formats (depth), multiple surfaces (repetition), more contrarian beliefs, higher signal and clearer messaging.
- Mapping: viewers = worldbuilding, minutes = obsession, stickiness = positioning. Association is the output: association = worldbuilding x obsession x positioning. 'Obsession generates the minutes, positioning makes those minutes stick, world building spreads those minutes out.'
- A recipe: 1 short-form platform for reach (7 posts a week), 1 long-form platform for depth (1 to 2 a week), 1 nurture channel (email or community). It will take 'hundreds and hundreds of posts'.

### The goal: the Pavlovian response and cult fans

- The ultimate goal: dopamine releases in a viewer's brain just from seeing your name or face, before they watch anything. Named after Pavlov: the bell rang, the dog got food, and eventually the bell alone made the dog's mouth water. It is 'king status in attention land', and the claim is that if you can do it on command you can build any business as big as you want.
- You get there by taking viewers up all six rungs of the dopamine ladder (full ladder in M4). Rungs 1 to 4 are about the message. Rungs 5 and 6 are about the messenger, and this is where cult fandom is built.
- Rung 5, affection: the viewer starts liking and trusting the person delivering the story and gives you 'a longer leash'. Four ways to raise likability: attractiveness (subconscious), overall vibe (how you look, dress, sit), smiling (joy transfers through the lens), and actually solving a problem for them. The last is the strongest and works regardless of looks. Do not force likability. Be authentically passionate.
- Rung 6, revelation: the viewer realizes you will be a continued source of value over time. Education reaches rung 6 far more easily than entertainment, because entertainment competes with Netflix, the NFL, Kai Cenat and MrBeast. If viewers deeply struggle with your problem area it may take a couple of pieces to go from rung 1 to 6. If your topic is a nice-to-have it takes many more.
- Faceless content can reach rungs 1 to 4 but struggles to reach the Pavlovian peak, because there is no personality to attach taste, trust and liking to.
- Cult fans act differently from casual fans in three ways. Some vs all (depth): casuals let posts come to them, cult fans go looking and consume everything. Watch vs share (reach): casuals watch and leave, cult fans share you. Think vs buy (money): casuals see your offer and think, cult fans buy.
- Category fandom, not peak fandom: being number 1 in a narrow category beats being number 5 overall.
- Four content levers with weights: direction 30% (useful, non-obvious, on-target topics), comprehension 40% (shorter words, simpler sentences, bite-sized chunks), effectiveness 20% (does the tip work when they try it) and likability 10%.
- The watering hole analogy: an elephant goes back to the hole that had water 9 out of 10 times, not the one that had it 1 in 3. Every useful post is water.

### Signature patterns, education vs entertainment and hook formats

- One creator starts videos with 'Check this out really quick' (on about the last 30 videos), a signature pattern people recognize by ear. Unique visuals, color palette, motion and aesthetic help cut through, because copied patterns desensitize the feed (see M6).
- Two types of video, both solving a pain point: entertainment solves boredom, education solves a specific problem. Education reaches trust, likability and the Pavlovian response faster. Entertainment rarely needs proof but struggles past rung 5.
- Hook formats as positioning: Teacher format builds authority to then sell expertise. Investigator format is for people who want to be at the leading edge, showing they find what most people do not know. Contrarian format suits the smarty pants expert or someone framing themselves as a contrarian in a crowded space. Fortune teller suits anyone building authority on the future of a topic.
- Topic clarity is also self-selection. Do not trick non-target viewers into watching: if you do not care about investing, an investing video wants you gone fast.

### Frameworks

**The personal brand formula**

1. Personal brand value = viewers x content minutes x stickiness
2. Viewers come from worldbuilding, minutes from obsession, stickiness from positioning
3. Association is the output of all three

**Cult fan vs casual fan**

1. Some vs all: depth of consumption
2. Watch vs share: reach
3. Think vs buy: money

**The four content levers**

1. Direction 30%: useful, non-obvious, on-target topics
2. Comprehension 40%: simple words, short sentences
3. Effectiveness 20%: the tip works
4. Likability 10%

### Examples

- **Joe Rogan and the obsession gene:** Rogan spans comedy, MMA, podcasting, archery and pool only because he is hyper obsessive about each and his show has enough content minutes to build each association.
  <br>*Why it works:* He is the exception. Most people should own one category.
- **The watering hole:** An elephant goes back to the hole that had water 9 out of 10 times, not the one that had it 1 in 3.
  <br>*Why it works:* Consistent usefulness makes people return.

### Common mistakes

- Talking about something you do not actually do, which people sniff out
- Ignoring stickiness and counting only views
- Forcing likability instead of being authentically passionate
- Spreading effort over many topics so no minutes stack up

### Try it

Apply the niche test: could you spend 40 hours a week on this topic for the next 10 years? If not, write what part of it you would still be obsessed with.

## 1.14 A brand that lasts in the AI era

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** AI leveled the building floor but not the trust floor. Taste, distribution and high agency are the moats, and a brand needs a one-of-one position to survive.

### The three moats

- The claim: about 90% of people using AI do the same thing: prompt, accept, ship, with no judgment and no taste. Developing even one of three moats makes you stand out. Taste is knowing what good looks like. Distribution is getting it in front of people who care. High agency is the willingness to figure things out when nobody shows you how.
- Why AI cannot replace them: judgment only comes from experience, trust only comes from consistency, and drive does not quit when the path gets unclear.
- The window (a claim): in about 12 months taste will not be rare, it will be expected, and distribution will be harder because everyone will try. Right now these moats put you ahead of 95% of people using the same tools.
- AI does not level the playing field, it tilts it. AI mirrors how much the user understands. 'The variable is always the human.' AI is a multiplier, not an equalizer: curiosity plus AI is 10x leverage, passivity plus AI is nothing ('zero times ten is still zero'). A speed multiplier on bad judgment gets you to the wrong place faster.
- High agency in practice: hit a wall and find a way around it, combine tools nobody told you to combine, open the docs and try four approaches before asking for help. Ask 'what if I tried this?' not 'how do I do this?'. The Replit CEO is quoted: 'you dont need any development experience. you need grit. you need to be a fast learner.' A Coinbase CEO is also reported saying best hires were often unqualified on paper but high agency.
- Patience is a strategic asset: 'when the market is flooded with fast and shallow, slow and deep becomes the competitive advantage.' High agency is the hardest to fake and the foundation of the other two. High agency people study the best builders on X, Reddit and communities, learn frameworks instead of copy-pasting prompts, and ask AI to critique their work.

### Taste: how it shows up

- Taste shows up in specific decisions at three levels. Small: you notice your AI site looks like every other shadcn template, so you spend 20 minutes on icons, a palette from real design research and a font that fits. Editorial: rewrite the first two sentences of an AI draft because it opened with something generic. 'The default hook is almost always wrong.' Obsessive: study converting websites, such as whitespace, CTA placement, onboarding flow and error messages.
- Taste is knowing what to reject, not only what you want: having an opinion about the defaults and overriding them.
- Taste cannot be shortcut. It comes from exposure to thousands of examples until you build an internal model of what works, and from reading enough great writing to feel when a sentence carries its weight.
- One builder's story: a year of vibe coding, built more in 3 months than in the previous 2 years, then looked back honestly. Half was mediocre, 'functional and technically correct' but forgettable. The trap: 'confusing output with quality.' They stopped building for a few weeks and spent hundreds of hours studying design, typography, spacing, hierarchy and storytelling.
- beechinour: 'You can't prompt your way into having taste.' Study people at the frontier where great taste meets new technology, save work that makes you stop scrolling and work out why, test one decision in your own work, and build a reference library. 'Good enough is the enemy of everything in the AI age, because good enough is now free and infinite.' 'Functional is free now. remarkable still costs something.'

### Trust as the premium asset

- AI leveled the building floor but not the trust floor. Making things approaches zero cost. Being trusted is as hard as ever, maybe harder, because AI content made people more skeptical. 'When everything could be AI, trust in the humans behind the work becomes the premium asset.'
- Data cited (needs checking before use): 'AI slop' named word of the year 2025 with mentions up 9x (461,000 to 2.4 million). Consumer trust drops about 50% when people can tell content was AI-made. An NYU and Emory study: AI ads beat human ones by 19% on clickthrough, but purchase likelihood dropped 33% once consumers learned they were AI-made.
- Trust comes from consistency, a recognizable voice and accumulated evidence of showing your work for months or years. AI compresses creation to minutes, trust still takes months or years: 'you cannot vibe-code trust into existence.'
- Followers are vanity. People who reply, share without being asked and come back daily are the moat. A passive audience is a commodity, an active community is a moat.
- People prefer to hear from a personal page than a brand page unless the product is groundbreaking, and early stage companies are dominated by founder-led content. Courses, communities and creator products sell through X and YouTube long-form first 'because people buy from someone they trust'.
- Traits are the moat. Work ethic, coachability, grit and the ability to adapt decide who wins as AI improves. 'Every role is becoming an ownership role.' Companies and employees should both be on X every day, because it is 'the best networking platform in the world'.

### 5 things a brand needs in the AI era

- One-of-one positioning: AI copies every post within hours, so 'informational moats are trending to zero'. Own a lane through at least 1 of 5: premium packaging, originality, tactical usefulness, rare skill combination, aura. The best brands stack 2 or 3. 'The algorithm doesn't punish you for being bad, it punishes you for being average.'
- Data for the topic, you for the take: pick topics the market already proved it wants (study top accounts' best posts), then add your own take ('what do I believe about this that everyone else gets wrong?').
- Violent volume on one platform until you own it. One creator stayed only on YouTube for 18 months before touching Instagram or TikTok. Over 5 platforms you give a fifth of your effort to each.
- The quality bar: 'when supply is limited, speed wins. When supply is infinite, quality wins.' The test: was this the best I could do with my time and skill today? If it was the only post people ever saw from you, would you be proud?
- Obsession: AI can copy your style but cannot copy real interest. Gut check: are you excited to make the next 100 posts on this topic?
- A ratio: use AI for about 80% (research, drafts, structure) and put your 20% into taste (the hook, what stays, what gets cut). Most people do the opposite.

### Frameworks

**The three moats**

1. Taste: knowing what good looks like and what to reject
2. Distribution: getting it in front of people who care, trust at scale
3. High agency: figuring things out with nobody showing you how

**Five things a brand needs in the AI era**

1. One-of-one positioning
2. Data for the topic, you for the take
3. Violent volume on one platform
4. A high quality bar
5. Real obsession

### Examples

- **The vibe coding year:** One builder made more in 3 months than in the previous 2 years, then admitted half was functional but forgettable because it used the same prompts and defaults as everyone. They paused building to study design and storytelling for hundreds of hours.
  <br>*Why it works:* Output is not quality. Taste fills the gap.
- **NYU and Emory study:** AI ads beat human ones by 19% on clickthrough, but purchase likelihood dropped 33% once people learned they were AI-made.
  <br>*Why it works:* A cited stat that needs checking before reuse. It shows why trust in the human matters.

### Common mistakes

- Prompt, accept, ship with no judgment
- Confusing output with quality
- Spreading across 5 platforms and giving a fifth of your effort to each
- Being average, because the algorithm punishes average
- Treating followers as the moat instead of active community

## 1.15 Plans, timelines and staying power

*11 min read*

> **The big idea:** Concrete plans exist for the first 90 days and the first years. Many creators and investors say the same hard thing: it takes longer than you think, and the people who win keep going.

### The 90-day plan

- Days 1 to 3, positioning, three questions: which game (mass fame or category fame), who exactly (the authority statement), and the 4 brand pillars (niche bullseye, contrarian vault, associations and objects, points of uniqueness). See lessons 5, 7 and 8.
- Days 4 to 90, content. Content formula = topic + format + visual layout. Aim for 3 to 4 topics x 1 to 3 formats x 1 to 3 layouts = 5 to 10 formulas.
- Days 1 to 60: about 50 posts (3 the first week, 5 the second, then 7 a week), tested 'widely and wildly'. The only filter is whether it is valuable to the ideal viewer.
- Then keep a formula only if it passes 4 checks: it performs, it brings the RIGHT people (read the comments and the bios of new followers), you enjoy making it, and you are proud of how it looks. Double down, cut, repeat. Formulas burn out, so go back to testing.

### A personal brand playbook

- There is no competition: out of 100 people who say they will start a personal brand in your niche, 80 never start, 10 quit early, 5 chase a shiny object and 2 get unlucky. 3 are left (illustrative numbers, not a study).
- View stages: stuck at 0 to 500, ask 'why would anyone care?' (entertainment or value? does it look good? does it stop them?). To 1K and up, work on structure (idea, hook, execution). At 10 to 50K, find your outliers and repeat them, but a signature series at most about 3 times a month or it dies. At 100K every post, build systems, team and process.
- 4 pillars, checked monthly: look at saves, views and shares, then drop, replace or fix a pillar. Keep testing new things even when old ones work, 'it's not going to work forever'.
- Monetize: 'why they watch is why they buy'. Contact in bio, then email list and simple landing pages, then services, low-ticket teaching, done-for-you high ticket.
- Formats that work: free work (design merch for billion-dollar brands unasked, they reply), niche-niche playbook ('3 chest exercises for men over 50'), would and wouldn't ('10 things I'd buy at Trader Joe's as a food scientist') and curation (save people time).
- Timeline: 30 days = 'I can do this', 100 days = it is a habit, 365 days = expect the first money, year 2 = now you know enough to make a plan, year 3 = stick to it, 5 years = 'you can do anything'.
- Identity marketing: in a lonelier world with fewer third places, brands became the way people find their people. Five identity reasons: belonging (sneakers), being better than others quietly (The Row), rebel (Liquid Death), stand out (the flare piece) and pride in a subculture ('if you know, you know'). Plus a private one: fix a weak spot in someone's identity. Four questions for any audience: who are they, who do they think they are, who do they want to be, who do they want to hear from?
- A private reason: fixing a weak spot in someone's identity. At Lift Foils, older guys who thought they were done with extreme sports could feel young again. An ad test split about 50/50 between buyers who wanted to see someone like themselves and buyers who wanted to see the younger person they wish they were.

### Expect it to take time

- A common warning: 'Do not expect any meaningful outcome for the first 36 months.' Plenty pop at 3, 6, 9 or 12 months, but not the majority. Better to expect nothing for 3 years and be shocked at month 9 than expect a pop at 3 months and be in year 2 with little traction. It is not about lowering ambition. Personal trainer analogy: wanting to lose 60 pounds in 2 months is unrealistic, and a trainer resets you to 4 to 6 pounds. Most people burn out from wrong expectations, not content strategy or positioning.
- People start with insane expectations, especially in the first 6 months, comparing themselves to top creators while not taking the actions those results need. Many ask a brand strategist to build what Gary Vee has with Team GaryVee, and the strategist ignores most because they will not make the investment of money, time, attention and privacy.
- It takes hundreds and hundreds of posts. There is no version where 3 weeks of posting dominates a category. The formula works in every niche if you have the patience, discipline and obsession to run it as long as it takes.
- You will not see insane results in under a year. Even if you do, do not expect to.
- One investor's golden rule: create value for people and you receive value back, in the form of their attention. He did not set out for follower growth but for creating value. His edge is consistency: 'I'm never gonna quit.' 'It's really hard to keep someone down if they keep showing up.' (He says he was the worst player and 'dumbest one' in his Stanford class.)
- The same investor on luck: much of what looks like luck is 'the macro result of thousands of micro actions' (engineered serendipity). Story: he went to the gym at 5:00 every day, and one of the regulars was Tim Cook, then new Apple CEO. Six months of daily chats became one of his most important mentor relationships, with no end goal.
- The investor changed his mind on hard work, from absolute (100 hour weeks, 4 hours sleep) to taking care of your full self. His definition of high performance: accelerating in output and quality, better than a month ago and much better than two months ago. Stay even keel, from baseball: never too low (you are one pitch from getting out of any situation), never too high (one pitch from getting hit).
- Longevity is the key. Most great investments come over the long term, so stay in the game, which is why a team matters.

### 7 brand principles and the content CEO

- Consistency (Casey Neistat vlogged 800 days straight). The math: 100 reps each 10% better is about 13,800x, which does check (1.1 to the power of 100). Pain builds resilience (Jensen Huang: Nvidia nearly died in 1995). Obsession with the craft (MrBeast had about 20 subs in 2012). Make what you love, the audience comes last (Rick Rubin). Delusional self-belief (Jordan cut from his high school team). Mastery takes decades (Jiro still 'hasn't perfected rice'). Insanely simple (Jobs cutting the iPhone to zero buttons). These are mostly mindset.
- One specific growth story: hand-drawn concept images for founders on LinkedIn, +110K followers in a month (the creator's claim).
- 'Every business these days is a media company.' Content is essential and cannot be delegated straight out of the gate, because the founder has to understand it first.
- On the north star: content was a personal development tool for one creator, pushing them past social anxiety. Focus on improving video by video, because it stacks up. The claimed member outcomes are in lesson 1.

## Cheat sheet: Personal brand and positioning

The whole module on one page.

- Decide what you want to be known for in 20 years before you make content.
- Start from the end: outcome, reputation, actions, skills (the Brand Journey).
- Do the thing first, then talk about it. Do not larp.
- Be a student or an expert, whichever is true. Never fake the expert.
- Bank your receipts: successes, failures and lessons.
- A brand is a repeated pairing. Wear the Nikes every game.
- List what you do NOT want to be linked with.
- Start in a narrow pond, widen only as proof grows.
- Own one category before five.
- Check your market size before blaming the niche.
- Make each idea work for core, casual and new viewers.
- Say what you stand for in one sentence.
- Keep 3 to 5 contrarian takes and repeat them.
- Be contrarian, not controversial. Never make the enemy a person.
- Optimize for trust, not virality, if you sell something.
- Name your concepts and redefine vague terms.
- Share real weaknesses, never faked ones.
- Build a world: surfaces, characters, language, rails between them.
- Show proof and let people conclude it themselves.
- Obsession is time spent doing the thing. Fake obsession gets sniffed out.
- Taste and trust are the AI-era moats.
- Expect about 36 months. Staying power wins.
