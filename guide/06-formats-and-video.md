# Module 6: Formats and video

*Pick a few formats you can keep making, write the hook around what people see first, keep production simple, and post at a pace you can hold.*

This module covers which content formats to choose, how short and long videos differ, and how to build hooks, structure and pacing for video. It also covers filming and editing, how often to post, reusing one idea on many platforms, and using AI in production. Creators disagree on some things, like volume versus quality and whether cutting one long piece into many clips still works, and each lesson shows both sides. The common thread: use a few repeatable formats, put the value early, and keep making them.

## 6.1 Choosing your formats

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A format is a repeatable shape for your content, like "a talking-head video giving one tip" or "a list post with numbered points". Pick a few formats that fit your platform, that you can keep making, and that you enjoy. Run them for a long time, and drop the ones that keep failing.

### Be native to the platform

- Every platform has its own language. Content that fits the platform gets pushed more, because the platform wants more of it. On X that might be short posts and articles, on LinkedIn it is longer personal posts.
- One approach: find the platform where the people who would buy from you spend the most time. Then use the format that platform pushes hardest. That format is called the platform's "hero format".
- Roughly: Instagram, TikTok, Facebook and Snapchat favour short videos. YouTube favours videos of 10 to 30 minutes. X and Threads favour short text. LinkedIn favours short text, and one creator calls its new short video "the biggest untapped opportunity right now" (that is an opinion, not a fact).
- X is a good pick if your audience is developers, crypto, startups or tech buyers.
- Another approach: X pushes many formats at once (threads, articles, images and video), so test all of them and see which ones your audience likes.
- If you post videos as a founder on X, keep them sincere and self-made. Skip the special effects.

### The 4 boxes: two mediums, two lengths

- You can post in two mediums (video or writing) and two lengths (short or long). That gives 4 boxes, and each does a different job. Long video builds deep trust. Short video gets raw reach, meaning lots of new people see you. Long writing (articles, newsletters) shows depth and builds authority. Short writing (posts) is a cheap place to test ideas fast, and the ones that win can become articles or long videos.
- A healthy mix covers all 4 boxes, and also every stage of someone getting to know you (first seeing you, starting to trust you, buying).
- You can fill all 4 boxes on one platform. One creator posts only on X, yet publishes articles, long videos, short videos and daily posts there.
- What works well right now: short videos for reach, carousels (swipeable image posts) for saves and teaching, founder-led content for trust, build in public (sharing your work as it happens so people follow along), long videos for deep trust, newsletters so you own your audience, and content from employees if your company has 20 or more people.
- "Writing" on social media is wider than it sounds. It includes captions, images with text on them, tweets, LinkedIn posts, and the scripts behind reels and YouTube videos. So if you don't want to show your face, you can still grow with writing, images and designs.

### A few formats, run for a long time

- Choose at most about 3 formats and rotate them for as long as they work. The rule is to change the input, not the structure. Keep the shape the same and swap in a new topic each time. People then know what to expect, and they can tell a friend what you do.
- One founder-creator runs three formats he loves. The first is deep breakdowns of systems. The second is whiteboard sessions, where he helps another founder live on camera. The third is behind the scenes. His audience's favourite is watching him help other founders.
- Another approach: stay on one platform and one format until your system has run for 30 days in a row without breaking. Spreading out too early is what one creator calls "failure by suffocation" and says was their biggest mistake.
- You can't force a signature format. Start with one visual element you repeat in every post. When one works, repeat it until it stops working.
- One format can be enough. One creator says she was "one format away from 100K" followers. Her format was stop-start reaction videos, where she pauses a clip to react. She posted 6 to 10 trial reels a day plus daily posts, and says she went from 10K to over 100K followers in a few weeks (her own account, not checked).

### Pick formats you can keep up and enjoy

- Choose formats you enjoy and are good at. You will make them hundreds of times, so if you dread them you will stop.
- Many people force themselves into formats they dislike, like vlogs, talking straight to camera, or video essays. If you would rather have someone off camera asking you questions, an interview format may suit you better. A format that works for someone else often fails for you if you hate making it.
- Constraints can make the choice easy. One creator never leaves his office, never moves the camera, and keeps his personal life out of his content. That narrows his formats to what he can make from one chair.
- Content pillars help you organise this. Pick 3 to 5 topics you will come back to again and again, and pair each with one or two formats. Give each pillar a job: some bring new people in, others give followers a reason to trust you. For example, say you are a filmmaker who uses AI. Finished films could be for attention, and breakdowns of how you made them could show clients how you think. Plan several posts per pillar each month, check whether each pillar is doing its job, then improve or replace it.

### Study formats, test them, and kill what flops

- Know your format, and only study winners in that same format (talking head, vlog, music montage, how-to, list). Formats carry over between topics better than topics carry over between formats. A productivity creator can learn from a fitness video if it uses the same format.
- The biggest shortcut is to copy the viral format of the moment, never the content, and bend it to your topic. Platforms keep changing what wins: Quora answers, then Medium articles, Twitter threads, LinkedIn carousels, and now X articles. If a format keeps showing up in your feed, it is there for a reason.
- The kill rule: if you post a style twice and it flops twice, drop that format, at least for now. Check your analytics every day to see it.
- Your job is still to come up with new, original ideas, angles and hooks. Take inspiration from what is trending, but be careful about chasing views by jumping on every trend.

### Frameworks

**Content formula**

1. Pick 3 to 4 topics you own.
2. Pick 1 to 3 formats.
3. Pick 1 to 3 visual layouts (how it looks on screen).
4. Combine them into 5 to 10 formulas you can repeat, like "topic A + whiteboard format + same simple layout".

**Content pillars**

1. Pick 3 to 5 topics you will keep returning to.
2. Pair each with 1 or 2 formats.
3. Give each a job: attract new people, or build trust.
4. Plan several posts per pillar each month.
5. Review whether each pillar does its job, then improve or replace it.

### Examples

- **Three formats that run for a long time:** One founder-creator keeps to three formats: deep breakdowns of systems, whiteboard sessions where he helps founders live, and behind the scenes. His best-performing content is the live, unscripted whiteboard sessions.
  <br>*Why it works:* Few formats, repeated, means people know what to expect and can describe what he does.
- **One format took an account past 100K:** One creator says stop-start reaction videos, posted at very high volume, took her account from 10K to over 100K followers in a few weeks. That is her own claim.
  <br>*Why it works:* Once one format works, do more of it instead of adding new ones.

### Common mistakes

- Filming in a format you hate because it works for someone else.
- Spreading across many platforms and formats before one system runs smoothly.
- Studying winners in a different format than yours.
- Trying to force a signature format instead of starting with one repeated element.
- Keeping a format that has flopped twice in a row.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say X pushes all formats, so test many at once. Others say to stay on one platform and one format until your system runs 30 days in a row without breaking.

### Try it

List every format you posted in the last month. For each one, mark whether you enjoy it, whether it suits the platform, and whether it worked or flopped twice. Keep at most 3 that you enjoy and that suit the platform. Write each as a formula: topic + format + visual layout.

## 6.2 Format menus: proven shapes to borrow

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** You never have to start from a blank page. There are ready-made lists of video formats, ways to talk on camera, story types and series ideas. Pick a topic, pick a known shape, and combine them.

### Short-video formats

- Here are 15 short-video formats you can borrow: clone (copy a video's structure with your own topic), red flag vs green flag, listicle, rapid-fire listicle, tier list ranking, top 10 ranking, this or that, bad / good / great, a "yap" (you talk to camera about a topic), reaction, don't vs do, split screen, screen share, sticky note, and overrated vs underrated.
- The hottest formats for 2026 are game-like ones: rankings, tier lists, good / better / best, "matters or doesn't matter", and clone videos. People enjoy them because the viewer can argue with the ranking.
- A talk-to-camera video can come in three shapes: game-like, straight to camera, or a list.
- Every talk video is made of three parts: a topic (what it's about), an angle (your take), and a format (the shape). Mix and match them and you will never run out of ideas. Say your topic is sleep: angle "naps are overrated", format "overrated vs underrated". That is a video.

### 12 types of stories and videos

- The 12 types are: breakdown, case study, problem and solution, simple tip, quick tutorial, full tutorial, listicle, ranking or rating, scenario ("imagine this happens"), personal update, day in the life, and narrative (a real story).
- Pick the type that shows off what you are best at. If you are great at explaining, do tutorials. If you are great at telling stories, do narratives.
- One creator says that at the end of 2025 and start of 2026, a cinematic personal narrative (a well-filmed story about your own life) was the hot type. The hot type changes every 3 to 6 months, so don't tie yourself to it.

### 5 ways to talk on camera

- Five talk formats cover about 90% of good content. 1. A strong take: say "X is the best" or "X is the worst", then defend it. Conviction makes people reply, and replies push your reach. 2. A strong take into teaching: the take is only the hook, then you teach something useful. 3. A small epiphany: "have you noticed how...". 4. Humor. 5. Story time.
- What is not on the list: "my top 3 favourite...", generic tips and motivational speeches. They are everywhere and give people no reason to stop.
- Here are 3 ready scripts. The basic one is hook, a story, point 1, point 2.
- The second is "the 8 Mile", named after a rap battle scene where a rapper says all his own weak points first. You open with a hook, then you say the other side's arguments yourself and knock them down, then you give your reason. It works because it answers people's objections while they are still thinking them.
- The third is "the 4 things": four small takes in one video. For example, "4 things I noticed at Coachella as a brand strategist". Each take gives people a reason to stay for the next one.
- Other talk formats that work: free work (design a logo or merch for a huge brand without being asked, then post it, and they often reply), the niche-niche idea ("3 chest exercises for men over 50", very narrow on purpose), would or wouldn't ("10 things I'd buy at Trader Joe's as a food scientist"), and curation (you sort through a lot of stuff so your audience saves time).

### Written formats and structures

- Six structures are used the most. 1. Problem, insight, takeaway: name a problem, share what most people miss, then give them something to do. 2. The list: a hook, numbered points and a closer, which is easy to save and share. 3. The graphic: one point packed into one easy visual. 4. Video. 5. Article. 6. Quote tweet.
- Every post needs a shape. Without one, even a great idea reads like a ramble.
- One creator's own mix of post types includes: big list posts, numbered lists of use cases, resource posts with a link (a post of "50 social media hooks" got 642 bookmarks and "Content advice from 50 of the biggest personal brands" got 613), videos with chapter lists, an article retold as a video, an expert interview with a "10 key takeaways" post, free templates, and news posts ("I went through all of it, here's what you need to know").
- Newer ideas to try: a "takeaways from a call with [person]" list that tags the people involved, a video about a company with chapters, a free guide of 15 or more pages with the link in the first reply, a solo podcast that only answers questions collected in your comments, a teaser post that hints at something coming, and dense list posts.
- One creator says that in his first 26 days he got 2.9M views across 97 posts, and that 86 of 98 posts were pieces that taught something. These are his own numbers, not checked. The useful part is the mix: mostly posts that teach.

### Series: make people binge

- A rough rule: someone usually needs about 4 to 5 minutes of your content before they decide to follow you. A series gets them there, because they keep watching.
- A hard series is numbered. One creator says a "30 lessons before 30" series took him from 0 to 1M followers in about 4 months (his own claim). A soft series has no numbers, but every video feels like the same show, for example every episode opens with the same line.
- Don't start a daily hard series if you will burn out. A series you drop halfway is worse than none.
- Another idea is to create a container instead of content. Put someone in a setting where just being themselves makes good content: kids asking a business YouTuber questions on the street, Q&A on stage, coaching calls, or podcasts.

### One event, many angles

- A single event, like a product launch, can give you several different posts. Say a company launches a new AI tool. You could write: the founder's story, what the product changes (which work it affects, who benefits, why one feature matters more than it seems), a video reaction (walk through the demo, pause at interesting parts, say what surprised you and where you are skeptical), a controversial take you can defend with evidence, or a post about competing interests (it saves one person money but threatens another person's business, so you explore the tension honestly).
- A post that says "insane launch" only says you are impressed. Every post needs its own reason to be read.
- This way one project gives you several useful pieces without lazy repurposing.

### Frameworks

**Problem, insight, takeaway**

1. Name a problem.
2. Share what most people miss about it.
3. Give them something to do.

**The 8 Mile script**

1. Open with a hook.
2. Say the other side's arguments yourself and knock them down.
3. Give your reason.

### Examples

- **A numbered series that grew fast:** One creator says a numbered series called "30 lessons before 30" took him from 0 to 1M followers in about 4 months. That is his own claim.
  <br>*Why it works:* Numbers make people want the next part.
- **The 4 things script:** "4 things I noticed at Coachella as a brand strategist" is four small takes in one video.
  <br>*Why it works:* Each take gives people a reason to stay for the next one.

### Common mistakes

- Posting generic tips, "my top 3 favourite" lists or motivational speeches.
- Writing a post with no shape, so it reads like a ramble.
- Writing a launch post that only says it's impressive.
- Starting a daily numbered series you can't keep up.

### Try it

Take one topic you know well and write it in 4 formats from this lesson: a tier list, an 8 Mile script, a 4 things video and a problem, insight, takeaway post. Keep the one that feels most like you.

## 6.3 Short form vs long form

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Short videos reach the most people. Long videos build depth and trust. Most creators say use both, with one platform for reach and one for depth, but they disagree on which to start with.

### What each length does

- Short form reaches the widest audience. Long form holds people for longer, and those minutes are more "sticky", meaning they build a stronger bond. Live streams are the stickiest of all.
- A simple formula: the value of your personal brand = how many people see you x how many minutes they spend with your content x how sticky those minutes are. A minute of long-form is stickier than a minute of short-form.
- A short post that holds attention for 10 seconds is small next to a video, podcast or newsletter that holds someone for 15 to 60 minutes. The people with the most power today are the ones who hold the most attention over a person's lifetime.
- This doesn't mean short form is useless. It is the best way to get new people to find you, so don't skip it.
- A 20 to 30 minute video shows people who you are far better than a 30-second clip. That is why one business YouTuber's long videos built such a strong bond with viewers, and why another creator started YouTube even after reaching 50K followers on Instagram.

### The modern stack and how often to post

- A simple personal brand stack has three parts. First, one short-form platform for reach, like Instagram or LinkedIn. Second, one long-form platform for depth, ideally YouTube, where you keep pushing your contrarian beliefs (ideas that go against what most people say). Third, one place to nurture people, ideally email or a private community. That is all you need.
- One suggested pace: 7 short videos a week and 1 to 2 long videos a week. Make as many short videos as you can, but stay on a narrow set of topics. It will take hundreds and hundreds of posts.
- Another suggested pace: YouTube once or twice a week, and short form once or twice a day.
- One creator predicts that within 3 to 6 months, original long and short video on X will be the best growth strategy. He says a single video brought him 1,000 new followers in a day (his own claim).

### Where to start

- One view: start on a short-form platform like X, Instagram or LinkedIn, because they are easier to grow on at the beginning. YouTube, podcasts and Medium need search optimisation and slow build-up. The claim is that on YouTube people won't really notice you until you have at least 50 videos, that the same goes for podcasts, and that podcast search is very poor. So build on X first, where you control how your posts spread, then move the audience to YouTube or a podcast. Meanwhile, practise video and speaking on the side while you get good at writing.
- Another view: start on one long-form platform (X or YouTube), then turn those ideas into short posts. Pour all your water into one cup instead of half-filling three or four.
- Long form is like many short videos under one theme, plus a second storyline. A famous YouTuber's main storyline was staying in 50 Airbnbs in 50 days. The second storyline was raising money for charity. That second storyline is what keeps people watching across the whole series.

### Attention windows differ

- On short-form platforms, people decide in about 4 seconds whether to stay. On YouTube, they give you about 1 to 2 minutes.
- The same hook formula works in short and long form. The first step, grabbing attention in the first 1 to 2 seconds, is identical.
- Don't aim for everyone to watch to the end. Only your biggest fans will. Instead, fight to keep people for each extra 30 seconds by putting the value early.

### Measure them separately

- Judge short and long video separately. Check your long-form views and your short-form views as two different numbers.
- When you search a niche to see how much demand there is for long videos, filter out YouTube Shorts.
- Longer videos usually do better when people watch on a TV.
- One example of video beating text: an investor was going to announce an investment with a thread, but made a simple video instead. It got 50,000 views in 24 hours on Twitter. A Twitter Space with the founder also pulled several hundred people quickly.

### Frameworks

**Modern personal brand stack**

1. Reach: one short-form platform, about 7 videos a week.
2. Depth: one long-form platform, 1 to 2 videos a week.
3. Nurture: email or a private community.

**Personal brand value**

1. Viewers: how many people see you.
2. Content minutes: how long they spend with you.
3. Stickiness: long-form and live minutes stick more than short-form minutes.

### Examples

- **The 50 Airbnbs series:** A famous YouTuber made a series about staying in 50 Airbnbs in 50 days. That was the main storyline. The second storyline was raising money for charity.
  <br>*Why it works:* Long form can be many short stories under one theme, and the second storyline keeps people watching the whole series.
- **An investment announced by video:** Instead of a thread, an investor posted a simple video announcing the investment. It got 50,000 views in 24 hours.
  <br>*Why it works:* The video was easy to make and stood out on a text platform.

### Common mistakes

- Judging short and long form by one combined number.
- Expecting YouTube or podcasts to work fast, without a long run of episodes.
- Spreading across 3 or 4 half-filled platforms.
- Chasing full watch-through instead of fighting for each extra 30 seconds.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say start with short form (X, Instagram) because it is easier to grow, then move people to long form. Others say start on one long-form platform and turn those ideas into short posts. A third group runs both at once, with 7 short videos plus 1 to 2 long ones a week.

### Try it

Write down your reach platform, your depth platform and your nurture channel. For each, write the pace you could keep for a year. Then take your best short post and outline how it would become one long piece.

## 6.4 Scripting a video hook: four layers and the key visual

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A video hook has four layers: what you say, what you show, the words on screen, and the sound. All four have to say the same thing. Viewers take in the picture first, so start with the key visual, the main thing people see, then write 2 to 4 tight lines to match it.

### The four layers, lined up

- Every video hook has four parts. The spoken hook is what you say. The visual hook is what you show. The text hook is the words on screen. The audio hook is the music and sound effects.
- One creator says the difference between 500 and 500,000 views is getting all four to say exactly the same thing. Even a slightly different visual from what you are saying counts as a mismatch.
- Why it matters: people can only be hooked if they fully understand what they see and hear. A mismatch leads to confusion, and confusion makes them leave.
- In a video, the hook is the first 1 to 2 seconds: movement, text on screen and your spoken line together.

### Eyes, ears, eyes again

- Here is how most people take in a video's first seconds. First they take in the picture and the text. Then their ears catch up and they hear what you say. Then they look back at the picture to check that what they heard matches what they saw.
- The claim is that eyes process 10 to 100 times more information per second than ears, like light being faster than sound. This is a creator's claim, not something we checked.
- So the most important part of a hook is not what you say, it is what viewers see first. That is the key visual.
- So show the topic before you say a word. One creator calls visual hooks 10 times more effective than spoken ones (his own claim).

### Writing the spoken lines

- A hook is 2 to 4 lines. Line 1 gives the context, meaning the topic. Next comes a word that makes people stop scrolling, a contrast word like "but", "however" or "yet". Last comes a contrarian snapback, a line that goes against what people expect. Put the topic in the first sentence and the contrast in the next one or two.
- Cut anything before the topic. Say your script has 13 lines and the topic first shows up on line 3. Then lines 1 and 2 are filler, and people swipe away during them. Delete them.
- Use short, punchy sentences in the hook, then medium and longer ones as the video goes on. Short sentences force you to be clear, and every word counts for more when time is most precious.
- For the rest of the video, repeat this loop: context, then immediate value, then repeat. The first piece of value should come in the hook or right after it.

### Six hook styles, chosen by your goal

- Fortune teller: you compare the present with the future, like "This is the future of animation." Use it for news, launches, and showing authority on where a topic is going.
- Experimenter: you show a demo or experiment, one peer to another, like "I used to do it this way, let me show you how I do it now." Use it for product demos, new frameworks, tool showcases and build videos.
- Teacher: you teach a lesson, like "Three things you can learn from Aritzia." Use it to show expertise so you can sell it.
- Investigator: you reveal a secret or a research finding, like "This is a secret Japanese city built at the base of Mount Fuji." Use it when you do deep research.
- Contrarian: you say outright what you believe that differs from common wisdom. Use it if you are an expert in a crowded space.
- Magician: a visual or spoken stun gun like "Check this out", which you can put in front of any of the other five.
- Any idea fits all six. Take a backpack. Fortune teller: "This backpack is going to completely change the way millennials travel." Experimenter: "I just took this backpack 7,000 miles... it has three major design flaws." Contrarian: "Everybody loves this bag, but... it is so overhyped." So pick the style that matches your key visual.

### Making a boring topic feel new

- Use this order: the new thing and what it does for you, then a contrast with the old belief, then urgency (only if it is real), then the closest proof. Tell it like a secret, without hedging.
- Say your topic is root canals. The script would be: a robotic root canal with zero needles, zero pain and back to work fast. Then contrast it with how painful root canals used to be. Then say few dentists are trained on it yet. Then mention a patient who was back at work within the hour.
- You can add more detail, like how the process works, after those core pieces.

### Frameworks

**5 steps to a hook, starting from the key visual**

1. Find the visual or visuals for the first 3 to 5 seconds. If you have no good visual, ask whether you should make the video at all, or create one with motion graphics or stock footage.
2. List the most interesting facts or angles. The one with the biggest contrast picks your hook style.
3. Write the spoken hook: 2 to 4 lines, with the topic, a contrast word, then the contrarian line.
4. Decide the on-screen text that supports the first visual.
5. Watch it back as a viewer would: picture, speech, picture. Is it clear what the video is about, and does it match the rest? If not, rework it.

**The four hook layers**

1. Spoken: what you say.
2. Visual: what you show.
3. Text: words on screen.
4. Audio: music and sound effects.

### Examples

- **A life-size floor plans video that got 15M views:** The video opens with a quick split-screen clip that stops you, then the key visual: people walking on floor plans projected onto a warehouse floor, with the text "life-size floor plans" and an arrow. The creator says: "Check this out. These are life-size floor plans. You can literally walk through your exact home design before you build it. I think this is the future of how people are going to design their homes."
  <br>*Why it works:* The words describe exactly what you see. The idea existing was contrast enough. The creator chose the fortune teller style because it was easier to explain and more shocking.
- **A video about world models that did far worse:** The creator put the text "future of storytelling" over rooms appearing, and said "It's called a generative world model... a photorealistic cinematic world."
  <br>*Why it works:* The text was abstract, the term was unknown to most people, and the visuals didn't explain it. The creator later said it should have been explicit ("These rooms are the future of commercials") or dropped.

### Common mistakes

- Saying one thing while showing something slightly different.
- Filler lines before the topic.
- Long sentences in the hook.
- Using abstract words the visuals don't explain.
- Choosing a hook style before finding the key visual.

### Try it

Pick your next video idea. List 3 possible key visuals for the first 3 seconds and pick the strongest. Write a 3-line spoken hook that describes exactly what is on screen, plus 3 to 5 words of on-screen text. Then watch it in your head: picture, speech, picture. Is the topic clear?

## 6.5 The first frame and visual hooks

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The first 1 to 2 seconds are won by what people see. The first frame should look different from the rest of the feed, leave clean space for a short line of text, and have just enough motion. This lesson gives concrete fixes.

### Stimulation: the first 1 to 2 seconds

- The first thing a hook has to do is stimulate. Think of a deer that notices movement and freezes. A viewer pauses when the colour, motion, brightness or contrast is different from what they usually see in their feed. This is the same in short and long videos.
- Slight zoom-ins and unusual angles help. For example, one YouTuber opened with a view from inside a car, a perspective you rarely see in a feed.
- Use just enough motion. Too much overwhelms people and they lose track of what is going on. Too little bores them.
- A "stun gun" clip, a short striking visual, can come before your key visual. One video opened with a split-screen of a girl with her arms going up, and only then showed the floor plans.
- Rapid cuts between similar things also stun people. For example, many backpacks cut together very fast. A sudden cut can also fake people out: one YouTuber cut from falling backwards to being dragged along while singing.
- Visual pacifiers are simple background clips, like someone doing makeup, someone stacking cups, or split-screen game footage. They hold a little of the viewer's attention in the background so they can focus on your main point.

### On-screen text

- Use a title of 3 to 5 words in a big, bold font. It tells people what the video is about. Choose words that can't be misunderstood: "future of home design" works better than a product name nobody knows.
- Use arrows to point the viewer's eye at the key visual.
- Text can ask the question the picture alone doesn't. In one video the text was "Bro, what did I just witness?", which made people want to know.
- Keep any other text to 1 or 2 lines, about 5 to 10 words, because people scroll fast and only scan.
- Podcast clips often put a big text hook on screen, like "THE TRUTH ABOUT ADDICTION", so the right viewers recognise it is for them.
- A repeated spoken opener, like always starting with "Check this out", becomes a sound people recognise.

### First-frame fixes

- Remove a caption that is too big, and the frame instantly looks cleaner.
- Leave clean space for text. In one kitchen example, the person, the kitchen and the clutter left no room for text. If you can't clear space, put a box behind the text, like the text background Instagram offers.
- Try different colours. On a coffee video, red text looked cheap and off, and an unexpected colour worked better. Small touches like "..." at the end of a line, which opens a loop, or a soft glow around the text can also help.
- You can mock up a better first frame with an AI image tool like ChatGPT. Describe it like this: "real screenshot, nice natural lighting, kitchen, big bowl of unhealthy carbs in the bottom half, scroll-stopping, 9:16". It shows you what the shot should look like before you film.
- For image posts, a carousel (swipeable set of images) does better than a single picture, but it still needs a hook on the first slide.

### Check it like a viewer

- Watch your hook with the sound off first and notice where your eyes go. Then watch with sound and check that picture, speech, picture is completely clear.
- For image posts, put the headline on the image. Keep it to one idea, and make sure it is readable on a phone.
- One creator thinks graphics on screen are the least important extra for a hook, and that your setting and how you deliver the line matter more.

### Frameworks

**First-frame checklist**

1. Safe zones: keep text away from the app's own buttons and captions.
2. Caption size: make it smaller than you think.
3. Framing: leave clean space, or add a box behind the text.
4. Font weight: vary it, bold for the key word.
5. Colour: test options and add small touches like "..." to open a loop.
6. Declutter: one clear subject, one light or one window.

### Examples

- **A shoelace video breakdown:** The video opens with a slight zoom-in from inside a car. Two people hug. The text says "Bro, what did I just witness?" A shoelace gets stuck in the car door, then a hard cut shows the guy being dragged along while singing.
  <br>*Why it works:* The unusual angle stops the scroll, the text asks the question, and the cut delivers the surprise.
- **A coffee video fix:** In the first version, the latte didn't stand out and the wall behind it gave no depth. A clean shot with the coffee as the focus looked far better.
  <br>*Why it works:* Decluttering and one clear subject matter more than expensive gear.

### Common mistakes

- Captions that are too big or sit under the comment box.
- Busy frames with no space for text.
- Too much motion, which loses people, or too little, which bores them.
- Text that can be misunderstood, like a product name nobody knows.
- Never watching your own hook with the sound off.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say the visual is the most important part of a hook. Others say on-screen graphics are the least important extra, behind text, setting and delivery.

### Try it

Screenshot the first frame of your last 3 videos. For each, check: is the text inside the safe zone, is it 3 to 10 words, is there one clear subject, and does it look different from the rest of the feed? Redo the worst one.

## 6.6 Short-form structures

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A short video needs a structure, not a riff. Several proven ones exist, for teaching (hook, explain, illustrate, teach), for stories (hook, progression, climax, plus V.I.R.A.L. and C.A.R.T.). All of them hold the payoff back until the end.

### Hook, explain, illustrate, teach

- Hook: set the context, say something that goes against what people expect, and open a loop (a question the viewer wants answered). You can also add proof, a promise and a plan.
- Explain: say what the video is about and why it matters to the viewer.
- Illustrate: tell a story, analogy or example that links something they know to something they don't yet.
- Teach: give ONE simple thing they can take away and apply. The trap is cramming 6 to 10 steps into a short video. Many videos also name a problem and never teach the change in behaviour.
- The creator of this structure says he uses it in 90% of his content, that it grew his Instagram, and that it works on every platform, not just short form. He says it went through a thousand versions while testing.
- Before it, his team would write a few ideas on screen and riff. Riffing without an order means people take nothing away. Educational creators should aim to change what the viewer does, not just entertain them.
- Frameworks beat creativity here: creativity doesn't scale, frameworks do, and with one you never start from a blank page.

### Hook, progression, climax

- Editing is how you present a video (cutting, pacing, captions). Structure is how you build it (the story and the payoffs). You need both.
- Hook: in the first 5 seconds, introduce the topic and leave many questions open. One example is a snowball fight in the desert.
- Progression: meet the expectations the intro set up. The creator shows the journey to get snow: the sand won't form balls, so they grab a cooler and head to the mountains. The bad version would be pulling a cooler of ice out of the car, which would disappoint.
- Climax: the moment the topic is fulfilled, the snowball fight. Use fitting music, a few jokes and editing that matches the style.
- Reveal the payoff at the end, not 5 seconds in. One short about the secret to a famous creator's success gave away the secret at second 5, so everyone left.

### The 60-second story and V.I.R.A.L.

- The 60-second story: start with a weird question, show constant progress toward the answer, add conflict (a second storyline), keep the answer uncertain, then pay off quickly and concisely.
- Example: a burger video that got 45M views asked "is it faster to cook a burger in the drive-through line?". Adding tomato, lettuce and cheese lets viewers track how close the creator is to finishing. Her mom adds conflict ("you're going to burn that car"). The burger was basically raw, and it didn't matter, because viewers wanted the answer.
- If everything is smooth sailing, nobody cares. And if it takes you longer to tell the story than to make the burger, you are overcooking both.
- V.I.R.A.L. is a newer framework. V is for Visual shock: even a teaching video needs a picture, like a jar of golf balls, rocks and sand to show that you should do big tasks first. I is for Immediate start: begin almost at the climax, with no setup. R is for Rising action: raise the stakes. "How I make coffee" is dull, "the coffee that keeps me awake 24 hours" is not. A is for Anticipation: give hints and puzzle pieces, never the answer early. L is for Lasting payoff: the last word is the answer. Put any call to action before the answer, because people leave the second they hear it.
- Show progress, because people leave when they can't see an end. Use timers on screen, tick off ingredients, or say "first, second".
- The number one mistake is selfish content, which is talking about yourself instead of what the viewer gains. Aim for topics with high demand and low supply. As one creator says, your video could be bad, but if nobody made it and people want it, you still get views.

### C.A.R.T. for 1 to 2 minute stories

- Context: who, where, when and what they want, in 2 to 3 sentences.
- Adversity: the longest part. Say what is at stake and how it felt.
- Resolution: how it ended.
- Takeaway: the lesson, stated as "what I learned is...".
- Don't end a story with "you should" or "the moral is". Let the takeaway come from what you learned.

### Endings that loop

- Write the last line first. Make it memorable enough to share on its own. In a short video the last line can also set up a loop back to the first line, so people watch again.
- Close the loops you opened. Entertainment shouldn't end on an unresolved cliffhanger.
- Research on memory, called peak-end research, shows people remember the high point and the end of an experience, not its length. That fits having one high point and a strong last line.

### Frameworks

**Hook, explain, illustrate, teach**

1. Hook: context, a surprising take, an open loop.
2. Explain: what it is and why it matters to them.
3. Illustrate: a story, analogy or example.
4. Teach: one simple thing they can apply.

**Hook, progression, climax**

1. Hook: the topic in the first 5 seconds, with open questions.
2. Progression: meet the expectations the hook set up.
3. Climax: deliver what they came for, at the end.

**V.I.R.A.L.**

1. Visual shock.
2. Immediate start.
3. Rising action: raise the stakes.
4. Anticipation: hints, never the answer early.
5. Lasting payoff: the last word is the answer, and any call to action comes before it.

**C.A.R.T.**

1. Context in 2 to 3 sentences.
2. Adversity, the longest part.
3. Resolution.
4. Takeaway: what I learned is...

### Examples

- **A drive-through burger video:** Can she cook a burger faster than the drive-through line? The added ingredients show progress, her mom adds conflict, and the answer stays uncertain until the end: "We cooked faster than the drive-through." The video got 45M views.
  <br>*Why it works:* A question, visible progress, conflict, an uncertain answer, and a fast payoff.
- **A desert snowball fight video:** The hook promises a snowball fight in the desert. The progression shows the drive to the mountains to find snow. The climax is the fight itself.
  <br>*Why it works:* The progression earns the climax instead of skipping to it.

### Common mistakes

- Revealing the payoff in the first seconds.
- Teaching 6 to 10 steps in one short video.
- Naming a problem and never teaching the change.
- Putting the call to action after the answer.
- Making selfish content about you, not the viewer's gain.
- Ending a story with "you should" or "the moral is".

### Try it

Take one tip you know well and write it as a 45-second script using hook, explain, illustrate, teach. Check that there is only ONE thing to apply, and that the illustrate step links it to something people already know.

## 6.7 Long-form structure: openings and sections

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A long video needs a strong opening that reassures people and opens a loop, then repeated sections that each make one point and lead into the next. A business YouTuber's script and a breakdown of MrBeast's openings show how.

### The long-form opening

- The basic opening answers three questions: what is this (promise), why should I trust you (proof), and how will you get me there (plan).
- A fuller version has 3 jobs. Reassure: make a promise that matches the title and thumbnail, give proof fast that answers "why you?", and handle the doubt "this won't work for me". Elevate: make the promise bigger with extras or a free bonus, and add urgency, either a deadline or something that just changed. Open a loop: show a road map on screen, and give your method a name.
- Here is a script from a business YouTuber. Promise: "the six phases to scale your business from 0 to a million dollars in 12 months". Proof: he went from 80-hour weeks to under 5 hours a week. Doubt handled: "the same six phases I took over 10,000 of my coaching clients through". Extra: a free template. Loop: the 6 phases listed on screen but blurred. Named method: "the 10-80-10 rule".
- A common pattern is a claim, a proof line in the first 15 seconds, the promise, then a numbered road map on screen, like "10 principles" or "7 strategies".

### The road map and sticking to your promise

- A road map means early on you show how many steps you will take them through. Blur the later ones so viewers wonder what is coming.
- Every piece of content makes a promise in its title and opening. Anything that drifts away from that promise should be cut, because it confuses people. This applies to videos and to posts. One creator calls this the sacred timeline.
- Research backs up showing progress. A 2006 study of car-wash loyalty cards gave some people a card with 2 of 10 stamps already filled in, and others a blank card needing 8 stamps. 34% of the first group finished the card, against 19% of the second. People keep going when they can see they have already started.

### Sections: point, explain, illustrate, lesson

- This is how every long video on one big business channel is built, and the creator calls it the most important thing he could teach.
- Point: say what it is, like "phase one, delegate your tasks". Explain: say why it matters, like "most entrepreneurs hold on to things they shouldn't". Illustrate: give an example, story or analogy, like a client who was stuck in their email all day. Lesson: give a clear next step, like the 10-80-10 rule, where you do 10% of the thinking together, they do 80% of the work, and you spend the last 10% giving feedback.
- Repeat this 2 to 6 times per video, sometimes 10 to 20 times. A video called "40 brutal truths I wish I knew in my 20s" uses it 40 times.
- Keep it practical, not long, drawn-out stories.
- The same idea works for any teaching: give each point a mini loop of what is going on, why it matters, an example and what to do. Then people always know where they are in the video.
- Link your sections with bridges. End each one with something like "but X isn't enough, which brings us to number Y". Each point raises a question that the next one answers.
- Put a free offer in the middle of the video, and a "watch this video next" at the end.

### Start broad, get denser

- Start with a broad problem everyone has, then get denser and more practical as the video goes on. The end can be a small nod to your core audience.
- For example, a video about meetings ends with "instead of lots of meetings, have clear scorecards, KPIs and owners for those KPIs". That last line is aimed at business owners, who recognise themselves.

### The MrBeast opening formula

- An analyst broke down MrBeast's video about 100 people on an island, which the analyst says got close to 40M views in two days. He says it rests on 3 things: near-perfect retention, a thumbnail and title that make people click, and a big budget to make it.
- The intro formula is: open fast, show the scale, then start the first challenge. Nearly every MrBeast video follows it.
- Establishing shots show the setting and wow viewers with how big the production is. The camera starts close on Jimmy, then zooms out to show the whole island. All 100 contestants stand in a line.
- Retention usually levels off once the first challenge or storyline starts, so the first challenge starts at about 17 seconds. The tour of the island comes after the first challenge, not before.
- After the intro, there is tension every few minutes. Characters repeat the rules ("the red line, that's all we're thinking about") so viewers always understand. Natural moments stay in so it isn't too polished, and the video ends on a high point.
- The look is deliberately 1080p, never over-produced.

### Frameworks

**Reassure, elevate, open a loop**

1. Reassure: promise, proof, and answer the doubt.
2. Elevate: make the promise bigger and add real urgency.
3. Open a loop: a blurred road map and a named method.

**Point, explain, illustrate, lesson**

1. Point: what it is.
2. Explain: why it matters.
3. Illustrate: an example, story or analogy.
4. Lesson: a clear next step.
5. Bridge to the next point.

**MrBeast opening formula**

1. Open fast.
2. Show the scale with a wide shot.
3. Start the first challenge, about 17 seconds in.
4. Explain everything else after.

### Examples

- **MrBeast's island video:** The camera zooms out from Jimmy to the whole island, 100 contestants stand in a line, the first challenge starts at 17 seconds, and the island tour comes after.
  <br>*Why it works:* Retention levels off once the storyline starts, so it starts early.
- **"40 brutal truths":** Point, explain, illustrate, lesson, repeated 40 times in one video.
  <br>*Why it works:* A repeated shape keeps a very long video easy to follow.

### Common mistakes

- An opening that doesn't match the title, like starting with a long unrelated story.
- Touring the setting before the first storyline starts.
- Stories that drift away from the promise.
- Long, drawn-out stories instead of practical sections.
- Energy that drops through the middle with no tension.

### Try it

Outline a 10-minute video. Write the first 30 seconds as promise, proof, answer to the doubt, and road map. Then write 4 sections as point, explain, illustrate, lesson, each ending with a bridge line to the next.

## 6.8 Pacing and retention

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Content is rarely too long. It is too slow. Make your early points fast so people feel progress, open a new question at every transition so there is no dead air, and keep the big question open until the right moment.

### Make early points faster than later ones

- Make your early points shorter than your later ones, so the video feels fast. It creates an illusion of progress, like an infomercial progress bar that rushes at first and then slows. Viewers think "I'm already on point 3" and don't want to give up what they have invested, so they stay.
- MrBeast's first section is always the fastest.
- Rough timings: point 1 about 30 seconds, point 2 about 1 minute, point 3 about 1.5 minutes, point 4 about 2 minutes. In longer videos: about 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 minutes.
- If you can be 3 minutes in and already at point number four, that is great for retention.
- It works for videos, sales copy, emails and LinkedIn posts. The creator who teaches it says he came up with it himself.

### A pacing curve

- Go fast for the first 15 seconds, medium until 30 seconds, then slow down right before the reveal, because that is when people are most invested and will wait.

### Open a new question at every transition

- The moment between two sections, when one question has been answered, is when people are most likely to leave. Open a new question, problem or consequence right there, in the same breath.
- Never leave dead air between sections. "Anyways, now I'm on to..." is the bad version. A better version is "but here's the problem with that".
- Online, don't use the school-essay shape of one slow build and one peak. Start at about 70 out of 100 intensity, hit the first conflict early, release, then peak again every few minutes. This is called re-hooking.
- In long videos, add a surprise or a misdirection about every 2 to 3 minutes.

### Managing the big question

- Ask the big question as early as you can, so the wait for the answer lasts longer.
- Before any important reveal, add context so the viewer can guess the answer first. Anticipation is highest just before the answer arrives.
- Viewers can only guess if they understand the facts. Odd details or facts they can't follow confuse them, and they leave.
- Close your loops at the end. If a question stays open, the viewer feels unsatisfied.
- In teaching videos, repeat this loop: context, immediate value, repeat. Put the value early to win each extra 30 seconds.

### Visual pace

- MrBeast's retention isn't only lots of cuts and fast talking. His own point is that talking to one camera too long without a cut is boring, and cutting between angles helps.
- One analyst counted a visual change every 1.1 seconds in the first 20 seconds of his island video.
- Cut moments that repeat themselves or lose the audience.
- In the hook, use short, punchy sentences, then medium and longer ones as the video goes on. Mix lengths so it doesn't drone. If you write one sentence per line, the right edge of your script should look jagged.

### Is attention really shrinking?

- A popular idea is that attention spans have shrunk because everything is faster now. But there is no evidence that human attention capacity has changed. The famous claim of an 8-second goldfish attention span traces back to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report that cited a website with no research behind it.
- A more accurate reason is competition. People have endless options, so they leave boring content faster. The problem is short patience for boring content, not short attention spans.

### Frameworks

**Faster early points**

1. Point 1: the shortest, about 30 seconds to 1 minute.
2. Point 2: a bit longer.
3. Point 3: longer again.
4. Point 4 and on: the longest, once people are invested.

**Re-hook**

1. Close the current question.
2. In the same breath, open a new question or problem.
3. Use a bridge phrase like "but here's the problem with that".

### Examples

- **MrBeast's island video:** There is a visual change every 1.1 seconds in the first 20 seconds, with cuts between camera angles.
  <br>*Why it works:* Variety keeps the eye busy, but retention also comes from structure, not cuts alone.

### Common mistakes

- Blaming length when the real problem is slow pace.
- Making every section the same length from start to finish.
- Leaving dead air between sections, like "Anyways, now I'm on to...".
- Building slowly to one single peak.
- Talking to one camera too long without a cut.
- Blaming short attention spans instead of boring content.

### Where the sources disagree

Some people explain faster viewer drop-off by saying attention has sped up. Others point out that attention capacity hasn't changed and competition has. The advice is the same either way: don't be boring.

### Try it

Time each section of your last long video or article. Are the early sections shorter than the later ones? Rewrite the first section to half its length, and add a line at the end of every section that opens a new question.

## 6.9 Scripts, outlines and script length

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Read-aloud scripts sound robotic, so film from an outline you have memorised. Whatever you write, count its length, get the hook and pacing checked, and keep the writing your own.

### Outlines, not scripts

- People sound like robots when they read a script word for word. Write an outline instead and use it as a guide. Some creators say that even calling it a script is a disservice, because it should be notes off to the side that you never read out.
- Follow it loosely and go off on small tangents. That is what makes people connect with you, and it is more fun to film.
- Think trail, not rail. A train stays on its rails. A hiker can wander to the edges of a trail and always comes back to the middle. Treat your outline like a trail.
- Write it yourself. One creator writes most scripts by hand and uses AI only to widen his options, for example to get 50 angles to choose from.
- Don't type "make me a viral video" into ChatGPT and read the result off a teleprompter. Audiences can tell.

### Filming without a script

- Here is a four-step method for filming without a script or teleprompter. Step 1: write the outline, ideally the same week you film so it is fresh. Step 2: remember it. Read it before you shoot, like actors learn their lines. Step 3: riff. Let yourself go on small tangents, because that is where natural delivery comes from. Step 4: repeat. If you are bad at delivering something, you just haven't said it enough times. One business YouTuber has said his main points hundreds of times to his team and on stage. Nobody is a natural, and the more you practise, the more natural you sound.

### Recording tricks

- Pause and stay silent while you think, then cut the silences out in editing.
- Re-record your intro at the end, when you are warmed up.
- People want to hear from someone at their level or one level above, so you don't need to sound like a guru.
- If you are clipping a podcast and the real moment has no punchy opening, re-record a hook afterwards, in the same clothes.

### Script length

- People talk at about 3.5 words per second, so a 30-second ad is about 105 words. Count your words, don't guess.

### AI as a script coach, not a writer

- Don't ask AI to write for you. Ask it to make you better.
- Give it videos you find extremely engaging plus your own best performers, and teach it what makes them work, including your structure and your voice.
- Then it can score your scripts, flag where people are likely to drop off, and say how to fix it. For example: "Hook is too slow." "You start teaching too early." "Tension drops here. Consider cutting this line." "This doesn't sound like you."
- One way to split the work: use AI for the first 10% (finding videos that did unusually well) and the last 10% (reviewing and pointing out blind spots). The middle 80%, which is scripting, shooting and editing, is done by real people.
- A quick rhythm test for a script: write one sentence per line. A straight right edge means it is monotone, and a jagged edge means it has rhythm.

### Frameworks

**Filming from an outline**

1. Write the outline the same week.
2. Remember it before you shoot.
3. Riff on small tangents.
4. Repeat your points until the delivery feels natural.

### Examples

- **An AI script coach's notes:** "Hook is too slow." "You start teaching too early." "Tension drops here. Consider cutting this line." "This doesn't sound like you."
  <br>*Why it works:* The AI has learned what works for you, so it flags drop-off points instead of writing generic scripts.

### Common mistakes

- Reading a script word for word and sounding like a robot.
- Reading an AI-written viral script off a teleprompter.
- Guessing script length instead of counting words.
- Recording the intro first, while still cold.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say never read a script and film from a memorised outline. Others write and check full hook scripts line by line, with 2 to 4 short lines and the topic in line 1. Both fit together: script the hook tightly and outline the body.

### Try it

Turn your next video into a 5-line outline, one line per point. Read it twice, then film it without looking. Film the intro again at the end. Count the words in your hook at 3.5 words per second: does it fit in 3 to 5 seconds?

## 6.10 Filming: delivery, setting and simple production

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Your topic, script and delivery matter far more than gear. Talk to one person, keep production simple and in the style of the platform, film in settings you enjoy, and learn the craft through many reps.

### Delivery: a conversation, not a presentation

- People watch alone, on a couch or at a computer. So talk to one person. Top creators say "you", never "you guys", "hey everyone" or "y'all". Group words turn it into a performance.
- Lower your tone. Many creators think they need to act as loud and hyped as MrBeast, but people see through it. Educators should be calm and collected. Don't scream, don't rush, and pause when you need to.
- Conviction beats charisma. One creator calls himself pretty bland and very stoic on camera, and he still grows because he believes what he says.
- Avoid upspeak, which is ending sentences with a rising tone. It sounds unsure. End sentences on a low note and it sounds like authority. Slow down and let pauses happen. When you are nervous you rush and ramble.
- Talk with people, not at them. Film as if you are talking to one close friend. One trick is to tape their photo under the camera lens.
- The gap between how you talk to the camera and how you talk to a friend should be tiny.

### Keep production simple

- The 20% that matters most is the idea, the script and your delivery, not editing, lighting or camera gear. You can't out-edit a bad topic.
- One creator grew to 50K followers with just his iPhone. He shot every video in one take and added only a caption at the top. His main edit is a road map on screen. He learned that bad audio does hurt, so he bought a cheap microphone.
- Time per week, from one creator: for short form, about 1 hour of ideas, 20 minutes of shooting and 2 hours of editing. A big business YouTuber spends 3 hours a week in total.
- One creator says a polished video got 47,000 views, while a raw clip in his kitchen got about 100 times that. Content that looks like it belongs on the platform earns trust.
- People are tired of fake setups, like a staged podcast in a studio cut into 50 reels. They want the real you and the behind-the-scenes. One creator claims he went from 0 to 50K followers in 6 months with about 200 videos of talking to his phone.
- MrBeast films in 1080p on purpose, because he never wants it to look too produced. Founder videos on X should also be sincere and self-made, with no special effects.
- Audio quality and other basics still matter, but if there are no obvious problems they are not why a video underperforms. Big hits come from the idea, the title or the thumbnail, almost never from a better camera.

### Setting and signature look

- Test different settings, like your car, office, home or desk. One creator says switching between his kitchen and his car gave him a video that did 10 times better than his usual.
- A signature visual style is made of four things: the look, the set, how you frame the shot, and how you edit. One creator with 1.8M followers uses the same cosy wall of framed pictures behind her, a slow calm pace, matching music and almost no editing. If you can't edit, the cheapest fix is to build one great set once and reuse it.
- Your edge can be new information or a new way of delivering known information. That creator's tips are basic, and the slow delivery is what makes her different.
- Familiarity is powerful. Repeat a filming style, a way of speaking, even a shirt. One business YouTuber always wears a blue shirt so people recognise him.

### Make filming sustainable

- Plan filming so it adds to your life instead of taking from it. One creator picks cool locations and Airbnbs, films mid-week, has his girlfriend and dog visit at the weekend, and filmed one video on a motorcycle ride.
- One team shot 10 pieces of content in 7 days on a trip to Lisbon. It worked because of structure, a team and clear ownership of who does what.
- Another idea is to create a container: coaching calls, Q&A on stage, podcasts, or asking people questions on the street. Give the person asking a 3-step question so the clip performs: where are you today, where do you want to be, and what is stopping you?
- When someone else is on camera, one team asks them questions to lead them, instead of giving them lines or a teleprompter.
- One podcast host asks short, broad questions, then therapist-style follow-ups like "how did that make you feel?". The best clips come from the second or third follow-up. Pick guests with energy. If a guest is flat, bring your own energy, because people mirror it.

### Learn the craft through reps

- Every top creator can roughly do it all: edit and make thumbnails. One made thumbnails in Photoshop for years. Early on, build the basics, like editing, thumbnails, storytelling, camera, simple lighting and colour grading, rather than deep strategy. If you lack a skill, have someone on your team who covers it.
- YouTube has the most moving parts of any platform: the idea, the packaging, a strong start and holding attention. Each is its own skill, so you need practice making whole videos.
- In your first 1 to 2 months you learn what the algorithm likes, editing basics, hooks, scripting, camera confidence, lighting, extra footage and captions with a shadow. Early videos look worse for every creator.
- One creator says his first 100 Instagram videos were bad. Get them done fast. He got better from spending 20 to 60 minutes on camera every week.
- Expect to stumble and stutter, and to feel the video isn't perfect. Ship anyway.

### Face or faceless

- Faceless content can win attention and keep people watching, but it rarely builds a strong bond, because there is no personality to like.
- Choose a format you are comfortable in. Some people would rather be interviewed than talk straight to camera.

### Frameworks

**A 3-step question for clips**

1. Where are you today?
2. Where do you want to be?
3. What is stopping you?

**Signature visual style**

1. Look.
2. Set.
3. Shot framing.
4. Editing.

### Examples

- **A raw kitchen clip:** One creator says a polished video got 47,000 views, while a raw clip in his kitchen got about 100 times more.
  <br>*Why it works:* Looking native to the platform is a free source of credibility.
- **A gallery-wall set:** One creator with 1.8M followers uses the same cosy wall of pictures behind her, a slow calm pace, matching music and almost no editing.
  <br>*Why it works:* A great set built once, plus a distinct delivery, can replace heavy editing.

### Common mistakes

- Copying MrBeast's high energy when you are an educator.
- Saying "hey everyone" or "you guys" instead of "you".
- Ending sentences with upspeak and rushing.
- Spending on gear and polish instead of topic and delivery.
- Filming in a way that drains you.
- Waiting for videos to be perfect.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say to build basics like editing, thumbnails and colour grading early. Others say editing, lighting and gear are outside the 20% that matters, and point to one-take iPhone videos that grew an account.

### Try it

Film the same 30-second tip three times: once in your usual spot, once in a new setting (car, kitchen, desk), and once while picturing one specific friend behind the lens. Watch them back. Which one sounds most like a conversation?

## 6.11 Editing craft and giving editors feedback

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Editing should help the viewer understand, not show off. Show on screen what you say, cut what bores people, keep cuts smooth, and separate notes that must be fixed from notes that are just taste.

### Show, don't tell

- Whatever is said, show it on screen as soon as you can. If the speaker says "12 AI companies", show the founders and logos. If he says "supercars", show the supercars.
- Most people learn visually. When you give an editor feedback, ask what on screen would help the viewer understand, not how many flashy graphics can fit.
- Show a visual for every point. For complex points, use a visual, or say it twice, the second time as a simple metaphor, like "600 mph is a plane driving on the road".
- Often the main edit is just a road map on screen.

### Editing techniques

- Cutaways: say a story mentions a famous bodybuilder, and the audience doesn't know who he is. The editor adds a short cutaway that gives context fast, like a clip showing he is one of the greatest bodybuilders of all time.
- Punch-ins: on a one-camera shoot, editors zoom in on a shot to fake a second camera angle. The wide shot and the tight shot should have the speaker's eyes in the same part of the frame, so the cut isn't jarring. Experienced editors see a mismatch as a serious error. Beginners don't notice it.
- Cutting between camera angles beats talking to one camera for too long. This is MrBeast's approach.

### Must-fix notes vs taste notes

- A note is mission-critical if, without fixing it, nobody understands what is said, follows the story or gets the point. It is also critical if it hurts your brand, like a moment taken out of context that sounds far more intense or off-message than you meant.
- Spelling errors are objective and critical.
- Most other notes, like whether the font is blue or orange, framing, or colour correction, are subjective. They are a matter of taste.
- One creator critiques every keyframe in his editors' videos because he cares more about the result than about their feelings. His comparison is that Olympic gold and silver are separated by milliseconds.
- Another creator says your editors don't suck, your system does. He claims a B-level editor can become an A-level one with the right systems and training.

### Encourage the good, and note "for future reference"

- Tell editors what to keep doing, not only what to fix. Most of one team's feedback comments are things they want more of, like "I love this cutaway, you did a great job with this". Editors usually only hear what to change, so on the next edit they waste energy guessing what to keep. Reinforce the behaviours you want to become habits.
- For the next two weeks, on every piece you review, leave more comments about what you liked than about what to change. Still flag what must change: spelling errors, moments out of context that hurt the brand, and repeated moments that lose interest.
- Split your fixes into "for now" and "for future reference". If a note isn't mission-critical and you post a lot, give it as a note for next time, with the reason. For example: "One note for future reference: I love when we punch in on a shot so the eye line matches up, that way it's not as abrupt." The claim is that this is the fastest way to speed up an editor's learning. It makes perfectionists uncomfortable.
- There is no golden ratio. If you currently fix everything now, start with 30% for future reference and 70% for now. Watch it for a month. Are you moving faster, or are more spelling errors and bad colours slipping through? Then adjust.

### How you give feedback depends on how much you post

- If you post about one YouTube video a month, the loop is slow, so 80 to 90% of notes should be "fix it on this video" and 10 to 20% "for next time".
- If you post 14 Instagram reels a week, speed and learning from your audience are worth more than taking a piece from 80% to 95 or 100%.
- On long edits, check in often. On a four-week vlog about a sensitive subject, weekly check-ins caught in week 2 that the editor was building the story around a moment the person on camera wouldn't want included. That avoided a full redo.
- In teaching content, the one thing you control is how clear the action steps are. Pair a download with a video to raise the odds that people act.

### Re-editing and mashups

- A mashup solves a constraint. If the person on camera can't film for a month, pull moments from past content around one theme into an inspirational mashup. You could brief an editor with: "a mashup of me talking about how to build a brand, only moments where I'm speaking on a podcast." A clear brief like this beats "make it go viral".
- One creator ran a test: a new TikTok account with zero followers, a first post that was a slight re-edit of a clip that had already done well. He says it got 8.2M organic views (his own claim).

### Frameworks

**Sorting editor notes**

1. Mission-critical: the point is lost or the brand is hurt. Fix now.
2. Objective: spelling errors. Fix now.
3. Subjective: colour, font, framing. A matter of taste.
4. Match the fix-now share to how much you post: low volume fixes on this video, high volume moves on.

### Examples

- **A bodybuilder cutaway:** A story mentions a famous bodybuilder, so the editor cuts away to a short clip showing he is one of the greatest bodybuilders of all time.
  <br>*Why it works:* Fast context, so the viewer isn't lost.
- **A four-week vlog:** A weekly check-in in week 2 caught that the editor was building the story around a moment the person on camera wouldn't want shown.
  <br>*Why it works:* Check-ins on long edits prevent full redos.

### Common mistakes

- Adding flashy graphics that don't help understanding.
- Punch-ins where the eye line jumps.
- Treating taste notes as critical and slowing a high-volume team.
- Waiting until the end of a long edit to review it.
- Blaming editors when the system is the problem.

### Try it

Watch one of your videos and write down every note you have. Mark each one as mission-critical, objective or subjective, and fix only the first two. Then find one moment where you name something and add a visual that shows it.

## 6.12 Posting frequency: volume, quality and batching

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Creators disagree on how often to post. One names two routes and says never pick the middle. Another argues for volume in some videos and for posting less in others. All agree on one thing: pick a pace you can keep for years.

### Two routes: pick one, never the middle

- The quality route is 3 to 5 posts a day, spaced at least 3 hours apart. Timing isn't that important, but spacing is, because each post needs time to run. Every post gets decent engagement over time.
- The quantity route is up to 24 posts a day, at least 1 hour apart. It only suits news accounts or very fast niches like AI and crypto. You accept that many will flop. It is like throwing darts and hoping 1 or 2 a day stick.
- The advice is to choose one of the two, not a blurry middle.

### A frequency test

- One creator tested posting from 2 a week up to 4 a day. One a day was the sweet spot. At 4 a day only half of the videos got picked up and quality dropped.
- In that test, the day of the week did not matter, but the time of day did. Post when your audience is active.
- Another creator disagrees and says the algorithm does not care what time you post.

### Volume beats luck

- Posting is a game of repetitions. You won't build a brand off 10, 20 or 30 posts. The amount you post is directly tied to how many followers you get, and more posts a day means more chances to go viral.
- One creator puts it this way: he would rather have volume on his side than luck, because he can control volume.
- One business YouTuber posted since 2014, weekly on YouTube and then daily on Instagram, for 12 years, and became an overnight success 3 years ago. Another creator says that if he posted a reel every day for 10 years and got no views, he would quit, and he doesn't expect that.
- A mentor's lesson: you don't understand quality until you have done quantity. Some people hide behind quality and post once every 6 months.
- Posting also keeps you top of mind. Showing up daily in even 10 to 1,000 feeds makes people want to help you, text you and DM you, and it brings opportunities. Think of a plumber friend who texts you every day for a year. He is the one you call when you need a plumber.

### The case for posting less

- In other videos the same creator says every second or third day is fine. One great piece beats 10 average ones, and quality beats 10 to 20 weak videos a day.
- 10 rushed YouTube videos don't get pushed by the platform. One great one can get pushed for a year or two. That business YouTuber has videos still getting views years later because he focused on quality.
- That creator's own pace is one reel every day since Nov 22, 2025, with no day missed, all shot on an iPhone to remove every excuse. His suggested pace is once or twice a week for YouTube and once or twice a day for short form. He adds that you don't want to have your big breakthrough moment too early, before you are ready for it.
- The fair reading of both: pick a pace you can keep for years, and make each piece as good as you can at that pace. Volume doesn't need to happen in a week. It could happen over a decade.

### First quality, then volume, then more quality

- First get your quality to 7 out of 10. Then raise your volume to the normal pace for the platform: short video 1 a day, YouTube 1 a week, short text about 5 posts a day. Then push quality to 9 out of 10.
- After that, stay consistent for 6 months, answer every comment, and move people to your email list.

### Batching

- Post daily but shoot weekly. Set one content day so you don't burn out.
- But never batch more than a week or two ahead. Batching content just batches your mistakes. You should learn from every shoot before you film the next one.
- If you are excited about an idea, make it now. Excitement fades by shoot day. Reduce the time between idea and action.

### Three ways to run your posting

- The sniper: heavy focus on quality and picking the right videos, sometimes with weeks or months between uploads. MrBeast works like this.
- The machine gun: upload every day, and some hit big while some don't. A popular commentary YouTuber works like this.
- The weekly operator: about one video every week for two years, improving steadily and building a catalogue. A well-known wildlife YouTuber worked this way.
- At the start, high frequency makes sense just to get practice and learn the craft. Later you choose the model that fits you, and commit to it.

### Frameworks

**The two routes**

1. Quality: 3 to 5 posts a day, at least 3 hours apart.
2. Quantity: up to 24 posts a day, at least 1 hour apart, only for news or fast niches.
3. Pick one, never the middle.

**Quality, then volume, then more quality**

1. Get quality to 7 out of 10.
2. Raise volume to the platform's normal pace.
3. Push quality to 9 out of 10.
4. Stay consistent for 6 months, answer every comment, and move people to email.

### Examples

- **A frequency test:** One creator tested 2 posts a week up to 4 a day. One a day was the sweet spot. At 4 a day only half got picked up and quality dropped.
  <br>*Why it works:* Another creator says the algorithm doesn't care about posting time, so treat the timing result as one person's test.
- **A decade of posting:** One business YouTuber posted from 2014, weekly on YouTube and then daily on Instagram, and became an overnight success 3 years ago.
  <br>*Why it works:* Used as proof that volume pays off over a decade.

### Common mistakes

- Picking a middle pace that is neither high quality nor high volume.
- Posting 4 times a day when only half get picked up and quality drops.
- Hiding behind quality and posting once every 6 months.
- Batching a month ahead, which batches your mistakes.
- Waiting for shoot day when you are excited about an idea now.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say volume beats luck, and that you can't build a brand off 10 to 30 posts. Others say post less, every second or third day is fine, and one great piece beats 10 average ones. They also disagree on timing: one says the time you post matters but the day doesn't, another says the algorithm doesn't care what time you post.

### Try it

Pick your route: quality (3 to 5 a day) or quantity. Write the pace you could keep for a year. Plan one weekly content day, and make sure no batch reaches more than 2 weeks ahead.

## 6.13 The speedrun playbook for short video

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** This is a six-part plan for growing fast on short video: make people binge, build a signature style, find your unique edge, write strong hooks, pick the right type of story, and post daily while copying your own winners.

### The six parts

- 1\. Make people binge. A follow usually needs about 4 to 5 minutes of your content (a rough rule), so make people watch several videos. A hard series is numbered. A soft series has no numbers, but each video feels like the same show.
- 2\. Build a signature visual style. It is made of your look, your set, how you frame the shot, and your editing. If you can't edit, the cheapest fix is to build one great set once and reuse it.
- 3\. Find your unique edge. That is either new information, or a new way of delivering information people already know.
- 4\. Write hooks that give two hits of interest before people leave: first something to see, then a tease of the value.
- 5\. Pick the type of story that shows off what you are good at, from the 12 types in lesson 2. The popular type changes every 3 to 6 months.
- 6\. Post daily, and review your results in batches of 10.

### Hooks in this playbook

- Give people something to look at first, then tease the value. That gives them two reasons to stay before they can swipe away.
- Make the value clear within about 2.5 seconds.
- Make it instantly relatable by showing a face or brand your viewer already knows. This is sometimes called cult hopping, because you borrow trust from someone they already like.

### The daily loop and the first big push

- Post daily. After each batch of 10, sort the batch by followers gained, not views, and copy what the winner did differently in your next batch.
- Growth often starts with one video that breaks out, plus a big account sharing it. That is how a small account gets its first big push.
- In one view, about 5,000 followers is the minimum for people to see you as credible.

### Frameworks

**Speedrun growth: 6 parts**

1. Make people binge with a series.
2. Build a signature visual style.
3. Find your unique edge.
4. Write hooks: something to see, then the value in 2.5 seconds.
5. Pick your type of story.
6. Post daily and review in batches of 10 by followers gained.

### Examples

- **Reviewing a batch:** After 10 videos, sort them by followers gained. Look at what the top one did differently, and copy that into the next batch of 10.
  <br>*Why it works:* Sort by followers, not views, because followers show what made people want more of you.

### Common mistakes

- Sorting a batch by views instead of followers gained.
- Starting with the style and skipping the hook.
- Expecting one video to do everything without a breakout video and a share from a big account.

### Try it

Take your last 10 short videos, sort them by followers gained, and write down one thing the top one did differently.

## 6.14 One idea, many platforms: repurposing and sequence

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** One piece of content can feed many formats and platforms, but creators disagree on how well mass repurposing still works. Some give a sequence (video first or article first) and a rule for each platform.

### Each platform needs its own treatment

- Make one clean video, then give it a different hook and caption for each app. The same video can do great on Instagram and flop on TikTok. So track how many people stay past the first second and how much of it they watch, not just views.
- A format that works on X may need different treatment on Instagram or TikTok. Split your plan by platform as you learn what each one likes.

### The waterfall and its limits

- The waterfall is the idea of making one long piece, like a long video or article, and cutting it into everything else: clips, posts, quotes.
- One view is that the waterfall works less than it did 6 to 12 months ago. Turning a long video into written posts still works. Cutting a YouTube video into short clips doesn't necessarily work anymore. Each platform needs its own plan.
- Another view says one long piece can become 5 or more short pieces. One creator's own route is to make the long piece first, then cut it down.

### Video first or article first

- One creator's example: he posted a video about the algorithm 2 days before the article on the same topic. For a copywriting topic he swapped the order, with the article first and the video the next day. Test the order for each piece.
- Video versions of articles are for people who don't like reading. One creator makes them with visuals created in Claude, raw and unedited.

### Market gaps

- One creator says almost nobody on X makes written UGC (content written for brands in a normal person's voice, instead of a brand's). He calls it a huge market gap.

### Frameworks

**One clean video, different wrappers**

1. Make one clean video.
2. Write a different hook and caption for each app.
3. Track how many people stay past the first second and how long they watch, not just views.

### Examples

- **Video then article, or the other way round:** A creator posted his algorithm video two days before the article. For a copywriting topic he swapped the order: the article first, then the video the next day.
  <br>*Why it works:* Test the order for each piece.

### Common mistakes

- Posting the same hook and caption on every app.
- Judging a cross-post by views only.
- Assuming a YouTube video cut into shorts still works the same.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say one long piece can become 5 or more short ones, and the waterfall was built on that idea. Others say the waterfall works less than it did 6 to 12 months ago, and that cutting YouTube into short clips doesn't necessarily work anymore.

### Try it

Take one long piece and write a different hook and caption for two platforms. After a week, compare how many people stayed past the first second.

## 6.15 AI in video production and editing

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** AI can work as an editor and a production tool. You teach it your style, give it raw footage, approve its plan, then let it edit. For AI-made video, learn each piece one at a time and keep your characters and products locked.

### An AI editor workflow

- Teach it your editing style. Give it finished videos, ask it to break down how they are edited and how you tell stories, and go back and forth until it explains your style back to you in plain English.
- Upload raw footage, even a 10-minute take, and it finds the best takes.
- Ask for an editing plan: the structure, the cuts, where extra footage goes, even photos from your drive.
- Approve it or give feedback before editing starts. This keeps a human in the loop.
- Then start the edit: cuts, graphics and music, in a tool like CapCut, DaVinci Resolve or Premiere Pro, or on the AI's own cloud machine. Review, refine, then post.
- One example: three videos fully produced by the AI. They were a Minecraft-style explainer, a "what if" natural disaster video, and a talking-head ad. The animated ones started from a one-sentence prompt, and the AI made the storyboard, script, narration and music.
- One creator gave it 10 raw files at once, and all were edited in about two hours, versus about two hours per video by hand. His point is that AI editing doesn't just make editing faster, it lets you produce several videos at the same time.

### Learning AI video production

- Learn in this order: images first, then voices, then video generation, then scripts, then putting it all together.
- Train your eye to spot what feels off: a washed-out starting image, a voice that sounds like a studio ad, a script nobody would say out loud. Fix each one on its own before combining them.
- Generate footage with options for the edit. Take a small section of the story, turn it into a montage prompt, and pull out the strongest moments. This teaches you how to structure prompts, use character references, keep continuity between shots, and add sound. Keep your references tight when you need consistency, and give the model freedom when you want interesting extra footage.
- For an ad: develop the look in still images, build reference sheets for anything that repeats (even the car), generate the footage, then find the film in the edit. The final piece must still hold together after the novelty of AI wears off.

### AI characters and ads

- UGC characters (made-to-look-like-real-people videos): describing a person in words gives you a different face each time. So make a character sheet first, with a close-up and a full-body image, and use those images for every clip.
- The person can hold the product but must never change it, or you get a second bottle or a sixth finger. Keep clip prompts short.
- For a long ad, lock your characters, products and places before making anything. Make it scene by scene, timed to the song.
- One creator claims you can use AI to analyse creators' body movements and make realistic AI UGC with the same hook and structure as a video that already worked, for about a tenth of the cost of hiring a creator.
- The same creator describes "AI slop stories": short, fully AI-made stories about the problem an app solves. People call it slop, but he says it gets views and converts.

### Frameworks

**AI editor workflow**

1. Teach it your editing style.
2. Upload raw footage.
3. Get an editing plan.
4. Approve it or give feedback.
5. Start the edit.
6. Review, refine and post.

**AI video learning order**

1. Images.
2. Voices.
3. Video generation.
4. Scripts.
5. Putting it all together.

### Examples

- **10 raw files at once:** One creator gave the AI editor 10 raw files at once. All were edited in about two hours, versus about two hours per video by hand.
  <br>*Why it works:* The claim is that AI lets you edit many videos in parallel.

### Common mistakes

- Describing a character in words each time and getting a new face.
- Letting the model change the product in the clip.
- Combining images, voice and script before fixing each one.
- Skipping the approval step on the editing plan.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say AI slop stories and AI replicas get views and convert, at a fraction of a creator's cost. Others say the final piece must still hold together after the novelty of AI wears off. Both are claims, not proven results.

### Try it

Pick one finished video. Ask an AI to describe how it is edited, and refine the description until it matches your style in plain English.

## 6.16 Satisfaction: deliver what the packaging promised

*6 min read*

> **The big idea:** Viewer satisfaction is the most underrated part of YouTube strategy. YouTube measures how much value a video gives per minute, and a great title and thumbnail can't save a video that doesn't deliver on them.

### What satisfaction is

- Viewer satisfaction is YouTube's way of measuring the value a video gives per minute. YouTube itself says it is the thing to focus on.
- It gets overlooked because there is no number for it in your dashboard, and people optimise for what they can see.

### Three things to do on every video

- Deliver on the promise in the intro. The title and thumbnail make a promise, and the quickest way to lose satisfaction is to not keep it. Aim to deliver it, with a picture or with words, within the first 5 seconds.
- Obsess over the details. Viewers notice a glitchy edit, a typo in a caption or a spike in audio, and they judge a video fast.
- Invest in things that don't scale. A creator who worked for MrBeast says it taught him the value of over-delivering. A 10-second moment could take 40 hours to film and edit, and those are the moments people remember.

### A video that got 8,000 views and died

- A YouTube strategist tweeted about how good one video's title and thumbnail were, and then watched it. The captions were sloppy, with typos and mismatched words. The video didn't deliver on its promise, and it had no structure or story.
- It shot up to 8,000 views on day one, then died. His bet is that if it had delivered, it would have passed 100,000 views. YouTube gave it a real chance, and it didn't deliver.

### The TV opportunity (2026)

- For one team's clients, TV has become the biggest device people watch on, by a wide margin.
- What the team found: the best video length for TV is over 20 minutes, a slower pace with deeper storytelling is paying off, and production quality matters more, because people don't put up with poor video quality, sound or lighting on TV the way they would on a phone.
- This is about long videos on TV. Short videos on phones still rely on fast change, as in the pacing lesson earlier in this module.

### Frameworks

**Satisfaction checklist**

1. Deliver the promise in the first 5 seconds.
2. Fix every typo, glitch and audio spike.
3. Add one over-delivered moment that doesn't scale.

### Examples

- **Great packaging, no delivery:** A video with a great title and thumbnail got 8,000 views on day one, then died. It had typos in the captions, didn't deliver the promise, and had no story.
  <br>*Why it works:* The strategist's bet: it would have passed 100,000 views if it had delivered.

### Common mistakes

- Making a promise in the title that the video doesn't keep early.
- Leaving caption typos and audio spikes.
- Ignoring satisfaction because there is no number for it.

### Where the sources disagree

The TV advice (slower, deeper, longer) pulls against the fast-pacing advice elsewhere in this module. Both can be true, because they describe different screens and lengths.

### Try it

Watch the first 5 seconds of your last video, or read the first 3 lines of your last post. Did they deliver what the title or hook promised? Rewrite them so they do.

## Cheat sheet: Formats and video

The whole module on one page.

- Pick the hero format of your platform (the one it pushes hardest) and stay on it until your system has run for 30 days.
- Run at most about 3 repeatable formats, and change the topic, not the shape.
- Study winners in your own format, not just your niche.
- If a style flops twice, drop it for now.
- Make the four hook layers (what you say, show, write on screen and play as sound) all say the same thing.
- Find the key visual first, then write the hook around it.
- Write the hook in 2 to 4 short lines, with the topic in the first sentence.
- Teach only one thing in a short video.
- Save the payoff for the end.
- Open long videos with the promise, the proof and the plan in the first 30 seconds.
- Make early points shorter than later ones so the video feels fast.
- Open a new question at every transition between sections.
- Use an outline instead of a script, and talk as if to one friend.
- Sort editor notes into must-fix now and taste for later.
- Show on screen what you say.
- Pick a posting pace you can keep for years.
- Don't batch more than 1 to 2 weeks ahead.
- Give each platform its own hook and caption, and track how many people stay past the first second.
- Review each batch of 10 posts by followers gained.
- When AI helps you edit, keep a human approval step.
