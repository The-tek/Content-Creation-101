# Module 6: Formats and video

*Pick a few formats, script the hook, pace it fast, keep production simple and show up at a pace you can hold.*

This module covers which formats to choose, how short and long form differ, and how creators build hooks, structures and pacing for video. It also covers delivery, editing notes, posting frequency, repurposing and AI in production. Creators often disagree, for example on volume versus quality and on whether the waterfall still works, and each lesson shows both sides. The common thread is to use a few repeatable formats, put the value up front and keep making them.

## 6.1 Choosing your formats

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A format is the repeatable shape of a piece of content. Creators agree you should pick a few formats that are native to your platform, that you can sustain, and that you enjoy, then run them for a long time and kill the ones that keep failing.

### Be native to the platform

- Every platform has its own language. For X that might be articles, for LinkedIn longer personal posts.
- One view: pick the platform where your buyers spend the most time, then the format that platform pushes hardest. This is called the platform's "hero format".
- Hero formats: Instagram, TikTok, Facebook and Snapchat = short video. YouTube = 10 to 30 minute videos. LinkedIn = short text or LinkedIn's new short video, called "the biggest untapped opportunity right now" (one creator's opinion). X and Threads = short text.
- X is a good pick for developer tools, crypto, startups and tech buyers.
- Another view: all formats get pushed on X, so test every format X is pushing: threads, articles and media.
- Founder videos on X should be sincere, self-made, with no special effects.

### The 4 boxes: two mediums, two lengths

- The 4 boxes: long video builds deep trust, short video gives raw reach, long writing (articles, newsletters) gives depth and authority, short writing (posts) is "your playground to test ideas fast". Winners from short writing become articles or long videos.
- Mix and match: cover every funnel stage AND both mediums (video and written) AND both lengths (long and short).
- You can tick all 4 boxes on one platform. One creator only posts on X but does articles, long and short videos and daily posts.
- What's working right now: short-form video (reach), carousels (saves and education), founder-led content (trust), build in public (people follow a journey live), long-form video (deep trust), newsletters (owned audience), employee-led content (for companies of 20+ people).
- "Writing" on social media includes captions, Instagram posts with text on the image, tweets, LinkedIn posts, reel scripts and YouTube scripts. You can grow without posting your body, images or designs.

### A few formats, run long

- One creator picks about three formats he loves and lets them run: deep systems, whiteboard sessions (helping a founder live on camera) and behind the scenes. "I keep it simple." His audience's favourite is watching him help other founders.
- Max 3 repeatable formats, rotate them forever. "Change the input, not the structure." A known format also makes word of mouth easy, because people can say what you do.
- One view: one platform and one format until a system runs 30 days in a row without breaking. Spreading too early is called "failure by suffocation", one creator's own biggest mistake.
- A signature format can't be forced. Start with ONE repeated visual element. Once one works, "run it over and over until it stops working".
- One format can be enough. A creator was "one format away from 100K": stop-start reaction videos. She tripled down (6 to 10 trial reels a day plus daily posts) and went from 10K to over 100K in a few weeks (that creator's own account).

### Pick formats you can sustain and enjoy

- "Focus on formats you enjoy" and "pick content formats you excel at".
- Many people film in formats they hate or feel uncomfortable in: vlog, direct to camera, video essay. Many would rather have someone off camera asking questions, interview style. Playing a game that works for others will not work for them.
- One creator uses constraints to choose his format: he never leaves the office or moves the camera, and keeps his personal life separate.
- Content pillars: pick 3 to 5 recurring concepts, pair each with one or two formats, and give each a job: what brings new people in vs what gives followers a reason to trust you. Example for an AI filmmaker: finished films for attention, production breakdowns to show clients how you think. Plan several posts per pillar per month, review against their purpose, improve or replace.

### Study formats, test, and kill what flops

- Know your format and only study winners in it (talking head, vlog, music montage, how-to, list, explaining vs vlogging, educating). Format transfers across niches more than niche does: a productivity creator can study fitness videos in the same format.
- The biggest lever is to copy the viral FORMAT of the moment, never the content, and bend it to your niche. Platforms keep changing (Quora answers, then Medium articles, Twitter threads, LinkedIn carousels, now X articles). If it's in your feed, it's there for a reason.
- The kill rule: "if you do a style of post twice and it flops twice, kill that format, at least for now". Review your analytics every day.
- The #1 advice for creators: your job is to create new, original concepts, formats, angles and hooks. Take inspiration, but be careful chasing views through pure trend-jacking.

### Frameworks

**The 4 boxes**

1. Long video: deep trust
2. Short video: raw reach
3. Long writing (articles, newsletters): depth and authority
4. Short writing (posts): a playground to test ideas fast, winners become long pieces

**Content formula**

1. Topic: 3 to 4 topics you own
2. Format: 1 to 3 formats
3. Visual layout: 1 to 3 layouts
4. Combine into 5 to 10 repeatable formulas

**Content pillars**

1. Pick 3 to 5 recurring concepts
2. Pair each with 1 or 2 formats
3. Give each a job: attract new people or build trust
4. Plan several posts per pillar per month
5. Review against purpose, improve or replace

### Examples

- **Three formats that run long:** One creator runs deep systems, whiteboard sessions and behind the scenes. His best-earning content is live, unscripted whiteboard sessions helping real founders.
  <br>*Why it works:* Few formats, repeated, so people know what to expect and can describe what the creator does.
- **One format took an account to 100K:** Stop-start reaction videos, posted at very high volume, took the account from 10K to over 100K in a few weeks.
  <br>*Why it works:* The point: once one format works, triple down on it.

### Common mistakes

- Filming in a format you hate because it works for someone else.
- Spreading over many platforms and formats before one system runs smoothly ("failure by suffocation").
- Studying winners in a different format than yours.
- Trying to force a signature format instead of starting with one repeated element.
- Keeping a format that has flopped twice in a row.

### Where the sources disagree

One view: all formats get pushed on X, so test many at once across the 4 boxes. Another: stay on one platform and one format until a system runs 30 days in a row without breaking.

### Try it

List every format you have posted in the last month. Mark each one: enjoy it or not, native to the platform or not, worked or flopped twice. Keep at most 3 that you enjoy and that are native. Write each as a formula: topic + format + visual layout.

## 6.2 Format menus: proven shapes to borrow

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** You never need to start from a blank page. The sources give ready lists of formats, talk shapes, storytelling types and series ideas, so you can mix a topic with a known shape.

### Short-form formats

- 15 short-form formats: clone, red flag green flag, listicle, rapid fire listicle, tier list ranking, top 10 ranking, this or that, bad good great, yap, reaction, don't vs do, split screen, screen share, sticky note, overrated underrated.
- The hottest formats for 2026 are gamified: ranking, tier list, good better best, matters or doesn't matter, clone videos.
- Yap sub-formats: gamified, straight to camera, listicle.
- Anatomy of a yap: topic + angle + format. Mix them so you're never stuck with a blank page ("an unlimited content engine").

### 12 storytelling types

- Breakdown, case study, problem-solution, simple tip, quick tutorial, full tutorial, listicle, ranking or rating, scenario, personal update, day in the life, narrative.
- Pick the type that shows off what you're good at.
- One view (end 2025, early 2026): cinematic personal narrative is hot right now, and the hot type changes every 3 to 6 months.

### Talk formats and scripts

- 5 talk formats cover about 90% of good content: strong take ("X is the best or worst", then defend it; conviction makes people reply, replies drive reach), strong take into education (the take is only the hook, then you teach), small epiphany ("have you noticed how..."), humor, and story time.
- What's NOT on the list: "my top 3 favourite...", generic tips, motivational speeches.
- 3 scripts: hook, story, point 1, point 2. "The 8 Mile": hook, name the other side's arguments yourself and knock them down, then your reason (it kills objections while they're thinking them). "The 4 things": 4 mini takes in one ("4 things I noticed at Coachella as a brand strategist"), each makes them stay for the next.
- Talk formats that work: free work (design merch for billion-dollar brands unasked, they reply), the niche-niche playbook ("3 chest exercises for men over 50"), would or wouldn't ("10 things I'd buy at Trader Joe's as a food scientist"), curation (save people time).

### Written formats and structures

- 6 structures used most: problem, insight, takeaway (name a problem, share what most people miss, give them something to do); the list (hook, numbered points, closer, easy to save and share); the graphic (one point packed into one easy visual); video; article; quote tweet.
- "Every post needs a shape. Without one, even great ideas read like a ramble."
- One creator's own mix: big list posts, numbered use-case lists, resource posts with a link (a "50 social media hooks" post got 642 bookmarks, "Content advice from 50 of the biggest personal brands" 613), videos with chapter lists (a crash course was the best post), an article retold as a video and quoted with the article, an expert interview plus a "10 key takeaways" post, open-sourced templates, news-jacking ("I went through all of it, here's what you need to know").
- Newer formats: "takeaways from a call with [person]" lists tagging the people, a company case study video with chapters, a free 15+ page guide with the link in the first reply, a planned solo podcast answering only questions collected in the comments, a teaser post, and dense list posts.
- One creator's claim from his first 26 days: 2.9M views across 97 posts and 86 of 98 posts were value pieces. Treat the numbers as his own.

### Series: make people binge

- A rule of thumb: a follow usually needs about 4 to 5 "content minutes", so make people binge.
- Hard series = numbered (a "30 lessons before 30" series went 0 to 1M in about 4 months by that creator's own account). Soft series = no numbers, but every video feels like the same show (a "Product vs Brand" series always opens the same way).
- Don't run a daily hard series if you'll burn out.
- Create a container, not content: put the person in a setting where being themselves makes content, like kids asking a business YouTuber questions on the street, Q&A on stage, coaching calls, podcasts.

### One event, many angles

- A launch quote menu: the founder's story; the product's implications (which work changes, who benefits, why a feature matters more than it seems); a video reaction (walk through the demo, pause at interesting parts, say what surprised you and where you're skeptical); a controversial take you'll defend with evidence; competing interests (saves one person money, threatens another's business, explore the tension honestly so both sides respond).
- "Insane launch" quotes only say you're impressed. A quote needs its own reason to be read.
- A single project can create several useful pieces without lazy repurposing.

### Frameworks

**Yap anatomy**

1. Topic: what it's about
2. Angle: your take on it
3. Format: the shape (gamified, straight to camera, listicle)

**The 8 Mile script**

1. Hook
2. Name the other side's arguments yourself and knock them down
3. Give your reason

**Problem, insight, takeaway**

1. Name a problem
2. Share what most people miss
3. Give them something to do

### Examples

- **A numbered series that grew fast:** A numbered hard series, "30 lessons before 30", grew from 0 to 1M in about 4 months by that creator's own account.
  <br>*Why it works:* Numbers make people binge the next part.
- **The 4 things script:** "4 things I noticed at Coachella as a brand strategist": four mini takes in one video.
  <br>*Why it works:* Each take makes them stay for the next one.

### Common mistakes

- Posting generic tips, "my top 3 favourite" lists or motivational speeches.
- A post with no shape, which reads like a ramble.
- A launch quote that only says it's impressive.
- Starting a daily hard series you can't keep up.

### Try it

Take one topic you know well. Write it as 4 different formats from the menus in this lesson: a tier list, an 8 Mile script, a 4 things video and a problem-insight-takeaway post. Pick the one that feels most like you.

## 6.3 Short form vs long form

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Short form wins reach, long form wins depth and trust. Most creators say use both, with one short platform for reach and one long platform for depth, but they disagree on which to start with.

### What each length does

- Short form is the widest format for viewers (reach). Long form gives stickier minutes (depth). Live stream minutes are the stickiest of all.
- A formula: personal brand value = viewers x content minutes x stickiness. The "law": a minute of long-form is stickier than a minute of short-form.
- A short post that holds 10 seconds of attention is nothing compared to a video, podcast or newsletter holding 15 to 60 minutes. "Modern power belongs to those who hold the most attention in their lifetime."
- Short form isn't as potent as long form, but don't neglect its benefits.
- A 20 to 30 minute video gets people closer to who you are than a 30-second clip. That's why one business YouTuber's long videos grew the relationship, and why one creator started YouTube after 50K on Instagram.

### The modern stack and cadence

- The personal brand stack: one short-form platform for reach (Instagram or LinkedIn), one long-form platform for depth (ideally YouTube) to keep pushing contrarian beliefs, one nurture platform (ideally email or a private community). "That's all you need."
- One cadence: 7 short-form videos a week and 1 to 2 long-form a week. Short form "as high volume as possible" but within narrow topic discipline. "This is going to take hundreds and hundreds of posts."
- Another suggested cadence: YouTube once or twice a week, short form once or twice a day.
- One creator predicts that in 3 to 6 months original long and short video on X will be "obviously" the best growth strategy. He says one video brought him +1,000 followers in a day (his own claim).

### Where to start

- One view: short-form platforms (X, Instagram, LinkedIn) are easier to grow on at the start. YouTube, podcasts and Medium need search optimization and slow buildup. The claim: on YouTube people won't really care until you have at least 50 videos, same for podcasts, and podcast search "is absolute trash".
- That route: build on X where you control the traffic mechanisms, then transfer the audience to YouTube or a podcast. Practise video and speaking on the side while getting good at writing.
- Another view: start on a long-form platform (X or YouTube), then "trickle down" ideas to short form. "Pour all your water into one cup" instead of half-filling 3 or 4.
- Long form = many shorts under one theme plus a B-plot. A famous YouTuber's A-plot was 50 Airbnbs in 50 days, the B-plot was raising money for charity, and the B-plot keeps you through the series.

### Attention windows differ

- The short-form attention timer is about 4 seconds, YouTube's about 1 to 2 minutes.
- A master hook formula works in short form, long form and everywhere. The first rung of attention (stimulation, the first 1 to 2 seconds) is the same for both.
- Don't aim for full completion. Super fans finish. You are fighting for each extra 30 seconds by front-loading value.

### Measure them separately

- Judge channels separately on long-form vs short-form views.
- When you search a niche to measure long-form demand, filter out Shorts.
- Longer videos typically do better on TV (a device type insight).
- One investor and a partner agreed to announce an investment with threads, but he made a video instead. It was easy to make and did 50,000 views in 24 hours on Twitter. A Twitter Space with the founder pulled several hundred people quickly.

### Frameworks

**Modern personal brand stack**

1. Reach: one short-form platform, about 7 videos a week
2. Depth: one long-form platform, 1 to 2 a week
3. Nurture: email or a private community

**Personal brand value**

1. Viewers: how many people see you
2. Content minutes: how long they spend with you
3. Stickiness: long-form and live minutes stick more than short-form minutes

### Examples

- **A YouTuber's 50 Airbnbs series:** 50 Airbnbs in 50 days (A-plot) while raising money for charity (B-plot).
  <br>*Why it works:* Long form can be a string of shorts under one theme. The B-plot keeps people across the whole series.
- **An investment announced by video:** Instead of a thread, an investor posted a simple video announcing the investment: 50,000 views in 24 hours.
  <br>*Why it works:* Video was easy to make and stood out on a text platform.

### Common mistakes

- Judging short and long form by one combined number.
- Expecting YouTube or podcasts to work fast without a long run of episodes (the 50 videos claim).
- Spreading over 3 or 4 half-filled platforms.
- Chasing full completion instead of fighting for each extra 30 seconds.

### Where the sources disagree

One view: start with short form (X, Instagram) because it's easier to grow, then move people to long form. Another: start on a long-form platform and trickle ideas down into short form. A third: run both at once (7 shorts plus 1 to 2 long a week).

### Try it

Write down your reach platform, your depth platform and your nurture channel. For each, write the cadence you can keep for a year. Then take your best short piece and outline how it would become one long piece.

## 6.4 Scripting a video hook: four layers and the key visual

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A video hook has four layers (spoken, visual, text, audio) that must say the same thing. Viewers take in the visual first, so build the hook from the key visual, then write 2 to 4 tight lines to match it.

### The four layers and max alignment

- Every video hook has four parts: spoken hook (what you say), visual hook (what you show), text hook (words on screen), audio hook (music and sound effects).
- "The difference between 500 and 500,000 views is unlocking max alignment between those four things." Even a slightly different visual from what you say is misalignment.
- Why: comprehension. You can't hook people unless they fully understand what they see and hear. Misalignment leads to confusion, confusion to comprehension loss.
- Hooks by format: in a video the hook is the first 1 to 2 seconds: movement + text on screen + spoken hook.

### The visual, audio, visual sandwich

- The "subconscious heat map": viewers first take in the visual and text hook, then their ears catch up and hear the spoken hook, then they look back at the visual to confirm what they heard.
- The claim: eyes process 10 to 100 times more information per second than ears ("speed of light vs speed of sound").
- So the most important part of a hook is the visual, not what you say. This is called the key visual.
- Also show while you tell: a visual that confirms the topic before people hear a word. Visual hooks are called "10x more effective" (a creator's own claim).

### Writing the spoken lines

- Hook = 2 to 4 lines: a context lean (the topic), a scroll stop interjection (a contrast word like but, however, yet), and a contrarian snapback. Topic in sentence 1, contrast in the next 1 or 2.
- Cut fluff before the topic. The test: in a 13-line script where the topic first shows up on line 3, lines 1 and 2 are fluff and people swipe during them. Cut them.
- Staccato: short sentences in the hook, then medium and longer as the video goes on. Short sentences force clarity and raise value per word when time is most precious.
- Value loop for the body: context, immediate value, repeat. The first hit of value comes in or right after the hook.

### Six hook formats, chosen by your goal

- Fortune teller: present vs future ("This is the future of animation"). For news, launches and authority on where a topic is going.
- Experimenter: a demo or experiment, peer to peer ("I used to do it this way, let me show you how I do it now"). For product demos, new frameworks, B2B tool showcases, build videos.
- Teacher: a lesson, teacher to student ("Three things you can learn from Aritzia"). For authority to sell expertise.
- Investigator: a secret or research finding ("This is a secret Japanese city built at the base of Mount Fuji"). For people at the leading edge or showing deep research.
- Contrarian: say outright what you believe that differs from common wisdom. For experts in crowded spaces.
- Magician: a visual or verbal stun gun ("Check this out") that can be stacked before any of the other five.
- Any idea fits all six. A backpack example: fortune teller "This backpack is going to completely change the way millennials travel"; experimenter "I just took this backpack 7,000 miles... it has three major design flaws"; contrarian "Everybody loves this bag, but... it is so overhyped". So choose the format from the key visual.

### Illusion of novelty script order

- The order for making a boring topic feel new: the new reveal plus its outcome, then contrast with the old belief, then urgency (only if real), then the closest proof, told as a secret without hedging.
- A root canal demo: a robotic root canal with zero needles, zero pain and back to work fast, contrasted with how painful root canals used to be, then that few dentists are trained on it yet, then a patient who was back to work within the hour.
- More detail (how the process works) can be added after those core pieces.

### Frameworks

**5-step hook process from the key visual**

1. Find the visual(s) for the first 3 to 5 seconds. No good visual: ask if you should make the video at all, or manufacture one with motion graphics or stock footage
2. List the most interesting facts or angles; the one with the biggest contrast picks the hook format
3. Write the spoken hook: 2 to 4 lines, context lean, interjection, contrarian snapback
4. Decide the on-screen text that supports the first visual and the re-confirmation
5. Watch it back: visual, speech, visual. Is it clear what the video is about and does it match the rest? If not, rework

**Four hook layers**

1. Spoken: what you say
2. Visual: what you show
3. Text: words on screen
4. Audio: music and sound effects

### Examples

- **A creator's life-size floor plans video (15M views):** A split-screen stun clip, then the key visual: people walking on floor plans projected in a warehouse, text "life-size floor plans" with an arrow. Script: "Check this out. These are life-size floor plans. You can literally walk through your exact home design before you build it. I think this is the future of how people are going to design their homes."
  <br>*Why it works:* The words describe exactly what you see. The thing existing was contrast enough. The creator chose fortune teller because it was easier to explain and more shocking.
- **A creator's generative world models video (about 100K views or less):** Text "future of storytelling" over rooms appearing, spoken "It's called a generative world model... a photorealistic cinematic world."
  <br>*Why it works:* Abstract text and terms nobody knows, with no visuals showing them. The creator later said it should have gone explicit ("These rooms are the future of commercials") or dropped the idea.

### Common mistakes

- Saying one thing while showing something slightly different (misalignment).
- Fluff lines before the topic.
- Long sentences in the hook.
- Abstract words the visuals don't explain (the world models video).
- Choosing a hook format before finding the key visual.

### Try it

Pick your next video idea. First list 3 possible key visuals for the first 3 seconds. Pick the strongest, then write a 3-line spoken hook that describes exactly what is on screen, and 3 to 5 words of on-screen text. Watch it in your head: visual, speech, visual. Is the topic clear?

## 6.5 The first frame and visual hooks

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The first 1 to 2 seconds are won with what people see: a frame that looks different from the feed, clean space for short text, and just enough motion. Concrete fixes follow.

### Stimulation: the first 1 to 2 seconds

- Rung 1 of the dopamine ladder is stimulation, the same in short and long form. Like a deer noticing movement, the viewer pauses when colour, motion, brightness and contrast differ from what they usually see.
- Slight zoom-ins and unusual perspectives help (a YouTuber example: a view from inside a car that you don't normally see on the feed).
- Just enough motion: too much overwhelms and loses context, too little bores.
- A visual stun gun clip can come before the key visual (a split-screen of a girl with arms going up, then the floor plans).
- Rapid match cuts work as a magician stun (many backpacks cut fast). A hard match cut can deliver a head fake (a YouTuber cutting from falling back to being dragged while singing).
- Visual pacifiers (a beauty creator doing makeup, a guy stacking cups, split-screen GTA gameplay) hold just enough subconscious attention so the viewer focuses on the main content.

### On-screen text

- Title text of 3 to 5 words, big bold font, building the context lean. Choose words that can't be misunderstood ("future of home design" over the product name).
- Use arrows to direct the eye to the key visual.
- On-screen text can pop the question the visual alone doesn't (Citizen's "Bro, what did I just witness?").
- Text on screen of 1 to 2 lines, about 5 to 10 words, because people scroll fast and scan.
- A podcast's clips use a big text hook on screen ("THE TRUTH ABOUT ADDICTION") so viewers self-select in.
- Signature audio opener: a repeated "Check this out" is a recognisable sound pattern.

### First-frame fixes

- Remove a too-big caption and the frame instantly looks cleaner.
- Framing: leave clean space for text. In a kitchen example the person, the kitchen and clutter left no usable space. Fallback: a background box behind the text (Instagram's own text background does this).
- Colour: try options. On the coffee frame red looked "off and cheapish", an unexpected colour worked better. Small touches like dots ("...") to open the loop or an outer glow help.
- Use an AI image tool (ChatGPT) to mock up a better first frame, with a prompt like "real screenshot, nice natural lighting, kitchen, big bowl of unhealthy carbs in the bottom half, scroll-stopping, 9:16", to show what the shot should look like.
- Carousels beat single pictures, but they still need a hook.

### Check it like a viewer

- Watch your hook with no sound first. Where do your eyes go? Then watch with sound. Is visual, speech, visual fully clear?
- One view: graphics on screen as a visual hook are the least important of the extras. Environment and delivery matter more.
- Image hook: the headline on the image, one idea, readable on a phone.

### Frameworks

**First-frame checklist**

1. Safe zones: keep text away from the app UI
2. Caption size: smaller than you think
3. Framing: leave clean space, or add a text box
4. Font weight: vary it
5. Colour: test options, add small loop openers
6. Declutter: one clear subject, a window or one light

### Examples

- **A shoelace video breakdown:** A slight zoom-in from inside a car, two people hugging, text "Bro, what did I just witness?", a shoelace stuck in the door, then a hard match cut to the guy being dragged while singing.
  <br>*Why it works:* An unusual perspective stops the scroll, the text pops the question, the match cut delivers the head fake.
- **A coffee video fix:** The latte didn't stand out and the wall gave no depth. A clean shot with the coffee as the focus looked night and day better.
  <br>*Why it works:* Decluttering and one clear subject matter more than gear.

### Common mistakes

- Captions that are too big or sit under the comment box.
- Busy frames with no space for text.
- Too much motion, which loses context, or too little, which bores.
- Text that can be misunderstood, like a product name nobody knows.
- Never watching your own hook with the sound off.

### Where the sources disagree

One view: the visual is the most important part of the hook. Another: on-screen graphics are the least important of the extras, behind text, environment and delivery.

### Try it

Screenshot the first frame of your last 3 videos. For each, check: is the text inside the safe zone, is it 3 to 10 words, is there one clear subject, does it look different from the feed? Redo the worst one.

## 6.6 Short-form structures

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A short video needs a structure, not a riff. Several exist: hook, explain, illustrate, teach for education; hook, progression, climax; a 60-second story and V.I.R.A.L.; and C.A.R.T. for short stories. All of them hold the payoff back until the end.

### Hook, explain, illustrate, teach

- Hook: context, a contrarian take and an open loop (plus proof, promise, plan).
- Explain: what the video is about and why it matters to the viewer.
- Illustrate: a story, analogy or example that links the known to the unknown.
- Teach: ONE simple thing they can take away and apply. The trap is 6 to 10 steps in a short video. Many videos name a problem and never teach the behaviour change.
- Its creator says it's used in 90% of his content, grew his Instagram on this one template, and works on all platforms, not only short form. It went through "a thousand evolutions" from testing (once it was called "hook, explainer, CTA and teach", so the third step may have changed).
- Before it, they wrote a few ideas on screen and riffed. Riffing without order means people take nothing away. "We're not entertaining creators. We are educational creators." The point is to change behaviour, "results in advance".
- "Frameworks beat creativity": creativity doesn't scale, frameworks do, and you never start from a blank page.

### Hook, progression, climax

- Editing = how you present the video (cutting, pacing, captions). Structure = how you compose it (storytelling, payoffs). You need both.
- Hook: the first 5 seconds introduce the topic while leaving many questions (a snowball fight in the desert).
- Progression: meet the expectations the intro set. The creator shows the journey to get snow (the sand won't form balls, so they grab a cooler and head for the mountains). The bad version, pulling a cooler of ice from the car, would disappoint.
- Climax: the moment the topic is fulfilled (the snowball fight), with fitting music, a few jokes, editing that matches the style.
- Reveal the payoff at the end, not 5 seconds in. Example: a short on "MrBeast's secret to success" that reveals the secret at second 5, so everyone leaves.

### The 60-second story and V.I.R.A.L.

- 60-second story: start with a weird question, show constant progress toward the answer, add conflict (a B-plot), make the answer feel uncertain, then pay off quickly and concisely.
- A burger video (45M views): "is it faster to cook a burger in the drive-through line?". Adding tomatoes, lettuce and cheese lets viewers track how close the creator is. Her mom is the B-plot ("you're going to burn that car"). The burger was basically raw, it didn't matter, viewers wanted the answer.
- "If everything is smooth sailing, nobody cares." And: if it takes longer to tell your story than to make a burger, you're overcooking both.
- V.I.R.A.L., a newer framework: Visual shock (even educational videos need a picture, like a jar of golf balls, rocks and sand = do big tasks first). Immediate start (begin almost at the climax, no context). Rising action (raise the stakes: "how I make coffee" is dead, "the coffee that keeps me awake 24 hours" is alive). Anticipation (hints and puzzle pieces, never the answer early). Lasting payoff (the last word IS the answer; put any call to action BEFORE the answer, people leave the second they hear it).
- Show progress: people leave when there's no end in sight. Timers on screen, ingredients ticked off, "first, second".
- The #1 mistake is selfish content (talking about yourself, not their gain). Pick high demand plus low supply: "your video could suck, but if nobody made it and people want it, you get views."

### C.A.R.T. for 1 to 2 minute stories

- Context: who, where, when, what they want, in 2 to 3 sentences.
- Adversity: the LONGEST part. What's at stake and how it felt.

### Endings that loop

- Write the last line first, memorable enough to share alone. In short video the last line also sets up the loop back to the first line.
- Close loops at the end. Entertainment shouldn't end on an unresolved cliffhanger.
- Science check: peak-end research shows people remember the peak and the end, not the length, which fits needing one high point and a strong last line.

### Frameworks

**Hook, explain, illustrate, teach (HEIT)**

1. Hook: context, contrarian take, open loop
2. Explain: what it is and why it matters to them
3. Illustrate: story, analogy or example
4. Teach: one simple thing they can apply

**HPC**

1. Hook: topic in the first 5 seconds, with open questions
2. Progression: meet the expectations the hook set
3. Climax: deliver what they came for, at the end

**V.I.R.A.L.**

1. Visual shock
2. Immediate start
3. Rising action: raise the stakes
4. Anticipation: hints, never the answer early
5. Lasting payoff: last word is the answer, CTA before it

**C.A.R.T.**

1. Context in 2 to 3 sentences
2. Adversity, the longest part
3. Resolution
4. Takeaway: what I learned is...

### Examples

- **A drive-through burger video (45M views):** Can the creator cook a burger faster than the drive-through line? Ingredients mark progress, her mom adds conflict, the answer stays uncertain until the end: "We cooked faster than the drive-through."
  <br>*Why it works:* Question, progress, conflict, uncertain answer, fast payoff.
- **A desert snowball fight video:** The hook promises a snowball fight in the desert. The progression shows the drive to the mountains for snow. The climax is the fight.
  <br>*Why it works:* The progression earns the climax instead of skipping to it.

### Common mistakes

- Revealing the payoff in the first seconds.
- Teaching 6 to 10 steps in one short video.
- Naming a problem and never teaching the change.
- Putting the call to action after the answer.
- Selfish content about you, not the viewer's gain.
- Ending a story with "you should" or "the moral is".

### Try it

Take one tip you know well. Write it as a 45-second script using hook, explain, illustrate, teach. Check: is there only ONE thing to apply, and does the illustrate step link it to something people already know?

## 6.7 Long-form structure: openings and sections

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A long video needs a strong opening that reassures and opens a loop, then repeated sections that each make one point and bridge to the next. A look at long-form scripts and a MrBeast breakdown show how.

### The long-form opening

- Promise, proof, plan: what is this, why me, how I'll get you there.
- An expanded version: reassure (a promise that matches the title and thumbnail, proof that answers "why you?" fast, handle the "this won't work for me" objection), elevate (scale the promise with extras or a free bonus, add urgency that is time-based or tied to something new), open a loop (a road map on screen, and a named unique mechanism).
- A real script example from a business YouTuber: promise "the six phases to scale your business from 0 to million dollar in 12 months"; proof from 80-hour weeks to under 5 hours a week; objection handled with "the same six phases I took over 10,000 of my coaching clients through"; a free template; the 6 phases listed on screen, blurred; "the 10-80-10 rule" as the mechanism.
- A common pattern: a claim, a proof line in the first 15 seconds, the promise, then a numbered road map on screen ("10 principles", "7 strategies").

### Road map and the sacred timeline

- Road mapping: early on, show how many steps you'll take them through. Blur the later ones so they wonder what's coming.
- The sacred timeline (named after the show Loki): every piece has a promise. Anything that drifts from it gets cut because it confuses people, in videos and in posts.
- Science check: a car-wash card study (2006) found people with 2 of 10 stamps already given finished more often (34%) than people needing 8 from zero (19%). Showing progress keeps people going.

### Sections: point, explain, illustrate, lesson

- This is how every long-form video on that channel is built, and "the most important thing that I could teach".
- Point: what it is ("phase one, delegate your tasks"). Explain: why it matters ("most entrepreneurs hold on to things they shouldn't"). Illustrate: an example, story or analogy (the client stuck in email). Lesson: a clear next step (the 10-80-10 rule: 10% ideation together, 80% execution by them, 10% integration with feedback).
- Repeat it 2 to 6 times per video, sometimes 10 to 20. A video called "40 brutal truths I wish I knew in my 20s" uses it 40 times.
- Keep it tactical, not long drawn-out stories.
- The architect level is the same idea for teaching: one mini-loop per point (what's going on, why it matters, an example, what to do), so people always know where they are ("story navigation").
- Bridges: every section ends with "but X isn't enough, which brings us to number Y". Each point raises the question the next one answers.
- Place a freebie CTA in the middle and a "watch this video next" at the end.

### Progressive density

- Start with a broad problem everyone gets, then get denser and more tactical as it goes. The end is a "wink" to the core audience.
- Example: the meetings video ends with "instead of lots of meetings, have clear scorecards, KPIs and owners for those KPIs".

### The MrBeast intro formula

- A 3-part equation behind the 100M-subscriber island video (the analyst's claim: close to 40M views in two days): aim for perfect retention, clickbait mastery (thumbnail), video financing.
- Intro formula: open fast, establish scale, move to the first challenge. Nearly every MrBeast video uses it.
- Establishing shots set the scene and wow viewers with the size of the production: the camera starts zoomed in on Jimmy, then zooms out to the whole island; all 100 contestants stand in a line.
- Retention usually stabilises once the first challenge or storyline begins, so it starts at 17 seconds. The island tour comes after the first challenge, not before.
- After the intro: tension every few minutes, characters who repeat the rules ("the red line, that's all we're thinking about") so viewers understand, organic moments left in so it's not too polished, and an ending on a high point.
- Production feel: 1080p on purpose, never too produced.

### Frameworks

**Reassure, elevate, open a loop**

1. Reassure: promise, proof, handle objections
2. Elevate: scale the promise, add real urgency
3. Open a loop: blurred road map, a named mechanism

**Point, explain, illustrate, lesson**

1. Point: what it is
2. Explain: why it matters
3. Illustrate: example, story or analogy
4. Lesson: a clear next step
5. Bridge to the next point

**MrBeast intro formula**

1. Open fast
2. Establish scale with a wide shot
3. Start the first challenge (about 17 seconds in)
4. Explain the rest after

### Examples

- **MrBeast's island video:** Zoom out from Jimmy to the whole island, 100 contestants in a line, first challenge at 17 seconds, island tour after.
  <br>*Why it works:* Retention stabilises once the storyline starts, so it starts early.
- **"40 brutal truths":** Point, explain, illustrate, lesson, repeated 40 times in one video.
  <br>*Why it works:* A repeated section shape lets a very long video stay easy to follow.

### Common mistakes

- An opening that doesn't match the title (the grandma puzzle story).
- Touring the setting before the first storyline starts.
- Stories that drift from the promise (the sacred timeline).
- Long drawn-out stories instead of tactical sections.
- An energy graph that drops through the middle with no tension.

## 6.8 Pacing and retention

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Content is rarely too long, it's too slow. Make early points fast so people feel progress, re-hook at every transition so there is no dead air, and keep the big question open until the right moment.

### Asymmetric pacing

- Make early points faster than later points so it feels fast. It creates an "illusion of progress", like an infomercial progress bar that rushes at first then slows. People think "I'm already on point 3" and loss aversion stops them leaving.
- MrBeast's first section is always the fastest.
- Timings: point 1 about 30 seconds, point 2 about 1 minute, point 3 about 1.5 minutes, point 4 about 2 minutes. In longer videos: about 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 minutes.
- "If you can be 3 minutes in and already at point number four, that is golden for your retention."
- It works for videos, copy, emails and LinkedIn posts. Its creator calls it a unique mechanism he named himself.

### A pacing curve

- Fast for the first 15 seconds, medium until 30, then SLOW right before the reveal, because that's when people are most invested.

### Re-hooks at transitions

- Section transitions, where one loop closes, are danger zones for attention. Open a new question, complication or implication there, in the same breath.
- No dead air between loops. "Anyways, now I'm on to..." is the bad version.
- A mistake to avoid: online, don't use the school story arc with one slow build and one peak. Start at about 70 out of 100 intensity, hit the first conflict early, release, then peak again every few minutes ("re-hooking").
- In long videos, add a misdirection about every 2 to 3 minutes (the illusionist level).

### Managing the big question

- Load the big question as early as possible so the dopamine drip lasts longer.
- Before any important reveal, add context so the viewer predicts first. Peak anticipation is just before the answer.
- Viewers can only predict if they understand the facts. Rogue details or facts they can't understand confuse them and they bounce.
- Close loops at the end. Leaving the loop open leaves the viewer unsatisfied.
- Value loop in teaching videos: context, immediate value, repeat. Front-load value to win each extra 30 seconds.

### Visual pace

- MrBeast's retention is not just lots of cuts and fast talking. MrBeast's own point: talking to one camera too long without a cut is boring, cutting between angles helps.
- An analyst counted a visual change every 1.1 seconds in the opening 20 seconds of the island video.
- Cut redundant moments that lose the audience's interest.
- Staccato sentences in the hook, then medium and longer ones as the video goes on. Mix lengths so it doesn't drone: write one sentence per line and the right edge of the script should look jagged.

### Science check: attention

- One view is that attention "sped up" from time compression. The science check: there is no evidence human attention capacity has changed, and the "8-second goldfish attention span" traces back to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report citing a website with no research behind it.
- The more accurate reason: competition. People have endless options, so they leave boring content faster. Short patience for boring content, not short attention spans.

### Frameworks

**Asymmetric pacing**

1. Point 1: shortest (about 30 seconds to 1 minute)
2. Point 2: a bit longer
3. Point 3: longer again
4. Point 4 and on: the longest, once people are invested

**Re-hook**

1. Close the current loop
2. In the same breath, open a new question or complication
3. Use a connective phrase like "but here's the problem with that"

### Examples

- **MrBeast's island video:** A visual change every 1.1 seconds in the first 20 seconds, cuts between camera angles.
  <br>*Why it works:* Variety keeps the eye busy, but retention also comes from structure, not cuts alone.

### Common mistakes

- Blaming length when the real problem is slow pace.
- Equal-length sections from start to finish.
- Dead air between sections: "Anyways, now I'm on to...".
- One slow build to a single peak.
- Talking to one camera too long without a cut (MrBeast's own point).
- Blaming short attention spans instead of boring content (science check).

### Where the sources disagree

One view explains faster viewer drop-off as attention speeding up. The science check says attention capacity hasn't changed; competition has. Same advice, different reason.

### Try it

Time each section of your last long video or article. Are the early sections shorter than the later ones? Rewrite the first section to half its length, and add a re-hook line at the end of every section.

## 6.9 Scripts, outlines and script length

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Read scripts sound robotic, so film from outlines you have memorised. Whatever you write, count its length, get the hook and pacing checked, and keep the writing your own.

### Outlines, not scripts

- People sound like robots because they read scripts. Write an outline and use it as a guide.
- Call them outlines ("calling them scripts is a disservice"): notes off to the side, never read word for word.
- Follow it loosely and take tangents. "That's what people resonate with", and it's more fun.
- Trail, not rail: a train stays on the rail. A hiker can wander to the edges of the trail and always comes back to the middle. Treat your outline like a trail.
- Write it yourself. One creator writes most scripts by hand and uses AI only to widen options (for example 50 angles to choose from).
- Don't type "make me a viral video" into ChatGPT and read it off a teleprompter. Audiences can tell.

### The off the dome method

- A named method for filming without a script or teleprompter.
- 1\. Write the outline, ideally the same week so it's fresh.
- 2\. Remember it: read it before you shoot, like actors learn their lines.
- 3\. Riff: let yourself go on small tangents, that's where authentic delivery comes from.
- 4\. Repeat: if you're bad at delivering something, you just haven't said it enough times. One business YouTuber has said his points hundreds of times to his team and on stage. "No one's a natural." Your authenticity compounds.

### Recording tricks

- Pause and stay silent while you think, then cut the silences out (the "recut method").
- Re-record the intro at the END, when you're warmed up.
- "People want to hear from people at their level or one level above."
- On podcast clips: if the real moment has no punchy opening, re-record a hook afterwards in the same clothes.

### Script length

- People talk at about 3.5 words per second, so a 30-second ad is about 105 words. Count, don't guess.

### AI as a script coach, not a writer

- Don't ask AI to write for you, ask it to make you better.
- Feed it videos you find extremely engaging plus your own top performers, and teach it what makes them work, your structure and your voice.
- Then it scores scripts, flags likely drop-off points and says how to fix them: "Hook is too slow." "You start teaching too early." "Tension drops here. Consider cutting this line." "This doesn't sound like you."
- One AI split: AI for the first 10% (finding outliers) and the last 10% (reviewing, pointing out blind spots). The middle 80%, scripting, shooting and editing, is real people.
- A rhythm test for scripts: write one sentence per line. A straight right edge means monotone, a jagged edge means rhythm.

### Frameworks

**The off the dome method**

1. Write the outline the same week
2. Remember it before you shoot
3. Riff on small tangents
4. Repeat your points until delivery is natural

## 6.10 Filming: delivery, setting and simple production

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Topic, script and delivery matter far more than gear. Talk to one person, keep production simple and native, film in settings you enjoy, and learn the craft through many reps.

### Delivery: a conversation, not a presentation

- People watch alone, on a couch or at a computer. Talk to one person. Top creators say "you", never "you guys", "hey everyone" or "y'all". Group words turn it into a performance.
- Lower your tone. "Content creators think they need to MrBeast themselves", people see through it. Educators should be calm, cool and collected. Don't scream, don't rush, pause when you need to.
- Conviction beats charisma: one creator calls himself "pretty bland and super stoic" on camera and still grows because he's convinced of what he says.
- No upspeak: ending sentences going up sounds unsure. End sentences low, it sounds like authority. Slow down and let pauses happen, nerves make you "word vomit".
- Talk WITH people, not at them. Film like you're talking to one close friend (one trick: tape their photo under the camera lens).
- The gap between you talking to the camera and you talking to a friend should be tiny.

### Keep production simple

- The 20% that matters is ideation, scripting and delivery. Not editing, lighting or camera gear. "You can't out-edit a bad topic."
- One creator grew to 50K with just his iPhone, shot every video in one take and added only a caption at the top. His main edit is a road map on screen. He learned bad audio does hurt, so he bought a cheap mic.

- One creator's polished video got 47k views, a raw kitchen clip got about 100x that. Look native to the platform.

## 6.11 Editing craft and giving editors feedback

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Editing should help the viewer understand, not show off. Show what is said on screen, cut what bores, keep cuts smooth, and separate notes that must be fixed from notes that are just taste.

### Show, don't tell

- Whatever is said, show it on screen as soon as possible. The speaker says "12 AI companies", they show the founders and logos. He says "supercars", they show the supercars.
- Most people learn visually. When giving editors feedback, ask what on screen would help the viewer understand, not how many flashy graphics fit.
- Show visuals for every point. For complex points, use a visual or say it twice, the second time as a simple metaphor ("600 mph = a plane driving on the road").
- The main edit is often just a road map on screen.

### Editing techniques (from a team course)

- Cutaways: when a story mentions someone the audience lacks context on (a famous bodybuilder, "one of the greatest bodybuilders of all time"), the editor adds short cutaway moments that give context fast.
- Punch-ins on a one-camera shoot: the wide shot and the tight shot should have the eye line in the same part of the frame, so the cut isn't jarring. Experienced editors see a mismatch as an "egregious error"; juniors don't know it.
- MrBeast's approach: cutting between camera angles beats talking to one camera too long.

### Mission-critical vs subjective notes

- Mission-critical: if not fixed, nobody understands what is said, hears the story or gets the point; or it hurts the brand (a moment out of context that sounds far more intense or off-message).
- Spelling errors are objective and critical.
- Most other notes (font colour blue vs orange, framing, colour correction) are subjective.
- One creator critiques the keyframes in his editors' videos because "I care more about the outcome than I care about their feelings". Olympic gold vs silver is milliseconds.
- "Your editors don't suck... your system sucks." A B-level editor can become an AA player with the right systems and training (the creator's claim).

### Encourage the good, and the "For Future Reference" rule

- Encourage the good: tell editors what to keep doing, not only what to fix. Most of one team's frame.io comments are things they want continued, like "I love this cutaway, you did a great job with this". Editors usually only hear what to change, so on the next edit they waste energy guessing what to keep. "Be the one who reinforces the behaviors that you want to see turn into habits."
- The first action: for the next two weeks, on every piece you review, leave more comments about what you liked than about what to change. Still flag what must change: spelling errors, moments out of context that hurt the brand, redundant moments that lose interest.
- The "For Future Reference" rule: split corrective notes into "for now" and "for future reference". If a note is not mission critical, in high-volume content give it as a note for next time, with the why. Example: "One note for future reference: I love when we punch in on a shot for the eye line to match up... that way it's not as abrupt." The claim: it is the fastest way to speed up an editor's learning. It makes perfectionists uncomfortable.
- Ratio: there is no golden rule. If you always fix everything now, start at 30% for future reference and 70% for now, watch it for a month (more speed, or more spelling errors and bad colour correction?) and adjust.

### Feedback loops by volume

- High vs low volume: with about one YouTube video a month the loop is long, so 80 to 90% of notes are "fix it on this video" and 10 to 20% "for next time".
- With 14 Instagram reels a week, speed and audience learnings are worth more than taking a piece from 80% to 95 or 100%.
- Real-time check-ins on long edits: on a four-week vlog about a sensitive subject, weekly check-ins caught in week 2 that the editor was building the story around a moment the talent wouldn't want included, avoiding a full redo.
- Clarity of action steps is the one thing you control in educational content. Pair a download with a video to raise the odds people act.

### Re-editing and mashups

- Mashups as a constraint fix: when the talent can't film for a month, pull moments from past content around one theme into an inspirational mashup. Or brief: "a mashup of me talking about how to build a brand, only moments where I'm speaking on a podcast." Creative guardrails like this beat "make it go viral".
- One creator's test: a new TikTok account, first post, zero followers, a slight re-edit of a clip that already did well, 8.2M organic views (his own account).

### Frameworks

**Sorting editor notes**

1. Mission-critical: the point is lost or the brand is hurt, fix now
2. Objective: spelling errors, fix now
3. Subjective: colour, font, framing, a matter of taste
4. Match the fix-now share to your volume: low volume fixes on this video, high volume moves on

### Examples

- **A bodybuilder cutaway:** The story mentions a famous bodybuilder, so the editor cuts away to show he is one of the greatest bodybuilders of all time.
  <br>*Why it works:* Fast context so the viewer isn't lost.
- **A four-week vlog:** A weekly check-in in week 2 caught the editor building the story around a moment the talent wouldn't want shown.
  <br>*Why it works:* Check-ins on long edits prevent full redos.

### Common mistakes

- Adding flashy graphics that don't help understanding.
- Punch-ins where the eye line jumps.
- Treating taste notes as critical and slowing a high-volume team.
- Waiting until the end of a long edit to review it.
- Blaming editors when the system is the problem.

## 6.12 Posting frequency: volume, quality and batching

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Creators disagree on how often to post. One names two routes and says never pick the middle. Another argues for volume in some videos and for posting less in others. All agree on one thing: pick a pace you can keep.

### Two routes: pick one, never the middle

- Quality route: 3 to 5 posts a day, spread at least 3 hours apart. 'Timing isn't that important, but spacing is key' because posts need time to run. Every post gets decent engagement over time.
- Quantity route: up to 24 posts a day, at least 1 hour apart. Only for news accounts or very fast niches such as AI and crypto. You accept flops: 'throwing darts and hoping 1-2 a day stick'.
- The advice is to choose one of the two, not a blurry middle.

### A frequency test

- One creator tested posting from 2 a week up to 4 a day. One a day was the sweet spot. At 4 a day only half of the videos got picked up and quality dropped.
- Day of the week did not matter in that test, time of day did: post when the audience is active.
- Another creator disagrees: 'the algorithm does not care what time you post'.

### Volume negates luck

- It is a reps game. You won't build a brand off 10, 20 or 30 posts. The amount you post is directly tied to followers, and more posts a day means more chances to go viral.
- 'I'd rather have volume on my side than luck on my side because I can control volume.'
- One business YouTuber posted since 2014 (12 years, weekly on YouTube, then daily on Instagram) and became an 'overnight success' 3 years ago. One creator's view: if he posted a reel every day for 10 years and got no views, he'd quit, and he doesn't expect that.
- A mentor's lesson: you don't understand quality until you do quantity. People hide behind 'quality' and post once every 6 months.
- Top of mind is the ultimate top of funnel. Showing up daily in even 10 to 1,000 feeds makes people want to help you, text you and DM you, and it brings opportunities. A plumber friend who texts you every day for a year is the one you call.

### Just post less

- In other videos the same creator says every second or third day is fine. One great piece beats 10 average ones, and quality beats 10 to 20 weak videos a day.
- 10 rushed YouTube videos don't get distribution. One great one gets pushed for a year or two. That business YouTuber has videos still getting views years later because they focused on quality.
- That creator's own pace: one reel every day 'no matter what' since Nov 22, 2025, never missed a day, all shot on iPhone to remove every excuse. The suggested cadence: once or twice a week for YouTube, once or twice a day for short form. 'You don't want to have your Oprah moment too early.'
- The fair reading of both: pick a pace you can keep for years and make each piece as good as you can at that pace. 'Volume doesn't need to happen in a week. It could happen over a decade.'

### The quality-quantity accordion

- First get quality to 7 out of 10. Then raise volume to the platform's normal. The numbers: short video 1 a day, YouTube 1 a week, short text about 5 posts a day. Then push quality to 9 out of 10.
- After that: 6 months of consistency, answer every comment and move people to email.

### Batching

- Post daily, shoot weekly. Set one content day so you don't burn out.
- But never batch more than a week or two ahead: 'batching content just batches your mistakes'. You should learn from every shoot.
- If you're excited about an idea, make it now. Excitement fades by shoot day. 'Reduce the time between idea and action.'

### Three operating models

- Sniper (MrBeast): heavy focus on quality and picking the right videos, sometimes weeks or months between uploads.
- Machine gun (Penguinz0): uploads every day, some hit big, some don't.
- Weekly operator (Forrest Galante): about one video every week for two years, improving steadily and building a catalogue.
- At the start, high frequency makes sense just to get reps and learn the craft. Later you choose the model that fits you, and commit.

### Frameworks

**The two routes**

1. Quality: 3 to 5 posts a day, at least 3 hours apart
2. Quantity: up to 24 posts a day, at least 1 hour apart, only for news or fast niches
3. Pick one, never the middle

**Quality-quantity accordion**

1. Get quality to 7 out of 10
2. Raise volume to the platform's normal
3. Push quality to 9 out of 10
4. Stay consistent for 6 months, answer every comment, move people to email

### Common mistakes

- Posting the same hook and caption on every app.
- Judging a cross-post by views only.
- Assuming YouTube cut into shorts still works the same.

### Where the sources disagree

## 6.15 AI in video production and editing

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** AI works as an editor and a production tool: you train it on your style, give it raw footage, approve a plan, then it edits. For AI video, learn the pieces one by one and keep characters and products locked.

### An AI editor workflow

- Train it on your editing style: give finished videos, ask it to break down how they're edited and how you tell stories, and go back and forth until it explains your style back in plain English.
- Upload raw footage (even a 10-minute take) and it finds the best takes.
- Get an editing plan: structure, cuts, B-roll placement, even photos from your drive.
- Approve or give feedback before editing starts (human in the loop).
- Kick off the edit (cuts, graphics, music) in CapCut, DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro or its own cloud machine. Review, refine, then post.
- One example: three videos fully produced by the bot (a Minecraft-style explainer, a 'what if' natural disaster video, a talking-head ad). The animated ones started from a one-sentence prompt (storyboard, script, narration, music).
- One creator gave it 10 raw files at once, all edited in about two hours, versus about two hours per video by hand. 'AI editing doesn't just make editing faster. It makes production parallel.'

### Learning AI video production

- A roadmap: learn in order. Images first, then voices, video generation, scripts, then assembly.
- Learn to spot what feels off: a washed-out starting image, a voice that sounds like a studio ad, a script nobody would say out loud. Fix each individually before combining.
- Generate footage with options for the edit: take a small section of the story, turn it into a montage prompt, pull the strongest moments. This covers prompt structure, character references, continuity and sound. Keep references tight versus give the model freedom for interesting coverage.
- For an ad: develop the look in stills, build reference sheets for anything that repeats (even the car), generate coverage, find the film in the edit. The final piece must hold together after the novelty of AI wears off.

### AI characters and ads

- UGC characters: describing a person in words gives a different face every time, so make a character sheet first (close-up and full-body) and use those images for every clip.
- The person can hold the product but never change it, or you get a second bottle or a sixth finger. Keep clip prompts short.
- Long-form ad: lock characters, products and places before making anything. Make it scene by scene timed to the song.
- AI replicas (a creator's claim): analyze creators' body movements with AI to make realistic AI UGC with the same hook and structure as a proven video, 10x cheaper than a creator.
- 'AI slop stories' (a creator's claim): short fully-AI stories about the problem the app solves. People call it slop but it gets views and converts.

### Frameworks

**AI editor workflow**

1. Train it on your editing style
2. Upload raw footage
3. Get an editing plan
4. Approve or give feedback
5. Kick off the edit
6. Review, refine, post

**AI video learning order**

1. Images
2. Voices
3. Video generation
4. Scripts
5. Assembly

### Examples

- **10 raw files at once:** A creator gave the AI editor 10 raw files at once. All were edited in about two hours, versus about two hours per video by hand.
  <br>*Why it works:* The claim: AI makes production parallel.

### Common mistakes

- Describing a character in words each time and getting a new face.
- Letting the model change the product in the clip.
- Combining images, voice and script before fixing each one.
- Skipping the approval step on the editing plan.

### Where the sources disagree

One view: AI slop stories and AI replicas get views and convert, at a fraction of creator cost. Another: the final piece must hold together after the novelty of AI wears off. Both are claims.

### Try it

Pick one finished video. Ask an AI to describe how it is edited, and refine the description until it matches your style in plain English.

## 6.16 Satisfaction: deliver what the packaging promised

*6 min read*

> **The big idea:** Viewer satisfaction is the most underrated part of YouTube strategy: YouTube measures the value per minute a video gives, and a great title can't save a video that doesn't deliver.

### What satisfaction is

- Viewer satisfaction is YouTube's way of measuring the value per minute a video provides, and YouTube itself says it's the thing to focus on.
- It gets overlooked because there's no metric for it in your dashboard, and people optimise for what they can see.

### Three things to do on every video

- Deliver on the promise in the intro: the title and thumbnail make a promise, and the quickest way to lose satisfaction is to not deliver it. Aim for visual or verbal delivery within the first 5 seconds.
- Obsess over the details: viewers notice a glitchy edit, a typo in a caption, a spike in audio, and they judge a video fast.
- Invest in things that don't scale: working for MrBeast taught the value of over-delivering. A 10-second moment could take 40 hours to film and edit, and those are the moments people remember.

### The 8k-view lesson

- A YouTube strategist tweeted about how good one video's title and thumbnail were, then watched it: sloppy captions with typos and mismatched words, no delivery on the promise, no structure or story.
- It shot up to 8k views on day one, then died. The bet: if it had delivered, it would have passed 100k. YouTube gave it a real chance and it didn't deliver.

### The TV play (2026)

- TV has become one team's clients' biggest device type, by a wide margin.
- What the team found: the best length for TV viewing is over 20 minutes, slower pace with deeper storytelling is paying off, and production value matters more because people don't tolerate on TV what they would on a phone (video quality, sound, lighting).
- This is about long-form on TV. Short-form on phones still lives on fast change (M6 pacing lessons).

### Frameworks

**Satisfaction checklist**

1. Deliver the promise in the first 5 seconds
2. Fix every typo, glitch and audio spike
3. Add one over-delivered moment that doesn't scale

### Examples

- **Great packaging, no delivery:** 8k views on day one, then dead. Typos in captions, promise not delivered, no story.
  <br>*Why it works:* The bet: it would have passed 100k if it had delivered.

### Common mistakes

- Making a promise in the title the video doesn't keep early
- Leaving caption typos and audio spikes
- Ignoring satisfaction because there's no number for it

### Where the sources disagree

The TV advice (slower, deeper, longer) pulls against fast-pacing advice elsewhere in M6. Both can be true: they describe different screens and lengths.

### Try it

Watch the first 5 seconds of your last video or read the first 3 lines of your last post. Did they deliver what the title or hook promised? Rewrite them so they do.

*Answer: b) YouTube's way of measuring the value per minute a video provides.* It has no dashboard number, which is why it gets overlooked.

**2. When should you deliver on the packaging's promise?**

## Cheat sheet: Formats and video

The whole module on one page.

- Pick the platform's hero format and stay on it until the system runs 30 days.
- Run at most about 3 repeatable formats and change the input, not the structure.
- Study winners in your own format, not your niche.
- If a style flops twice, kill it for now.
- Align the four hook layers: spoken, visual, text, audio.
- Find the key visual first, then write the hook.
- Hook = 2 to 4 lines with the topic in sentence 1.
- Teach one thing in a short video.
- Reveal the payoff at the end.
- Open long videos with promise, proof, plan in 30 seconds.
- Make early points faster than later ones.
- Re-hook at every section transition.
- Use an outline, not a script, and film like talking to one friend.
- Sort editor notes: mission-critical now, subjective later.
- Show on screen what you say.
- Pick a posting pace you can keep for years.
- Don't batch more than 1 to 2 weeks ahead.
- Give each platform its own hook and caption and track stay past the first second.
- Review each batch of 10 by follows gained.
- With AI editors keep a human approval step.
