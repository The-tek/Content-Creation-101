# Module 6: Formats and video

Pick a few formats you can keep making, build the hook around what people see first, keep production simple, and post at a pace you can keep

This module covers which formats to choose, how short and long videos differ, and how to build hooks, structure and pacing. It also covers filming, editing, how often to post, reusing one idea on many platforms and using AI. Creators disagree on some points, and the lessons show both sides

## Lesson 6.1: Choosing your formats

### Big idea

A format is a shape you repeat in your content, like a talking-head video (you talk to the camera) giving one tip, or a list post with numbered points. Pick a few formats that fit your platform, that you can keep making and that you enjoy, then stick with them

### How it works

#### What a format is and why to use few

Say you post a video where you give one tip to the camera, again and again, with a new topic each time. That video shape is a format

Pick at most about 3 formats and keep them for as long as they work. Change only the topic. People then know what to expect, and they can tell a friend what you do

#### Fit the platform

Every platform has its own style, and it shows more of the content that fits that style. Instagram, TikTok, Facebook and Snapchat favour short videos. YouTube favours videos of 10 to 30 minutes. X, Threads and LinkedIn favour short text

A simple way to choose: find the platform where the people who would buy from you spend the most time, then use the format that platform pushes hardest. X is a good pick if you want to reach developers, startup founders or people who buy tech

#### The 4 boxes

You can post video or writing, short or long. That gives 4 boxes, and each does a different job:

- long video builds deep trust
- short video gets many new people to see you
- long writing (articles, newsletters) shows you know your topic
- short writing (posts) is a cheap way to test ideas fast, and the winners can become articles or long videos

A healthy mix covers all 4 boxes. Writing is wider than it sounds: it includes captions, images with text and video scripts, so you can grow without showing your face

#### Pick formats you can keep up

You will make each format hundreds of times, so choose ones you enjoy and are good at. If you dislike talking to a camera, an interview format with someone asking you questions may suit you better. A format that works for someone else often fails for you if you hate making it

Content pillars help you organise this. A pillar is a topic you come back to again and again (3 to 5 in total). Give each pillar one or two formats and one job: bring new people in, or give followers a reason to trust you

Say you are a filmmaker who uses AI. Finished films could bring attention, and breakdowns of how you made them could show clients how you think

#### Study, test and drop

Study only winners in the same format as yours. A productivity creator can learn from a fitness video that uses the same format, because a format works on any topic

The fastest shortcut is to copy a format that is getting lots of views right now and put your own topic in it. Do not copy the content

The drop rule: if you post a format twice and it flops twice, drop it for now. Check your analytics (the numbers for each post) to see it

### Steps

Content pillars:

1. Pick 3 to 5 topics you will keep returning to
2. Pair each with 1 or 2 formats
3. Give each a job: attract new people, or build trust
4. Plan several posts per pillar each month
5. Review whether each pillar does its job, then improve or replace it

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say test many formats at once. Others say stay on one platform and one format until your system has run 30 days in a row without breaking

### Mistakes

- filming in a format you hate because it works for someone else
- spreading over many platforms and formats before one system runs smoothly
- studying winners in a different format than yours
- keeping a format that has flopped twice in a row

### Try it

List every format you posted in the last month. For each one, mark whether you enjoy it, whether it suits the platform, and whether it flopped twice. Keep at most 3 that you enjoy and that suit the platform

## Lesson 6.2: Ready-made formats to borrow

### Big idea

You never have to start from a blank page. Pick a topic, pick a known shape, and combine them

### How it works

#### Topic, angle and format

Every talk video has three parts: a topic (what it is about), an angle (your take on it) and a format (the shape). Mix and match them and you will not run out of ideas

Say your topic is sleep, your angle is "naps are overrated" and your format is "overrated vs underrated". That is a video

#### Short-video formats to borrow

These are easy to copy with your own topic:

- red flag vs green flag
- tier list (sort things from best to worst) or top 10 ranking
- this or that
- bad, good, great
- don't vs do, or overrated vs underrated
- listicle (a list video), screen share, reaction

Game-like formats such as rankings and tier lists are popular because viewers like to argue with the ranking

#### 5 ways to talk on camera

These five cover most good talking videos:

1. A strong take: say "X is the best" or "X is the worst", then defend it. Strong opinions get replies
2. A strong take that turns into teaching: the take is the hook, then you teach something useful
3. A small thing you noticed: "have you noticed how..."
4. Humor
5. Story time

Skip "my top 3 favourite...", tips everyone already knows and motivational speeches. They are everywhere and give people no reason to stop

#### Two scripts to copy

The 8 Mile script: open with a hook, say the other side's arguments yourself and show why they are wrong, then give your reason. It works because it answers people's doubts while they are still thinking them

The 4 things script: four small takes in one video, like "4 things I noticed at a music festival as a brand strategist". Each take gives people a reason to stay for the next one

#### Written post shapes

Every post needs a shape, or even a great idea reads like random thoughts. Six shapes are used the most:

- problem, what people miss, what to do
- the list: a hook, numbered points and a closer. Easy to save and share
- the graphic: one point in one easy picture
- video
- article
- quote tweet (you share someone's post with your own comment)

#### Series and one event, many angles

Someone usually needs a few minutes of your content before they decide to follow you. A series helps, because people keep watching. A numbered series ("lesson 1 of 30") makes people want the next part. A series with no numbers works too if every video opens the same way

Do not start a daily numbered series if you will get too tired to finish it. A series you drop halfway is worse than none

One event, like a product launch, can give several posts. Say a company launches a new AI tool. You could write the founder's story, what the product changes and for whom, or an opinion many people may disagree with. A post that only says "insane launch" is not enough. Each post needs its own reason to be read

### Steps

Problem, what people miss, what to do:

1. Name a problem
2. Share what most people miss about it
3. Give them something to do

### Mistakes

- posting generic tips, "my top 3 favourite" lists or motivational speeches
- writing a post with no shape, so it reads like a ramble
- writing a launch post that only says it is impressive
- starting a daily numbered series you can't keep up

### Try it

Take one topic you know well and write it in 3 formats from this lesson: a tier list, a 4 things video and a problem, what people miss, what to do post. Keep the one that feels most like you

## Lesson 6.3: Short videos vs long videos

### Big idea

Short videos reach the most people. Long videos build a deeper bond and more trust. Most creators say use both, but they disagree on which to start with

### How it works

#### What each length does

Short videos reach the widest audience. They are the best way to get new people to find you

Long videos hold people for longer, and those minutes build a stronger bond. A 20 to 30 minute video shows people who you are far better than a 30-second clip. Live streams build the strongest bond of all

So the value of your personal brand grows with three things: how many people see you, how many minutes they spend with your content, and how strong the bond in those minutes is

#### A simple setup

A simple personal brand setup has three parts:

- one short-video platform for reach (getting seen by lots of new people), like Instagram or LinkedIn
- one long-video platform to go deeper, ideally YouTube
- one place to keep in touch with people, ideally email or a private community

One suggested pace is 7 short videos a week and 1 to 2 long videos a week. Stay on a narrow set of topics. It will take hundreds of posts

#### Where to start

One view: start on a short-video platform like X, Instagram or LinkedIn, because they are easier to grow on at the beginning. YouTube and podcasts need people to find you through search, so they grow slowly. Once you have an audience, move people over to YouTube

Another view: start on one long-video platform, then turn those ideas into short posts. Put all your effort into one platform instead of spreading it over three or four

Both agree on one thing: do not spread yourself over many platforms at the start

#### How long people give you

On short-video platforms, people decide in about 4 seconds whether to stay. On YouTube, they give you about 1 to 2 minutes

The same hook idea works for both: grab attention in the first 1 to 2 seconds. Not everyone will watch to the end, only your biggest fans. Put the value early so people stay for each extra 30 seconds

#### Measure them separately

Judge short and long videos as two different numbers. A short video that gets 50,000 views and a long video that gets 2,000 are doing different jobs

### Steps

Simple personal brand setup:

1. Reach: one short-video platform, about 7 videos a week
2. Depth: one long-video platform, 1 to 2 videos a week
3. Keep in touch: email or a private community

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say start with short videos because they are easier to grow, then move people to long videos. Others say start on one long-video platform and turn those ideas into short posts. A third group runs both at once

### Mistakes

- judging short and long videos by one combined number
- expecting YouTube or podcasts to work fast, before you have many videos or episodes
- spreading your effort over 3 or 4 platforms
- trying to get everyone to watch to the end

### Try it

Write down your reach platform, your depth platform and the place where you keep in touch with people. For each, write the pace you could keep for a year

## Lesson 6.4: Writing a video hook: four layers and the key visual

### Big idea

A video hook (the start of the video that makes people stay) has four layers: what you say, what you show, the words on screen and the sound. All four have to say the same thing. Viewers see the picture first, so start with the key visual, then write 2 to 4 short lines to match it

### How it works

#### The four layers

Every video hook has four parts:

- spoken: what you say
- visual: what you show
- text: the words on screen
- audio: the music and sound effects

All four must say the same thing. If the picture shows something slightly different from your words, people get confused, and confused people leave

#### Start with the key visual

Most people take in a video's first seconds in this order: they see the picture and text, then they hear your words, then they look back at the picture to check that it matches

So what viewers see first is more important than what you say. The key visual is the main thing people see in the first 3 seconds. Show the topic before you say a word

#### Writing the spoken lines

A hook is 2 to 4 short lines. Put the topic in the first sentence. Then add a contrast word like "but", "however" or "yet", and a line that goes against what people expect

Cut anything before the topic. Say your topic first appears on line 3 of the script. Then lines 1 and 2 are filler, and people swipe away during them. Delete them

Short sentences keep you clear, and every word counts for more when time is short

#### Six hook styles

Pick the style that matches your key visual:

- fortune teller: compare now with the future, like "this is the future of animation"
- experimenter: show a demo, like "i used to do it this way, let me show you how i do it now"
- teacher: teach a lesson, like "three things you can learn from this store"
- investigator: reveal a secret or a research finding
- contrarian: say something that goes against what most people think
- magician: a visual or spoken shock like "check this out", which can go in front of any other style

#### Making a boring topic feel new

Use this order: the new thing and what it does for you, then a contrast with the old belief, then urgency (a reason to act now, only if it is real), then the closest proof

Say your topic is root canals. You would open with a root canal with no needles and no pain, then contrast it with how painful they used to be, then say few dentists are trained on it yet, then mention a patient who was back at work within the hour

### Steps

5 steps to a hook, starting from the key visual:

1. Find the visual for the first 3 to 5 seconds. If you have no good one, ask whether you should make the video at all
2. List the most interesting facts or angles. The one with the biggest contrast picks your hook style
3. Write the spoken hook: 2 to 4 lines with the topic, a contrast word, then the line that goes against what people expect
4. Decide the on-screen text that supports the first visual
5. Watch it back as a viewer would: picture, speech, picture. If it is not clear what the video is about, rework it

### Examples

Words that match the picture:

Say the video shows people walking on floor plans drawn on a warehouse floor. The text says "life-size floor plans". The creator says: "Check this out. These are life-size floor plans. You can walk through your home design before you build it"

Why it works: The words describe exactly what you see, so nobody is confused

### Mistakes

- saying one thing while showing something slightly different
- filler lines before the topic
- long sentences in the hook
- using abstract words the picture does not explain
- choosing a hook style before finding the key visual

### Try it

Pick your next video idea. List 3 possible key visuals for the first 3 seconds and pick the strongest. Write a 3-line spoken hook that describes exactly what is on screen, plus 3 to 5 words of on-screen text

## Lesson 6.5: The first frame and visual hooks

### Big idea

The first 1 to 2 seconds are won by what people see. The first frame (the first picture of the video) should look different from the rest of the feed, leave clean space for a short line of text, and have just enough motion

### How it works

#### Grab attention

A viewer pauses when the colour, motion, brightness or contrast is different from what they usually see in their feed. Slight zoom-ins and unusual angles help, like a view from inside a car

Use just enough motion. Too much overwhelms people, and too little bores them

A short, surprising clip can come before your key visual, or quick cuts between similar things. A calm clip in the background, like someone stacking cups, holds a little attention so people can focus on your main point

#### On-screen text

Use a title of 3 to 5 words in a big, bold font. Choose words nobody can misunderstand: "future of home design" works better than a product name nobody knows

Add an arrow to point the eye at the key visual. Keep any other text to 1 or 2 lines, about 5 to 10 words, because people scroll fast and only scan

Text can ask the question the picture does not, like "what did i just witness?", which makes people want to know

#### First-frame fixes

- make captions smaller than you think. A big caption makes the frame look messy
- leave clean space for text. If you can't, put a box behind the text
- try an unexpected colour. On a coffee video, red text looked cheap
- keep one clear subject and one light source

You can also ask an AI image tool to draw a rough picture of the shot you want, so you see what to film

#### Check it like a viewer

Watch your hook with the sound off first and notice where your eyes go. Then watch with sound and check that picture, speech, picture is completely clear

For image posts, put the headline on the image, keep it to one idea and make sure it is readable on a phone

### Examples

A first-frame fix:

Say your coffee video starts with a latte on a messy table, and the wall behind it gives no depth. You redo it as a clean shot with the coffee as the only focus

Why it works: A clean frame with one clear subject does more than expensive gear

### Mistakes

- captions that are too big or sit under the comment box
- busy frames with no space for text
- too much motion, which loses people, or too little, which bores them
- never watching your own hook with the sound off

### Try it

Screenshot the first frame of your last 3 videos. For each, check: is the text 3 to 10 words, is there one clear subject, and does it look different from the rest of the feed? Redo the worst one

## Lesson 6.6: Short video structures

### Big idea

A short video needs a structure. Making it up as you go means people take nothing away. Proven structures hold the payoff (the thing the viewer came for) until the end

### How it works

#### Why use a structure

If you riff (make it up as you go), people take nothing away. A framework is a set of steps you reuse. A framework can be used again and again, so you never start from a blank page

#### For teaching: hook, explain, illustrate, teach

Use these four steps for a video that teaches:

1. Hook: set the context, say something that goes against what people expect, and open a loop (a question the viewer wants answered)
2. Explain: say what the video is about and why the viewer should care
3. Illustrate: tell a story, analogy (a comparison) or example that links something they know to something new
4. Teach: give ONE simple thing they can use

The trap is cramming 6 to 10 steps into a short video, or naming a problem and never saying what to do differently

#### For stories: hook, progression, climax

Hook: in the first 5 seconds, say the topic and leave questions open. Say the video is about a snowball fight in the desert

Progression: show the journey. The sand will not form balls, so you grab a cooler and drive to the mountains for snow. Climax: the snowball fight, the moment you promised

Do not give the payoff away early. A video that reveals its secret in second 5 gives people a reason to leave

#### For stories: show progress and keep the answer uncertain

People leave when they can't see an end. Show progress with a timer on screen, crossed-off steps, or "first, second". Add some conflict, and keep the answer uncertain until the end

If everything goes smoothly, nobody cares. Put any CTA (asking people to follow or buy) before the answer, because people leave the second they get it

#### For 1 to 2 minute stories: C.A.R.T

1. Context: who, where, when and what they want, in 2 to 3 sentences
2. Adversity (the problem or struggle): the longest part, with what was at stake and how it felt
3. Resolution: how it ended
4. Takeaway: the lesson, as "what i learned is..."

Do not end with "you should" or "the moral is"

#### End with a loop

Write the last line first, and make it memorable enough to share on its own. In a short video it can also lead back to the first line, so people watch again

Close every question you opened. People remember the best moment and the ending most, so aim for one high point and a strong last line

### Steps

Hook, explain, illustrate, teach:

1. Hook: context, a surprising take, an open loop
2. Explain: what it is and why they should care
3. Illustrate: a story, analogy or example
4. Teach: one simple thing they can use

Hook, progression, climax:

1. Hook: the topic in the first 5 seconds, with open questions
2. Progression: meet the expectations the hook set up
3. Climax: deliver what they came for, at the end

### Mistakes

- revealing the payoff in the first seconds
- teaching 6 to 10 steps in one short video
- naming a problem and never teaching the change
- putting the CTA after the answer
- making content about yourself and not about what the viewer gains

### Try it

Take one tip you know well and write it as a 45-second script using hook, explain, illustrate, teach. Check that there is only ONE thing to use, and that the illustrate step links it to something people already know

## Lesson 6.7: Long video structure: openings and sections

### Big idea

A long video needs a strong opening that reassures people and opens a loop (makes them want to know what comes next), then repeated sections that each make one point and lead into the next

### How it works

#### The opening

The opening answers three questions: what is this (the promise), why should I trust you (proof), and how will you get me there (the plan)

The promise must match the title and thumbnail. Give proof fast, and answer the doubt "this won't work for me"

#### The road map

Early on, show how many steps you will take people through, like "6 phases" or "10 principles". Blur the later ones so viewers wonder what is coming

Every video makes a promise in its title and opening. Cut anything that drifts away from it, because it confuses people

#### Sections: point, explain, illustrate, lesson

Build the middle from repeated sections. Each one has four steps:

1. Point: say what it is, like "step one, delegate your tasks (hand them to someone else)"
2. Explain: say why it is important
3. Illustrate: give an example, story or analogy
4. Lesson: give a clear next step

Repeat this 2 to 6 times per video, or more in a long list video. Keep each section practical and short, so people always know where they are

#### Bridges between sections

Link sections with short lines that join them, like "but that is not enough, which brings us to number 3". Each point should raise a question that the next one answers

Start with a broad problem everyone has, then get more specific and practical as the video goes on

#### Open fast

Open fast, show the scale of what is going on, then start the first challenge or storyline. People stop leaving once a storyline starts, so start it early. Show the setting after that, not before

### Steps

Point, explain, illustrate, lesson:

1. Point: what it is
2. Explain: why it is important
3. Illustrate: an example, story or analogy
4. Lesson: a clear next step
5. Bridge to the next point

### Mistakes

- an opening that does not match the title, like a long unrelated story
- touring the setting before the first storyline starts
- stories that drift away from the promise
- long, drawn-out stories instead of practical sections

### Try it

Outline a 10-minute video. Write the first 30 seconds as promise, proof, answer to the doubt and road map. Then write 4 sections as point, explain, illustrate, lesson, each ending with a bridge line to the next

## Lesson 6.8: Pacing and keeping people watching

### Big idea

People leave a video because it feels slow, and length alone is not the reason. Make early points fast, open a new question every time you change topic, and keep the big question open until the right moment

### How it works

#### Make early points faster than later ones

Make your early points shorter than your later ones. It gives a feeling of progress: viewers think "i'm already on point 3" and stay, because they don't want to waste the time they have put in

Say point 1 takes about 30 seconds, point 2 about 1 minute, point 3 about 1.5 minutes. In longer videos the steps are about 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 minutes

This works for videos, emails and posts

#### Open a new question at every change

The moment between two sections, when one question has just been answered, is when people are most likely to leave. Open a new question or problem right away

Avoid dead air (a quiet gap where nothing happens), like "anyways, now i'm on to...". Say "but here is the problem with that" instead

A video that builds slowly to one big peak loses people. Start fairly strong, hit the first conflict early, let the tension drop, then peak again every few minutes

This is called re-hooking

#### Handle the big question

Ask the big question as early as you can, so the wait for the answer lasts longer

Before any big reveal (the moment you show the answer), give enough context that the viewer can guess. Excited waiting is highest just before the answer

Then close every question you opened, or the viewer feels unsatisfied

#### Visual pace

Talking to one camera for too long without a cut is boring. Cut between angles, and cut moments that repeat themselves or lose people

In the hook, use short sentences, then medium and longer ones as the video goes on. Mixing lengths keeps your voice from sounding flat

#### Is attention really shrinking?

There is no evidence that human attention has got shorter. The real reason people leave is competition: they have endless options, so they leave boring content faster

The advice is the same either way: don't be boring

### Steps

Re-hook:

1. Close the current question
2. In the same breath, open a new question or problem
3. Use a bridge phrase like "but here is the problem with that"

### Mistakes

- blaming length when the video is just slow
- making every section the same length
- leaving dead air between sections
- building slowly to one single peak
- talking to one camera too long without a cut

### Try it

Time each section of your last long video or article. Are the early sections shorter than the later ones?

Rewrite the first section to half its length, and add a line at the end of every section that opens a new question

## Lesson 6.9: Scripts, outlines and script length

### Big idea

Read-aloud scripts sound robotic, so film from an outline you have memorised. Count your script length, and keep the writing your own

### How it works

#### Outlines, not scripts

People sound like robots when they read a script word for word. Write an outline (a short list of points) and use it as a guide

Follow it loosely and go off on small tangents (side thoughts). That is what makes people connect with you, and it is more fun to film. Think of the outline as a hiking trail: you can wander to the edges, and you always come back to the middle

Write it yourself. AI can give you more options, like 50 angles to choose from, but if you read an AI-written video off a teleprompter (a screen that shows your words while you film), people can tell

#### Filming without a script

1. Write the outline, ideally the same week you film so it is fresh
2. Remember it. Read it before you shoot, like actors learn their lines
3. Riff (talk freely) and let yourself go on small tangents. That is where natural delivery comes from
4. Repeat. If you are bad at delivering something, you have not said it enough times

#### Recording tricks

Pause and stay silent while you think, then cut the silences out in editing. Re-record your intro at the end, when you are warmed up

#### Script length

People talk at about 3.5 words per second, so a 30-second video is about 105 words. Count your words, don't guess

#### Use AI as a script coach

Write the script yourself and ask AI to make you better. Give it videos that keep you watching plus your own best videos, and teach it what makes them work

Then it can flag where people are likely to leave. For example: "hook is too slow", "you start teaching too early" or "this doesn't sound like you"

### Mistakes

- reading a script word for word and sounding like a robot
- reading an AI-written script off a teleprompter
- guessing script length instead of counting words
- recording the intro first, while still cold

### Try it

Turn your next video into a 5-line outline, one line per point. Read it twice, then film it without looking. Film the intro again at the end

## Lesson 6.10: Filming: delivery, setting and simple production

### Big idea

Your topic, script and delivery are far more important than gear. Talk to one person, keep production simple, film in settings you enjoy, and learn by making many videos

### How it works

#### Delivery: talk like in a conversation

People watch alone, on a couch or at a computer. So talk to one person: say "you", not "you guys" or "hey everyone"

Group words turn it into a performance

Speak lower and calmer. Loud, hyped acting does not suit an educator

Don't rush, and pause when you need to. Believing what you say works better than being charismatic

Avoid upspeak (ending sentences on a rising tone), because it sounds unsure. End sentences on a low note

One trick is to film as if you are talking to a close friend, and tape their photo under the camera lens

#### Keep production simple

What counts most is the idea, the script and your delivery. You can't fix a bad topic with editing, lighting or gear

Content that looks like it belongs on the platform earns trust. A raw clip filmed in your kitchen can do better than a polished video, because people want the real you and the behind-the-scenes (what happens off camera)

Audio is the one basic worth fixing. Bad sound hurts, so a cheap microphone is a good first buy

#### Setting and a look people recognise

Test different settings, like your car, office, home or desk, and see which one does better

A look people link to you has four parts: the look, the set, how you frame the shot, and how you edit. If you can't edit, build one great set once and reuse it

Repeating a filming style, a way of speaking, even a shirt helps people recognise you

#### Make filming something you can keep doing

Plan filming so it adds to your life. Pick places you enjoy, and film in blocks

A team with a clear plan and a clear job for each person can shoot many pieces in a few days

When someone else is on camera, ask them questions to lead them, instead of giving them lines. A good 3-step question for clips: where are you today, where do you want to be, and what is stopping you?

#### Learn by doing it many times

Early videos look worse for every creator, so get your first 100 done fast. Build the basics first: editing, thumbnails, storytelling, camera and simple lighting

If you lack a skill, have someone on your team who covers it

Expect to stumble and stutter. Post it anyway

Faceless content (no person on camera) can get views, but it rarely builds a strong bond, because there is no personality to like

### Steps

A 3-step question for clips:

1. Where are you today?
2. Where do you want to be?
3. What is stopping you?

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say to build basics like editing and thumbnails early. Others say editing, lighting and gear are outside the 20% that counts most, and point to one-take phone videos that grew an account

### Mistakes

- acting loud and hyped when you are an educator
- saying "hey everyone" or "you guys" instead of "you"
- ending sentences with upspeak and rushing
- spending on gear and polish instead of topic and delivery
- waiting for videos to be perfect

### Try it

Film the same 30-second tip three times: once in your usual spot, once in a new setting (car, kitchen, desk), and once while picturing one specific friend behind the lens. Watch them back and pick the one that sounds most like a conversation

## Lesson 6.11: Editing and giving editors feedback

### Big idea

Good editing helps the viewer understand. Show on screen what you say, keep cuts smooth, and sort notes into ones that must be fixed and ones that are just taste

### How it works

#### Show, don't tell

Whatever is said, show it on screen as soon as you can. If the speaker says "12 AI companies", show the founders and logos

When you give an editor feedback, ask what on screen would help the viewer understand. Flashy graphics for their own sake don't help

Often the main edit is just a road map on screen

#### Two common edits

A cutaway is a short clip that cuts away from the speaker. Say a story mentions a famous bodybuilder and the audience does not know who he is

A cutaway shows he is one of the greatest of all time, so nobody is lost

A punch-in is a zoom into the shot. On a one-camera shoot it fakes a second camera angle

The wide shot and the tight shot should have the speaker's eyes in the same part of the frame, so the cut is not harsh

#### Must-fix notes vs taste notes

A note is a must-fix if, without fixing it, nobody understands what is said or gets the point. It is also a must-fix if it hurts your brand, like a moment taken out of context that sounds off-message

Spelling errors are clearly wrong too

Most other notes, like whether a font is blue or orange, framing or colour correction, are taste. Fix those only if you have time

#### Praise what is good

Tell editors what to keep doing, not only what to fix. Editors usually only hear what to change, so on the next edit they waste energy guessing what to keep

Say "i love this cutaway" when you do

Split your fixes into "for now" and "for future reference". A note for next time sounds like this: "i love when we punch in so the eye line matches, it is less abrupt"

It speeds up an editor's learning

#### Match feedback to how much you post

If you post one YouTube video a month, fix almost everything on that video. If you post 14 reels a week, speed and learning are worth more than taking a piece from good to perfect

On long edits, check in often. A weekly check-in can catch a wrong turn in week 2, before it becomes a full redo

### Steps

Sorting editor notes:

1. Must-fix: the point is lost or the brand is hurt. Fix now
2. Clearly wrong: spelling errors. Fix now
3. Taste: colour, font, framing. Leave for next time
4. Match the fix-now share to how much you post

### Mistakes

- adding flashy graphics that don't help understanding
- punch-ins where the eye line jumps
- treating taste notes as must-fix and slowing down
- waiting until the end of a long edit to review it

### Try it

Watch one of your videos and write down every note you have. Mark each as must-fix, clearly wrong or taste, and fix only the first two

Then find one moment where you name something and add a visual that shows it

## Lesson 6.12: How often to post: volume, quality and batching

### Big idea

Creators disagree on how often to post. Some say post a lot, others say post less. All agree on one thing: pick a pace you can keep for years

### How it works

#### Two routes: pick one

The quality route is 3 to 5 posts a day, at least 3 hours apart, so each post has time to be shown to people

The quantity route is up to 24 posts a day, at least 1 hour apart. It only suits news accounts or very fast niches. You accept that many posts will flop and hope 1 or 2 a day work

Choose one of the two and skip the blurry middle

#### The case for posting a lot

You won't build a brand from 10 or 20 posts. More posts means more chances to go viral (get seen by a huge number of people), and you control how much you post, which you can't say about luck

Posting daily also keeps you top of mind. Say a plumber friend texts you every day for a year. He is the one you call when you need a plumber

You don't understand quality until you have done quantity. Some people hide behind quality and post once every 6 months

#### The case for posting less

One great piece can do more for you than 10 average ones. Rushed videos get shown to fewer people, while a great one can keep getting views for a year or two

Putting both views together: pick a pace you can keep for years, and make each piece as good as you can at that pace

#### Quality, then volume, then more quality

1. Get your quality to 7 out of 10
2. Raise your volume to the normal pace for the platform: about 1 short video a day, 1 YouTube video a week, or about 5 short text posts a day
3. Push quality to 9 out of 10

After that, stay consistent for 6 months, answer every comment, and move people to your email list

#### Batching

Post daily but film weekly. Batch your work (make many pieces at once) and set one content day so you don't burn out

Don't batch more than a week or two ahead, because batching also batches your mistakes. Learn from every shoot before you film the next one. If you are excited about an idea, make it now, because excitement fades by shoot day

### Steps

Quality, then volume, then more quality:

1. Get quality to 7 out of 10
2. Raise volume to the platform's normal pace
3. Push quality to 9 out of 10
4. Stay consistent for 6 months, answer every comment, and move people to email

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say posting a lot gives you more chances than luck does. Others say post every second or third day, because one great piece is worth more than 10 average ones

### Mistakes

- picking a middle pace that is neither high quality nor high volume
- hiding behind quality and posting once every 6 months
- batching a month ahead, which batches your mistakes
- waiting for shoot day when you are excited about an idea now

### Try it

Pick your route: quality (3 to 5 a day) or quantity. Write the pace you could keep for a year. Plan one weekly content day, and make sure no batch reaches more than 2 weeks ahead

## Lesson 6.13: A fast-growth plan for short videos

### Big idea

A six-part plan for growing fast on short video: make people watch many videos in a row, build a signature style, find your edge, write strong hooks, pick the right type of story, and post daily while copying your own winners

### How it works

#### Make people watch many videos

A follow usually needs a few minutes of your content, so get people to watch several videos in a row. A numbered series ("lesson 1 of 30") does this

A series with no numbers works too, if each video feels like part of the same show

#### Style and edge

A signature visual style is a look people link to you: your look, your set, how you frame the shot and your editing. If you can't edit, build one great set once and reuse it

Your edge is what makes you different. It is either new information, or a new way of delivering information people already know

#### Hooks with two hits of interest

Give people something to look at first, then hint at the value. That gives them two reasons to stay before they can swipe away

Make the value clear within about 2.5 seconds

You can also make people feel at home by showing a face or brand your viewer already knows

#### Post daily and review in batches of 10

Pick the type of story that shows off what you are good at, and post daily

After each batch of 10 videos, sort them by followers gained, then copy what the winner did differently in your next batch

Growth often starts with one video that takes off, plus a bigger account sharing it

### Steps

Fast growth: 6 parts:

1. Make people watch many videos with a series
2. Build a signature visual style
3. Find your unique edge
4. Write hooks: something to see, then the value in 2.5 seconds
5. Pick your type of story
6. Post daily and review in batches of 10 by followers gained

### Mistakes

- sorting a batch by views instead of followers gained
- starting with the style and skipping the hook
- expecting growth without one video that takes off

### Try it

Take your last 10 short videos, sort them by followers gained, and write down one thing the top one did differently

## Lesson 6.14: One idea, many platforms: reusing and order

### Big idea

One piece of content can be reused in many formats and platforms, but each platform needs its own version. Creators disagree on how well cutting a long piece into short ones still works

### How it works

#### Each platform needs its own version

Make one clean video, then give it a different hook and caption for each app. The same video can do great on Instagram and flop on TikTok

So track how many people stay past the first second and how much of the video they watch, and don't look at views alone

#### The waterfall method and its limits

The waterfall is making one long piece, like a long video or article, and cutting it into everything else: clips, posts and quotes

Some creators say it works less than it did a year ago. Turning a long video into written posts still works, but cutting a YouTube video into short clips may not

Others say one long piece can still become 5 or more short ones. Test it on your own account

#### Video first or article first

Test the order for each piece. Say you post a video about a topic two days before the article on the same topic

For another topic, you swap them: article first, video the next day

A video version of an article helps people who don't like reading

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say one long piece can become 5 or more short ones. Others say the waterfall works less than it did a year ago, and that cutting YouTube into short clips may not work any more

### Mistakes

- posting the same hook and caption on every app
- judging a cross-post by views only
- assuming a YouTube video cut into shorts still works the same

### Try it

Take one long piece and write a different hook and caption for two platforms. After a week, compare how many people stayed past the first second

## Lesson 6.15: AI in video production and editing

### Big idea

AI can work as an editor and a production tool. You teach it your style, give it raw footage, approve its plan, then let it edit. For AI-made video, learn each piece one at a time and keep your characters and products the same in every clip

### How it works

#### An AI editor workflow

1. Teach it your editing style. Give it finished videos, ask it to break down how they are edited, and go back and forth until it explains your style back to you in plain English
2. Upload raw footage (clips straight from the camera). It finds the best takes
3. Ask for an editing plan: the structure, the cuts and where extra footage goes
4. Approve the plan or give feedback before editing starts. This keeps a human in charge
5. Let it edit, then review, improve and post

#### Why it helps

AI can edit several videos at the same time, and not only faster. Say you give it 10 raw files at once. You review the results while it works on the next ones

#### Learning AI video production

Learn the pieces in this order: images first, then voices, then video generation, then scripts, then putting it all together

Train your eye to spot what feels off, like faded colours in the starting image, a voice that sounds like a studio ad, or a script nobody would say out loud. Fix each one on its own before combining them

#### Keep characters and products the same

Describing a person in words gives you a different face each time. So make a character sheet first, with a close-up and a full-body image, and use those images for every clip

A person can hold your product, but the product must stay exactly the same, or you get a second bottle or a sixth finger. Keep clip prompts short

For a long ad, fix your characters, products and places before making anything, then make it scene by scene

### Steps

AI editor workflow:

1. Teach it your editing style
2. Upload raw footage
3. Get an editing plan
4. Approve it or give feedback
5. Let it edit
6. Review, improve and post

### Mistakes

- describing a character in words each time and getting a new face
- letting the model change the product in the clip
- combining images, voice and script before fixing each one
- skipping the approval step on the editing plan

### Try it

Pick one finished video. Ask an AI to describe how it is edited, and refine the description until it matches your style in plain English

## Lesson 6.16: Viewer satisfaction: give what the title and thumbnail promised

### Big idea

YouTube measures how much value a video gives per minute, and a great title and thumbnail can't save a video that doesn't deliver on them

### How it works

#### What satisfaction is

Viewer satisfaction is YouTube's way of measuring the value a video gives per minute. It is easy to overlook because there is no number for it in your dashboard, and people work on the numbers they can see

#### Three things to do on every video

1. Deliver on the promise in the intro. The title and thumbnail make a promise, and the quickest way to lose satisfaction is to not keep it. Deliver it within the first 5 seconds, with a picture or with words
2. Care about every detail. Viewers notice a glitchy edit, a typo in a caption or a sudden loud sound, and they judge a video fast
3. Spend time on moments that take lots of effort and don't scale. Over-delivering (giving more than you promised) is what people remember

#### What happens when a video does not deliver

Say a video has a great title and thumbnail, but sloppy captions and no structure. It may get a burst of views on day one, because YouTube gives it a real chance. Then it stops, because viewers leave when it does not deliver

If it had delivered, it could have kept growing

#### Long videos on TV

For many viewers, TV is now the biggest screen they watch YouTube on. For TV, videos over 20 minutes with a slower pace and deeper storytelling work well, and production quality counts more, because people don't put up with poor picture, sound or lighting on TV

Short videos on phones still need fast pacing

### Steps

Satisfaction checklist:

1. Deliver the promise in the first 5 seconds
2. Fix every typo, glitch and audio spike
3. Add one over-delivered moment that doesn't scale

### Where creators disagree

The TV advice (slower, deeper, longer) can look like it clashes with the fast-pacing advice. Both can be true, because they describe different screens and lengths

### Mistakes

- making a promise in the title that the video doesn't keep early
- leaving caption typos and sudden loud sounds
- ignoring satisfaction because there is no number for it

### Try it

Watch the first 5 seconds of your last video, or read the first 3 lines of your last post. Did they deliver what the title or hook promised? Rewrite them so they do

## Cheat sheet: module 6

- pick the formats that fit your platform, and keep at most about 3
- change only the topic and keep the shape of each format
- study winners in your own format
- drop a format that flops twice
- make what you say, show, write and play all say the same thing
- find the key visual first, then write the hook around it
- teach only one thing in a short video and save the payoff for the end
- open a long video with the promise, the proof and the plan
- make early points shorter than later ones
- open a new question at every change of topic
- use an outline, and talk as if to one friend
- sort editor notes into must-fix and taste
- pick a posting pace you can keep for years
- give each platform its own hook and caption
- keep a human approval step when AI edits
