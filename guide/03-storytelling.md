## 3.1 Why stories work

*6 min read*

> **The big idea:** Storytelling is a set of steps you can learn. It works because a good story keeps the listener guessing what happens next

### A skill you can learn

Great storytelling feels like magic, but it follows a pattern. It is a short loop of steps that repeats

The best speakers are not more talented than everyone else. They use the same few patterns again and again

It works everywhere, not just in videos: posts, emails, presentations, sales calls and the story of your business

### What a good story does

A good story does 3 things. It makes people feel something (emotion), it teaches them something (value) and it makes them want more (curiosity)

It also makes a topic yours. Two people can explain the same idea, but a different story and angle makes the audience react in a different way

A story is one tool, and some messages work best with plain facts

### The flat story problem

The weakest story is the reporter kind: you list facts in order. Say you tell it like this: "I woke up, I went to work, I had a meeting, I went home." Nothing surprises anyone, so the listener's brain guesses the next line and gets bored

Think of a vending machine. You press a button and get exactly what you expected, so you feel nothing

A slot machine is different: you do the same action but can't predict the result, and that keeps people playing

A good story works like the slot machine. If the listener already knows what comes next, it is a vending machine story

### What research says

In a small 2010 brain study with 11 listeners, brain activity lined up with the speaker's during a 15-minute story. It sometimes even ran slightly ahead, because the listeners were guessing what came next

Other research found that when people get pulled into a story, they tend to take on its beliefs and argue against it less. Later studies found mixed results, so treat it as a tendency

These studies show that people guess ahead and look forward to what comes next. They don't prove that a video makes people "addicted"

**Example: Flat story vs story with a surprise**

Flat: "I woke up, went to work, had a meeting and went home." With a surprise: "I got to work and found a note on my desk that said, you're running the meeting today." (Made up for the example)

*Why it works:* The second version makes you wonder what happens next

### Mistakes to avoid

- thinking storytelling is a gift you are born with
- telling events in order with nothing unexpected
- claiming more than the science says, like "dopamine addiction"

### Try it

Pick a story you told recently and write it as 5 plain facts in order. Find the point where a listener could not guess what comes next

If there is none, add one: a twist or an open question

## 3.2 Context plus conflict

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A story is built from 2 blocks: a little context (where you were) and a conflict (a problem or question). Give only the context people need, get to the conflict fast, and link the steps with "but" or "therefore" instead of "and then"

### The storytelling equation

Every story has 2 blocks. Context tells people where you were in life at that moment

Conflict is the problem or question that creates tension, and tension makes people want to hear what happens next

Say an actor starts a story like this: "For years I only played the nice guy in romantic comedies, and a child was on the way. Was I more than a nice guy?" The first sentence is the context

The question is the conflict, and it makes you want the answer

### Only the context people need

Most people ramble: "We were at a party, there was a red couch, a stool in the corner, someone handed me a beer..." The listener is still waiting for the story to start

You only need 3 things: where you were, when it was and what you were doing. Then go straight to the conflict

A good opening: "In 2023 I was at the gym when a founder called and said, I want to build a media company." One sentence gives all the context

Name the place, don't describe it. If you say "conference room", the listener pictures their own, and that works better than your description

### "But" and "therefore"

The simplest technique is a dance: a bit of context, then a conflict, then more context, then another conflict. Each new conflict gives people a new reason to keep watching

Link the steps with "but" (a turn) or "therefore" (a result of the step before). If you link them with "and then", you only have a list of events

"He lost the job, and then he moved" is a list. "He lost the job, therefore he had to move, but the new city had no work" is a story

To test it, read your script, put a linking word between every step and rewrite each "and then" as "but" or "therefore"

### Start with the point

At school we learn to tell stories in this order: where and when, then who and what, then how and why. Online, that loses people before the point arrives

Start with what and why: "The future of entertainment is about to look very different." Then add who and how so people believe you, and put where and when last as a small detail

**Example: The call from a founder**

"In 2023 I was at the gym and a founder called me and said, I want to build a media company."

*Why it works:* It gives where (the gym), when (2023) and what the speaker was doing, and the conflict starts in the same sentence

**Example: A cafe story**

Say a cafe was about to close, BUT a food blogger posted about it, THEREFORE 500 people came on Saturday, BUT they only had flour for 50 pastries

*Why it works:* Every "but" brings back the curiosity, and every "therefore" moves the story forward

### Mistakes to avoid

- rambling context: furniture, who said hello, what was on the table
- linking steps with "and then", which turns a story into a list
- starting with where and when instead of what and why

### Try it

Take a story you like to tell. Rewrite the opening as one sentence with only where, when and what you were doing, then the conflict

Next, write a linking word between each step and turn every "and then" into "but" or "therefore"

## 3.3 Zoom into the moment

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Weak stories sum things up from far away. Good stories zoom into one moment so the listener feels inside it. 5 tools do that: the place, your actions, your thoughts, your feelings shown through your body and the exact words people said

### Replay it, don't sum it up

The best stories take the listener into the moment. Say you tell it as a summary: "My boss was very angry." Now replay it: "My boss walked in, put the report on my desk and said, who approved this?"

A simple trick is to tell it in the present tense: "I'm on my couch, I open my laptop." That puts the listener in the moment

### Place and actions

Place: name where you are. "September 2019, I'm standing in front of the conference room, ready to walk in." The listener pictures their own conference room, so skip the table, the TV and the floor

Actions: say what you are doing with verbs (walking, shouting, reading, waiting) and skip background that adds nothing. "I open my laptop and start reading a message from my manager"

### Thoughts and feelings

Thoughts: say what went through your head, raw and a bit nervous. "I thought, oh man, this is bad, everyone will think I'm stupid" is easy to relate to

"I thought, this is a great opportunity for growth" sounds fake, because nobody thinks like that

Feelings: naming a feeling ("I was relieved") is flat. Show it through the body: "I lean back and let out a big sigh"

Facts alone sound like a robot. Feelings connect you to the listener, so ask yourself what you were thinking and feeling

### Dialogue

Give the exact words someone said at the key moment. Instead of "my friend was very disappointed", say "my friend looked at me and said, what on earth was that?"

Keep it short, like real speech. Nobody says "I'm very dissatisfied with how that project was done"

Dialogue is the most used of the 5 tools. Two lines like "You're young." "Is that going to be a problem?" already carry tension

### One odd detail

You forget tiny details within days: what you wore, the smell of the air, a strange object on the table. They make a story feel real, because nobody can say "that didn't happen"

Pick one odd detail, and skip the long list of furniture

**The 5 tools to zoom in**

1. Place: name it, with no extra detail
2. Actions: the verbs, what you are doing
3. Thoughts: raw and a bit nervous
4. Feelings: show them through your body and face
5. Dialogue: the exact words, short and catchy

**Example: At customs**

"Who are you visiting?" "My wife." "Is she an actress?" "Her name's Emily Blunt." "You married Emily Blunt?"

*Why it works:* An actor can tell this in 20 seconds, and the replay is far more fun than the summary "the officer was very surprised"

### Mistakes to avoid

- summing up from far away instead of replaying the moment
- adding too many details about the place
- using polished, professional-sounding thoughts and dialogue
- naming feelings instead of showing them

### Try it

Pick one moment from this week that took under a minute. Tell it in 5 lines, one per tool: the place, what you were doing, one raw thought, one feeling shown through your body and one exact line someone said

Read it out loud in the present tense

## 3.4 Surprise and emotion

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Every great story has a surprise, and the main goal of a story is to make people feel something. Start with the interesting or weird part, then ask yourself how the event made you feel

### Start with the surprise

The most common storytelling mistake is the "once upon a time" opening, where nothing surprising happens early. Every great story has a surprise

Without one it is just a list of facts

A weird true detail from your life is an easy way to open: "I used to work in a strip club." It gets attention instantly, and it is honest, because it really happened

Everyone has weird parts of their life. Most people hide them, so their stories never make anyone curious

### Feeling is the goal

If people don't feel anything, your story means nothing

To find the feeling, ask yourself: how did this event, person or thing make me feel? Then say it

Telling a feeling honestly creates the same feeling in the listener

You don't need many words. A famous six-word story reads: "For sale: baby shoes, never worn." It says almost nothing, and you feel everything

### Feel before you make

Right before you write or tell a story, read an essay or watch a video that moves you. Feelings are contagious, and they carry into what you make

For writing that spreads fast, pick the feeling before you write: laughing, shock, "finally someone said it", sweet, amazed. If the reader feels nothing at the end, the piece will not get far

### The short formula

Start with an interesting idea, add a feeling, and build it around something that really happened to you

### Mistakes to avoid

- opening with "once upon a time" and no surprise
- hiding the weird parts of your life
- telling a story without asking how it made you feel

### Try it

List 5 weird or surprising facts about your life that you normally leave out. Pick one and write the first line of a story that opens on it

Then answer in one sentence: how did that time in my life make me feel? Add that feeling to line 2 or 3

## 3.5 Stakes and the big question

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A story that keeps people hooked repeats a loop of 4 steps: stakes, a big question, a head fake and a re-hook. This lesson covers the first two. Stakes give people a reason to care, and a specific big question gives them something to guess

### The loop that keeps people watching

The loop has 4 steps that repeat: stakes, big question, head fake, re-hook. This lesson teaches the first two, and the next one teaches the last two

Think of blackjack. What keeps gamblers playing is the wait between placing the bet and finding out what happens. A story told with this loop keeps the listener's brain busy guessing the whole time

### Step 1: stakes

Stakes are the reason to care: what could be won or lost. If people have no reason to care, they don't guess what comes next, and they leave

Stakes have 3 parts:

- a character someone can cheer for. It can be you, your client or "a creator I was talking to the other day"
- something at risk, like "I was about to lose my biggest client"
- a deadline, like "the call was in 2 hours". A ticking clock makes people guess faster

Stakes don't have to be life or death. They only have to be important to your viewer, like 48 hours left to find out why an ad budget is not working

People skip this step most often, because the reason to care feels obvious to you. Test your intro: if a viewer could say "why would I even care?", it needs a stake

### Stakes for any topic

Stakes are not only for big creators. You can add one to any topic

A consequence stake shows what the viewer can win or lose. For a video on investing: "Following these rules can be the difference between 1 million and 100,000 in retirement"

An enemy stake names someone who doesn't want the viewer to know this: "Big banks don't want you to know what I'm about to tell you"

### Step 2: the big question

The big question gives the viewer something specific to guess. In blackjack the dealer shows one card, and you start thinking: I have 16, the dealer shows a 10, should I take another card?

The most common mistake is being too vague: "something crazy happened", "you won't believe it". The brain can't guess from that

Give just enough information to make a guess, but not so much that it overwhelms. Good: "I got on a call with my client, and within 30 minutes she told me something that completely changed my plan for the next 6 months." Now you are guessing: a complaint? A new trend? Weak: "I had a call with my client and it went well." There is nothing to guess

Put a big question near the beginning, because the earlier it appears, the longer people stay curious. Before you reveal something important, add a little context first, so the viewer can guess

### Why just enough works

Psychology describes curiosity as the uncomfortable feeling of a gap in what you know. It only appears when you already know something about the topic, and a gap that is too big or too small does not pull you in

**The 4-step loop**

1. Stakes: a character to cheer for, something at risk, a deadline
2. Big question: specific enough that the viewer can guess
3. Head fake: the outcome breaks the guess but makes sense afterwards
4. Re-hook: open the next question in the same breath, then repeat

**Example: The prince and the dragon, flat**

A kingdom is in trouble. The thing that can save it is at the top of a tower guarded by a dragon. The prince rides there, beats the dragon, and the kingdom is saved

*Why it works:* The events are fine, but nothing makes you guess or care. It is a vending machine story

**Example: The prince and the dragon, with stakes**

A plague is spreading toward the town where the prince's family lives. The only cure is a flower at the top of a tower guarded by a dragon nobody has beaten. If he doesn't go, everyone he loves is dead by the end of the week. At the tower he sees the dragon is 10 times bigger than he thought, and he has one sword and no armour. How is he going to get past it?

*Why it works:* There is a character, something at risk, a deadline and a clear question, so you start guessing

### Mistakes to avoid

- skipping stakes because the reason to care feels obvious to you
- using vague teasers like "something crazy happened" that give the viewer nothing to guess
- revealing the important thing before the viewer has had a chance to guess it

### Try it

Take one story from your work. Write 3 lines: who it happened to, what was at risk and the deadline. Then write the big question as one sentence with a specific detail in it (a number, a time, a person) so a stranger can make a guess

## 3.6 Head fakes and re-hooks

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** These are steps 3 and 4 of the loop. A head fake (a surprise ending the viewer didn't guess, but one that makes sense afterwards) breaks the guess. A re-hook opens the next question in the same breath, so there is never a moment to leave

### Step 3: the head fake

A head fake is an ending the viewer didn't guess. In blackjack you take another card on 16 and get a 5 for 21, or the dealer flips a 21. Researchers call a wrong guess a prediction error, and say a bigger surprise gives a stronger reward signal in the brain

A bad head fake comes out of nowhere with no setup. It is shocking but makes no sense, so the viewer feels confused instead of delighted

A great head fake is something you would never have guessed, but the moment you hear it you see why it is true. The clues were there all along

Think of a magician. He shows you the hat, shows you it is empty, says "look over here", and then the rabbit appears. You are surprised, yet it makes sense

### How to build one

There are 2 steps:

1. Work out what the viewer will naturally guess from what you have told them
2. Give an ending that is different from that guess but makes sense as soon as they hear it

Say you write "a car pulls up and he gets in". The viewer imagines 4 things that could happen next, and then a fifth one happens

In a long video, aim for one surprise like this every 2 to 3 minutes. After one, viewers pay closer attention, because they don't want to be fooled again

### Step 4: the re-hook

In a casino a new hand is dealt before you finish thinking about the last one. One loop closes and another opens at the same moment, so there is never a gap where you think "okay, I'm done"

A re-hook works the same way. Open the new question at the exact moment the head fake lands, while the viewer is still taking it in

Good: "And that's when she told me she was doubling her budget, which would have been amazing if I hadn't just signed an exclusivity deal with her competitor the night before." Weak: "She doubled her budget. Anyway, now I'll move on to the next point"

Some phrases close one loop and open the next in one breath:

- "which would have been great, except..."
- "but here's the problem with that..."
- "and that's when I realized..."

### Where to put re-hooks

Find every place in your script where one part ends and a new one starts. People often leave at these points, so put a new question, a complication or a consequence right there

When you solve one problem, open the next one right away: "But great creative is wasted unless you fix this too." After 3 or 4 closed loops, the viewer feels you solved 4 problems for them and stays for the next one

**Example: The prince and the dragon, with a head fake**

As the prince approaches, the dragon pulls away, scared. A massive chain bolts its neck to a metal plate. The dragon is a prisoner, so it was never guarding the tower. He breaks the chain, climbs to the top, and finds the flower and the king, his father, who sent him

*Why it works:* You expected a fight and got a rescue, and the clues were there (it pulled away, it looked scared). The re-hook lands at once: why is the king there?

### Mistakes to avoid

- using random twists with no setup, which confuse people instead of delighting them
- leaving dead air after a payoff ("anyway, now I'll move on")
- closing a loop without opening the next one

### Try it

Write down the ending of a story you tell. Above it, write what a stranger would guess after hearing your setup. If the guess is the same as the ending, find a true detail that makes the real ending surprising, and plant one clue earlier. Then write the next sentence with "which would have been great, except..."

## 3.7 Captivation, anticipation, validation

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The core of any script is 3 beats: put a question in the viewer's head, let them guess the answer, then close the loop with an answer they didn't expect

### Captivation: the open question

Captivation means the viewer is asking an open question in their own head. It is a curiosity loop

The bigger, less obvious and more relevant the question, the stronger the pull. The viewer has to care about it: "when are the aliens visiting Earth?" is a huge question, but a viewer who doesn't care about aliens leaves

### Anticipation: let them guess

Anticipation is the viewer guessing the answer, like a mystery novel playing out in their head. Curiosity is highest just before the answer

So give details that bring the answer close. Then, just before it, either head fake them in another direction, which resets the loop, or give the answer and ask a second question right after

Viewers can only guess if the facts are clear. Random details that don't fit the question confuse them, and they leave

Ask yourself 3 things:

1. Based on the question they have, what will they expect the answer to be?
2. Which facts bring them closer?
3. Should I give the answer now, or head fake them to reset?

### Validation: close the loop

Validation means answering the question you opened. The answer should be something the viewer would not have predicted

In entertainment this is the ending: don't end on a cliffhanger, and tie up the loose ends. In teaching it is the tip, story or recommendation, with real, concrete value

If you leave the loop open, the viewer is unsatisfied, because they never got the final reward

### Why open the loop first

A 2014 study found that when people were very curious about a question, they remembered the answer better. They also remembered unrelated faces shown while they waited, both right away and a day later

So open the loop first, then teach. What you say while the viewer is curious sticks

**Question, guess, answer**

1. Captivation: a big, non-obvious question in the viewer's head that they care about
2. Anticipation: facts that let them guess, then a head fake, or an answer plus a new question
3. Validation: close the loop with an unexpected answer that has real value

### Mistakes to avoid

- asking questions the viewer doesn't care about, like the alien example
- adding random or confusing details, so the viewer can't guess and leaves
- ending on a cliffhanger, or never giving the actual tip
- giving an obvious answer, which is no reward

### Try it

Pick a short video or post that held you to the end. Write down the first question it put in your head, the moment you started guessing, and the final answer. Then map one of your own drafts the same way and mark which beat is missing

## 3.8 Making boring topics feel new

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Content that holds attention is relevant (it is about me), new and interesting. Even a dull topic can be all three if you show a new angle, set it against what people used to believe, add real urgency, give proof close to the viewer, and tell it like a secret

### The 3 ingredients

Content that grips people gets a yes to 3 questions. Is this about me (relevant)? Is this new to me (new)? Am I curious to learn more (interesting)?

Relevant is the easy one: call out the viewer, like "if you run a small dental practice...". New and interesting are harder with a dull topic

While scrolling, the brain ignores anything familiar and only stops for new things. But new alone is not enough, because if it isn't relevant, people keep scrolling

### Step 1: a new reveal tied to a result

Make an old thing feel new by revealing that something about it has changed, then connect it to a result the viewer wants

Take water. "Drinking more water is good for you" has been heard 10,000 times. Say you try: "New research says when you drink water gives you more energy than your morning coffee." That is a new reveal (the timing) tied to a result (energy)

Every topic has old ideas, and old ideas have new angles: a new way to explain it, a new name, an update, or a finding nobody talks about

### Step 2: compare it to what people believed

Put the new reveal right next to the old belief. The brain can't tell something is new unless it compares it to something old

"You've been told your whole life you need 8 glasses of water a day. That number was pretty much made up. What changes how you feel is when you drink it"

It works because when the brain thinks it might be wrong, it pauses and listens. Compare with the true opposite of the old belief, because a random, unrelated comparison only confuses

A few shapes you can use:

- "everyone thinks X, but actually Y"
- "you've been told X your whole life. Turns out Y is what to do if you want Z"

### Step 3: urgency (optional)

Urgency tells the viewer to pay attention now, because something just changed. "There's a new AI tool that writes newsletters in your voice" becomes "this new AI tool came out yesterday"

Skip it if it doesn't fit. Don't add a fake deadline: people can smell it, and they trust you less

### Step 4: proof close to the viewer's life

Show evidence that your reveal is true. The best proof is the closest to the viewer's own life

Compare 2 versions. "A study says the timing of drinking water affects energy." Versus: "Say a friend your age crashed at work every day at 3 p.m. He changed when he drinks water, and his energy went up." People don't trust claims from far away, and they do trust seeing someone like them get the result

From strongest to weakest proof:

- a one-to-one example that matches the viewer: same age, same situation, same problem
- a group of people who already know you getting the result
- proof from someone else, like a study, an article or an award

Climb as high as you honestly can, and never invent proof

### Step 5: keep it feeling new

Stand behind your angle. Don't say "this is basic biology, people have known it for decades". To anyone who doesn't know the old way of seeing it, it really is new

Sound like a friend sharing a secret: "Okay, so not many people know about this yet..." Shouting "Huge news! This changes everything!" sounds like a billboard and makes people think you are selling

**Making an old topic feel new**

1. New reveal plus result: a new angle tied to what the viewer wants
2. Comparison: put it next to the true opposite of the old belief
3. Urgency (optional): real, recent news, never fake
4. Proof close to home: as close to the viewer as you can honestly get
5. Keep it feeling new: stand behind it, and tell it like a secret

**Example: Water and energy**

Old: drink more water. New: when you drink it gives you more energy than coffee. Comparison: the famous 8 glasses was pretty much made up. Proof: a friend your age who crashed at 3 p.m. and fixed it by changing the timing (Made up for the example)

*Why it works:* The fact behind it is the same as the old advice, but the new angle, the result and the proof make people stop scrolling

### Mistakes to avoid

- repeating old advice as it is, so the brain tunes it out as familiar
- a new reveal with no result the viewer wants: people look, but don't stay
- fake urgency or fake deadlines, which feel like selling
- saying the new angle is an old idea, or announcing it loudly like an ad

### Try it

Take any dull topic. Write the new angle in one line, the old belief it goes against in one line, the result the viewer wants, and the closest-to-home proof you honestly have

## 3.9 Short stories: the 60-second story, HPC and tension

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Short stories work when you hold the answer back and the viewer can always see progress and conflict. 3 simple structures help: the 60-second story, V.I.R.A.L. and HPC (hook, progression, climax)

### Hold the answer back

Say you make a short called "the secret to success" and reveal the secret at second 5. Everyone leaves, because their question is answered

The answer goes at the end. Start with a question, build toward the answer, and keep it uncertain until the last second

### The 60-second story

A great story can take less than 60 seconds. It works in 5 steps:

1. Start with a question people never asked but now want answered, shocking enough to get attention right away
2. Build toward the answer with visible progress, like ticking off ingredients, so viewers can see how close you are
3. Add conflict before the answer, because if everything goes smoothly nobody cares. A second storyline beside the main one (a B plot) gives viewers something else to care about
4. Keep the answer uncertain to build tension
5. Give the answer fast, in few words

### V.I.R.A.L.: a checklist for short posts

Each letter is one thing to check:

- visual shock: give people something to look at. In text, a concrete picture works better than a general claim
- immediate start: begin almost at the climax (the biggest moment), with no setup
- rising action: "how I make coffee" is dull, "the coffee that keeps me awake for 24 hours" raises the stakes
- anticipation: drop hints, but don't give the answer early
- lasting payoff: the last word is the answer. If you have a CTA, put it before the answer, because people leave the second they hear it

### HPC: hook, progression, climax

Structure is how you put a video together: the story and the payoffs. A simple structure has 3 parts:

1. Hook: the first 5 seconds introduce the topic and leave many questions open, like "a snowball fight in the desert"
2. Progression: give the viewer what the hook promised. Show the hot desert, the sand that won't form balls and the trip to the mountains to get snow. Pulling ice out of a cooler in the car would disappoint viewers
3. Climax: the moment the promise is kept and the audience gets what they came for

### Tension in long videos

Many videos start high, sag in the middle and only peak at the end. Build tension every few minutes with a new challenge or question, so there is no good place to leave

Repeat the key rules now and then so viewers always know what is at stake. End on a high point, either dramatic or heartwarming, because it leaves the lasting impression

**Example: The drive-through burger**

The question: is it faster to cook a burger in the drive-through line? The progress: she adds tomatoes, lettuce and cheese. The conflict (B plot): her mom complains about the smoke. Then an uncertain ending and a quick answer

*Why it works:* One question, visible progress, a side conflict and a fast answer. That is the whole 60-second story

### Mistakes to avoid

- revealing the secret at second 5
- everything going smoothly, with no conflict or B plot
- putting a CTA after the answer, so people leave before they hear it
- a progression that doesn't match what the hook promised

### Try it

Take one short video or post idea and write it as a question, 3 visible progress steps, one conflict and a one-line answer. If you have a CTA, put it before the answer

## 3.10 Craft: rhythm, tone, direction and your angle

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Besides the loop you already learned, a few small skills make a story better: vary your sentence length, talk like you are speaking to one friend, write the last line first, pick your own angle on a shared topic, and show the topic while you say it

### Rhythm

If every sentence has the same length, the script sounds flat. "This sentence has five words

Here are five more words. Five word sentences are fine

But several together become boring." Hear how dull that gets

Mix short, medium and long sentences. A quick test: write one sentence per line

The right edge should look uneven, because a straight edge means a flat voice

### Tone

Write and film as if you are talking to one close friend, not to a crowd. Some creators tape a photo of a friend under the camera lens and talk to that photo

### Direction: write the last line first

Before you write anything else, write the last line. Make it memorable enough that someone could share it on its own

Then you know where the story is going

In a short video, the last line can loop back to the first line, so the video feels like it starts again when it ends

### Your angle

Everyone covers the same topic, so what makes yours different is your angle (the way you look at it)

Say a big sports game is on. Most people talk about who won

You talk about how much money the sponsors made. Nobody else covers that, so you have no competition

Before you write, ask: what is everyone saying about this topic, and what side is nobody covering?

### The first line and showing

The first line should say what the video is about, so the viewer knows right away whether it is for them

Show while you tell. Put a visual on screen that confirms the topic before people even hear the words, because people understand a picture faster than a sentence

**Small skills for any story**

1. Rhythm: an uneven right edge, with short and long sentences mixed
2. Tone: talk to one close friend
3. Direction: write the last line first
4. Angle: take a side of the topic nobody else is covering
5. Opening: say the topic and show it at the same time

### Mistakes to avoid

- making all sentences the same length, so the script sounds flat
- writing at the audience instead of to one friend
- finding the ending last, so there is no line worth sharing
- covering the same angle as everyone else

### Try it

Paste a script with one sentence per line and look at the right edge. If it is straight, rewrite some lines shorter and some longer

Then write the last line first and check that the story leads to it

## 3.11 Storytelling mistakes and the story ladder

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Most weak stories make the same few mistakes: a slow build, no enemy, nobody to cheer for, and an idea too big to repeat. The story ladder shows 6 levels of skill, from listing facts to your own signature style

### Mistake: the school-style arc

At school, stories are a slow build with one peak near the end. Online, that loses people before the peak arrives

Start at around 70 out of 100 in intensity, hit the first conflict early (about 90), let it settle, then peak again every few minutes. Each new peak is a re-hook

People leave faster online because there is endless content to watch, and human attention itself has not changed. The "8-second goldfish" statistic is a myth

### Mistake: no villain

A hero against a villain is stronger than a hero alone. The villain can be an idea or a group, like boring corporate marketing or posting just for likes

Ask in every piece: what is the common enemy here?

### Mistake: nobody to cheer for

Make your hero someone people can relate to. If nobody sees themselves in the hero, nobody cares if they win

### Mistake: ideas too big to repeat

Shrink the idea so someone can repeat it after one watch. "The British are coming" is a whole story in 4 words

Say your point twice: once as you would to a colleague, and once with a simple comparison, as you would to a child. "600 mph is like a plane driving on the road" is easier to picture than the number

### The story ladder

The ladder has 6 levels of skill, and each one adds something to the one below:

1. The reporter: facts in order, with nothing to guess
2. The illusionist: surprises inside the story
3. The champion: stakes aimed at your viewer's biggest problem
4. The architect: arcs and loops. In teaching content, repeat a mini loop for each point: what is going on, why it is important, an example, what to do
5. The translator: a visual or simple comparison for every complex point
6. The maestro: your own signature style, the thing only you do. It comes after hundreds of tries, so don't force it

### Mistakes to avoid

- a slow build with one late peak
- a hero with no villain, idea or group to push against
- an idea too big to repeat after one watch
- forcing your own signature style before you have practised enough

### Try it

Take one lesson or tip and write a mini loop for it: what is going on, why it is important, an example, what to do. Then shrink the idea to one line someone could repeat

## 3.12 Finding, writing and editing your stories

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Stories come from living, noticing and collecting. A simple structure (C.A.R.T.) helps you tell them, a few exercises help you find them, and writing first and editing after finishes the job

### C.A.R.T.: a structure for short stories

C.A.R.T. is a 4-part structure for stories of 1 to 2 minutes:

1. Context: who, where, when and what they want, in 2 to 3 sentences
2. Adversity: the problems. This is the longest part: what was at stake, what went wrong and how it felt
3. Resolution: what they did and how it turned out
4. Takeaway: say "what I learned is..." instead of "you should" or "the moral is". Nobody likes being lectured

It gets stronger if you open with a question ("yesterday I had the worst meeting of my life"), share raw thoughts and use the exact words people said

### Finding stories

Try first, last, best, worst. Pick a prompt like gift, job, car or injury and write your first, last, best and worst one in 1 to 2 words each. Forgotten stories come back

Every evening, write: if I had to tell one story from today, what would it be? One coach says he did this for 3 years and got about 1 good story a week

### Live it, notice it, collect it

You first have to live stories. If you never try anything new, there is nothing to tell

Don't wait for inspiration. Write stories down while they are fresh, because you forget the tiny details within days

Keep a story bank (a list of your stories). For each one, save its title, its point, a short summary, where you could use it and a rating

### Write it, then edit it

Write the story down first. Writing is thinking: it forces you to work out the events and link them to your main point. Write the way you talk, with real dialogue and short forms like BTW

Then edit with 3 goals: fewer words, simpler words, and words a 10-year-old would use. The best stories are the ones people can understand

### Skip parts of the timeline

Your goal is to inspire, entertain, convince or teach, and to save the reader time. Telling events in order is predictable, so jump around and cut whole years that don't help your point

**Example: A story bank entry**

Title: the 3 p.m. crash. Point: when you drink water is more important than how much. Summary: a friend fixed his energy by changing when he drinks water. Where to use it: a post about habits. Rating: 4 out of 5. (Made up for the example)

*Why it works:* 5 fields for every saved story, so you can find it later

### Mistakes to avoid

- waiting for inspiration instead of keeping a library
- ending with "you should" or "the moral is"
- spending most of the story on context and rushing the problems
- telling the whole story in order

### Try it

Tonight write one line: if I had to tell one story from today, what would it be? Then add it to a story bank note with its title, point and a rating

## 3.13 Your brand as a story

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** A personal brand can be told as a story: where you were, what made you change, where you are now, and then chapter after chapter. Decide where the story ends, plan the chapters, and share both wins and losses

### A brand is a story with chapters

Decide the end of your story first, then work backwards to plan the chapters. Random posts give people no reason to follow along. A story in steps gives them a reason to come back for the next one

Tesla did this in 2006. The end goal was clean energy. Chapter 1 was an expensive sports car, chapter 2 a cheaper car and chapter 3 an affordable one. People followed along to see if it would work

Your voice has to fit the audience you want. If you say you share useful things but your page is mostly jokes, you attract the wrong people

### Your own story in 3 parts

A simple structure: where you were, what made you change, and where you are now

Use failures to show you are like your audience. People assume big creators were always big, because they can't see the earlier chapters. Showing them lets people see the whole picture

Questions to build your story bank:

- where did you start?
- what made you want to change?
- what did you struggle with?
- when did something finally click?
- what do you wish you had done differently?

Every answer is content. In replies, tell how you went through the same thing, because a personal story in a reply makes people trust you more

### The hero's journey for a brand

One way to plan the story is a hero's journey in 5 chapters:

1. The mission: why you exist and what you fight against
2. The challenge: the obstacles and the people who doubt you
3. The setbacks: failures you share openly
4. The comeback: wins and launches
5. The new chapter: a bigger mission, and the cycle starts again

People don't connect with a brand that only wins, because it feels like a robot. Share strong opinions, raw videos, what you struggle with and what you are building

### The other view

Telling how you were in a dark place and became a hero can sound like someone with a course to sell. A feed of only success is boring and hides the truth

Sharing only failures to win sympathy is just as bad. Be honest, humble and real, and tell the truth

### Story post formats

Formats you can copy:

- the first-job photo: show a photo from your first job, like flipping pizzas, and share what it taught you
- the year-by-year ladder: "at 16 I bought courses, at 18 I spent my whole salary on a specialist, at 19 I paid for a community that led to a well-paid job"
- the reintroduction: "Hi, I'm [name]. Reintroducing myself..." and then 3 facts about you
- the behind-the-scenes post: show how you make your content step by step
- a story opening like "it took me 8 years to realize this..."

### Mistakes to avoid

- posting random thoughts with no order and no end in sight
- showing only wins, which feels like a robot
- telling only a dark-place-to-hero story, or only sharing failures for sympathy
- telling success or failure stories with no lesson for the audience

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say the hero's journey is a great way to plan a brand, with a mission, setbacks and a comeback told in public. Others say the classic dark-place-to-hero story sounds self-promoting, and that sharing only failures is just as bad. They agree on one thing: don't show only wins

### Try it

Write your brand story in 3 lines: where you were, what made you change, where you are now. Then decide how your story ends and list 3 chapters on the way to that end

## 3.14 Trust through transformation, losses and takeaways

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** People like to follow someone who is changing, more than a finished expert. They trust you more when you share losses with a lesson than when you only show proof. This lesson also shows where else a story belongs: sales pages, ads, launches and article openings

### Follow the change

People follow someone who is changing more than a finished expert. They can't copy someone born a genius, rich or famous, but they can follow someone who went from nothing to success

So share the journey you went through and the one you are on now. Start sharing while you are at zero, before you know how it ends

A simple arc is documenter, doer, teacher. First you share what you are learning, then you do the thing, then you talk about what you have done. Each stage is a chapter

### Say your goal in public

Say your goal is to go from 0 to 100,000 followers with just a phone. By saying it publicly, you let people get behind you: they send messages, share it, and some start the same mission

A goal said out loud turns your posts into one story people can follow

### Losses build trust

Many creators lead with proof: results, numbers, wins. Proof is useful, but if it is all you show, people see a finished expert, not someone they relate to

Say you posted that you hoped to reach 100,000 followers in 6 months and got to 60,000. People see you are human, pay attention and give you tips

A loss needs a takeaway. A story that only makes people feel sorry for you has no lesson. Admitting you were bad at something makes you human, and adding a lesson makes it useful

### Connect the unknown to the known

To illustrate means to use a comparison or example that links something people already understand to your new idea

Making content without a plan is like putting a puzzle together without the picture on the box. Everyone knows how hard that is, so they get the point at once

### Where else a story belongs

Stories are not only for videos:

- sales pages: open the "how it works" section with the story of how you found the solution
- ads: give the product a real role in the story, and tell it as you would to a friend first
- product launches: start with the founder's story, like a personal problem that led to building it
- article openings: start inside one person's story and save the point for after the surprise
- replies: a personal story in a reply builds trust

### Mistakes to avoid

- leading only with proof and wins
- sharing sad or honest stories with no lesson
- telling nobody about your goal, so nobody gets behind it
- waiting until you know the outcome before you start sharing

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say losses build more trust than wins, and admit they lead with proof too often. Others warn that sharing only failures gets old fast. Together they say: share your losses, but only with a lesson, and keep it honest and humble

### Try it

Write one loss or miss you had, how it felt, and the takeaway someone could use. Then decide where to publish it

## Cheat sheet: Storytelling

The whole module on one page

- give the viewer a reason to care first: a character, something at risk and a deadline
- make the big question specific, so the viewer can guess the answer
- make the surprise make sense afterwards, so it never feels random
- open the next question in the same breath as the payoff
- start with only where, when and what you were doing, then go straight to the conflict
- link the steps with "but" or "therefore" instead of "and then"
- put the point first and the setting last
- replay the moment: place, actions, thoughts, feelings and the exact words said
- start with a surprise or a weird true detail from your life
- ask how the event made you feel, and tell it that way
- for a dull topic, find a new angle, put it next to the old belief and add proof close to the viewer's life
- don't fake urgency, and don't say the new angle is an old idea
- hold the answer to the end, and put any CTA before it
- write the last line first, and shrink your idea so someone can repeat it after one watch
- give every honest story a takeaway the audience can use
- keep a story bank (a list of your stories) and add to it every day
- write the way you talk, then edit it to be short and simple
- decide how your brand story ends first, then plan the chapters
