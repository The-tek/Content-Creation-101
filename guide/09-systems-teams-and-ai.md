## 9.1 Systems beat inspiration

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet says most people do not have a creativity problem, they have a process problem. Caleb Ralston adds that content is hard because people build it in a way they cannot keep up, so the first system is one you can sustain.

### A process problem, not a creativity problem

- Sam: "You don't have a creativity problem, you have a process problem." Build a system for ideas (topic, angle, format) and templates so you never start from a blank page.
- Sam: creators who wait for inspiration let their content depend on how they feel. "The level of your systems will dictate the level of your results."
- Sam's belief: good process can raise performance even without changing the ideas.
- Oren Meets World's view stages: stuck at 0 to 500 views, ask why anyone would care. To 1k+, fix structure (idea, hook, execution). At 10k to 50k, find your outliers and repeat them. Getting 100k on every post is about systems, team and process.
- Matt Gray: "It takes a team to build a media empire", and to stay in the game long term. If it all comes down to you, you likely won't stick with it.

### Build artifacts, not telephone games

- Sam: every process becomes an SOP, checklist or template so it lives on paper, not in your head.
- Sam's frameworks were internal artifacts for his team before they were ever content. He made them to move what was in his head to the people doing the work.
- Templates over blank pages: the more you templatize, the easier it gets (Sam).
- Ryan's systems for scale: SOPs for ideation, writing, editing, packaging and posting, plus a voice doc and a swipe file of hooks and formats.
- Matt Gray's writing system: a checklist of every lesson learned, applied to every post.
- Ryan: teaching, at work or online, forces you to rethink your own frameworks.

### Build a process you can keep (Caleb)

- People start with insane expectations, especially in the first 6 months, comparing themselves to top creators without taking the actions those results need.
- Many people film in formats they hate (vlog, direct to camera, video essay). Some would rather be interviewed by someone off camera. A game that works for others will not work for them.
- Do not make content on topics that make you want to quit. His example: a team asking the CEO of a \$600 million a year company to film "best networking hacks", the last thing that CEO wants to talk about.
- Make filming additive to your life, not subtractive: Caleb films at Airbnbs, at cool locations and on motorcycle rides, so he links making content with things he loves.
- Play a character only if it is really you: "if I'm not being myself, I'm not going to do it for more than 6 months."

### What a working system costs in time (Sam's numbers)

- Dan Martell: about 3 hours a week in total, 1 for Instagram and 2 for YouTube.
- Dan's brand at 1M followers (Nov 2023): a team of 3 plus 3 hours of Dan's time a week.
- Sam's own short form: about 1 hour ideating, 20 minutes shooting and 2 hours editing a week. Long form: about 1 hour ideating, 30 minutes shooting, then an editor.
- Martell Media today: a team of 17. All of these are Sam's claims.

### Frameworks

**Oren's view stages** (Oren Meets World)

1. 0 to 500 views: ask why anyone would care (value or entertainment? does it look good? does it stop people?)
2. To 1k+: fix structure (idea, hook, execution)
3. 10k to 50k: find your outliers and repeat them (a signature series max about 3 times a month)
4. 100k every post: systems, team and process

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, frameworks as internal artifacts:** Sam's named frameworks started as documents for the Martell Media team, made to transfer his thinking to editors and writers. Only later did they become content.
  <br>*Why it works:* Per Sam, writing it down is what lets other people do the work without a game of telephone, and the same principles double as content.
- **Caleb Ralston, filming on a motorcycle ride:** Caleb films his videos in places he enjoys, mid-week at Airbnbs, with his girlfriend and dog visiting at the weekend, and filmed one video on a motorcycle ride.
  <br>*Why it works:* Pairing content with things you enjoy makes it something you keep doing instead of something that drains you.

### Common mistakes

- Waiting for inspiration, so output depends on mood (Sam).
- Keeping the process in your head, so every handoff becomes a game of telephone (Sam).
- Copying a top creator's format or schedule you do not enjoy and quitting within 6 months (Caleb).
- Expecting top-creator results in the first 6 months without their investment of money, time and privacy (Caleb).

### Try it

Write down the steps you take from idea to published post as a numbered checklist. Mark the step you dread most, and write one change (format, place, time of day) that would make it more enjoyable, per Caleb.

## 9.2 The content workflow loop

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Every source describes the same loop: find ideas, make, produce, distribute, measure, feed the lessons back in. Sam Gaudet's 5M process and his weekly 5-step flow give it names, and he says project management is half the job.

### Sam's 5M process

- Map: the brand and 3 to 4 content buckets.
- Model: copy what already performs before you modify it. Internal ideation = what you have already said that worked (posts, coaching calls, stage talks, takes that make friends go "mm, good take"). External ideation = other people's topic, angle and format, never their words.
- Make: use a template and containers, then record and direct.
- Multiply: repost, re-edit, re-record, repurpose.
- Measure: the 4 numbers and a reporting rhythm (lessons 5 and 6).

### Sam's weekly 5-step flow

- 1 Ideate: find ideas already performing and check demand. "It's way easier to enter an existing conversation than to create a completely new one." Take topic, angle and format, not the content.
- 2 Create: block time to shoot. Push the hard thinking into pre-production so the shoot is just "how fast can I get through this".
- 3 Produce: hand it to someone, or simplify (one take, one caption).
- 4 Distribute: primary platforms first, then repurpose everywhere else.
- 5 Optimize: review the best performers of the last 6 to 12 months and feed them back into ideation. The loop runs every week.

### Other versions of the same loop

- XCreators' creator flywheel: learn, find inspiration, generate ideas, script, record, edit, distribute, analyze, repeat.
- Ryan's pipeline: ideas, production, review, publish, report. A kanban board works. Tools he lists: Notion or ClickUp for planning, Slack for team chat, Google Drive for files, Typefully for scheduling, platform analytics plus a simple weekly report.
- Sahil Bloom's content engine: consume newsletters and podcasts, pull out novel insights, log them on a Notion board, write from the board when inspired, then distill into a thread.
- Paddy Galloway: pre-production is the biggest difference between top creators and beginners. Top creators spend far more time planning before they press record. At some level YouTube is a decision-making game, like Amazon's boardroom picking which of 100 opportunities to pursue.

### Block time and plan the whole process

- Sam: give each of the 5 steps a time block, at least weekly. Short cycle times matter: if you shoot monthly, a lesson learned tomorrow waits 4 weeks.
- Audit the calendar every month or so. Sam moved all content creation to the weekend because weekdays are his busiest and most stressful.
- Dan Koe: block 1 to 2 hours for writing content, newsletters and promotions.
- Sam: project management is half the job. Notion boards, kanban boards and realistic timelines to key targets.
- Sam's "30 videos in 30 days" example: most people just make a video today. Instead plan the whole process: shoot once or twice a week? how many per shoot? edit in 5, 6 or 7 days?

### The four software needs (Dan Koe)

- 1 Somewhere to generate traffic. 2 Somewhere to collect emails (direct access and remarketing). 3 Somewhere to take payment and host products or services. 4 Somewhere to save ideas and writing.
- Keep it simple and low cost; most tools are free to start.
- Matt Gray: the newsletter platform does not matter (Beehiiv, Kit, HubSpot all fine), consistency does.

### Frameworks

**The 5M process** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Map: brand and 3 to 4 buckets
2. Model: copy what performs (internal and external) before modifying
3. Make: template, containers, record and direct
4. Multiply: repost, re-edit, re-record, repurpose
5. Measure: 4 numbers and a reporting rhythm

**The flow of content** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Ideate: ideas already performing
2. Create: blocked shoot time, thinking done in pre-production
3. Produce: hand off or simplify
4. Distribute: primary platform first, then repurpose
5. Optimize: feed last 6 to 12 months of winners back into ideation

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, moving content to the weekend:** Sam audited his calendar and moved all content creation to the weekend, because weekdays are his busiest and most stressful days.
  <br>*Why it works:* The system fits around real life instead of competing with the hardest days.

### Common mistakes

- Making one video today with no plan for shoot days, batch size or edit time (Sam).
- Long cycle times, so lessons wait weeks before they reach the next piece (Sam).
- Skipping pre-production and doing the thinking on camera (Galloway, Sam).
- Starting a brand-new conversation instead of entering one people already care about (Sam).

### Try it

Open your calendar and block one slot each for ideate, create, produce, distribute and optimize for next week. Write next to each slot how long it should take, then compare with how long it really took at the end of the week.

## 9.3 Idea banks and swipe files

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ideas are captured all week, not invented on demand. Every source keeps some kind of bank: insights, hooks, keywords, outliers, questions, stories, and saves them in one place you can find again.

### One place for ideas and writing

- Dan Koe: about 80% of an online business owner's work is ideas and writing (marketing, content, newsletters, products, services, and outlining and writing them). You need a place to save it: Notion, Google Docs and Obsidian all work (he notes Obsidian pairs well with Claude via MCP).
- Koe: scattered Apple Notes you never find again work at first, but eventually you will want something serious.
- Koe: think of your brand as a collection of notes.
- Ryan: use a database (Notion or ClickUp) for ideas.
- MrBeast: save found ideas, outliers, thumbnails and alerts into one collection for later.

### Banks of what works

- Mino Lee: a hook bank (hooks written down from 50K+ like videos in your format) mashed together with your idea list, plus a keyword bank of emotion words for your niche. "Write down a bank of keywords and a bank of hooks because you really want to download what works."
- Dan Koe: save anomaly posts (ones that did far better than normal) with 3 to 5 bullets on why each worked. Save the six persuasive elements as reference too.
- Paddy Galloway: screenshot successful videos into a doc and answer reflection prompts about why they worked.
- Matt Gray: X is "a copywriting Olympics". Build a swipe file of the top 20 posts you could not help saving, and hold your posts to that bar.
- Codie Sanchez: a swipe file of the top 100 posts in your niche, saving the hook, the first 30 seconds and the top comments. Steal the hook, never the content, and pour your expertise in.
- Kallaway's routine: a doc with "daily inspiration" (topics, angles, visuals worth stealing) and "long-term patterns" (hooks, structures and formats that keep showing up over weeks).
- Kallaway describes a database of 400+ top videos from the best creators in each niche, tagged by hook format, strategy, visuals, text and audio, used to pull out patterns. (Mentioned in his course pitch; the method is what matters.)

### Banks of what people ask

- Codie Sanchez: mine unanswered questions in comments ("but how do I do this with no money?" is the next video).
- Codie: keep a problem bank in the audience's exact words ("I don't have the money and I'm scared I'll look stupid asking", not "capital constraints").
- Ryan: his bot watches his DMs, and when 3+ people ask the same question he makes content on it. He says his crash course with 202k views came from exactly that.

### Banks of real material

- Philipp Humm's story bank: title, point of the story, summary, where to use it, rating. Feed it with "homework for life" (from Matthew Dicks): every evening write the one story from today you would tell.
- Dominic Bair (domschats) calls this his most valuable workflow: record the founder's meetings, strip anything private, send the transcripts to the copywriter. Endless real material.
- XCreators' inspiration system: build curated feeds, and let an AI watch saved videos and organise breakdowns into your planning doc, so hundreds of saved videos become ideas you can make.
- Ryan's Bookmarks Brain bot logs every bookmark and sends an evening summary of 3 saves worth turning into content.

### Frameworks

**Codie's 4-step content ideas** (Codie Sanchez)

1. Swipe file: top 100 posts in your niche (hook, first 30 seconds, top comments)
2. Steal the hook, never the content, and pour your expertise in
3. Mine unanswered questions in comments
4. Keep a problem bank in the audience's exact words

**Story bank fields** (Philipp Humm)

1. Title
2. Point of the story
3. Summary
4. Where to use it
5. Rating

### Examples

- **Ryan, DMs into content:** When three or more people ask Ryan the same question in DMs, he turns it into content. His crash course came from that.
  <br>*Why it works:* Repeated questions are proof of demand in the audience's own words.
- **Dominic Bair, meeting transcripts:** Record the founder's meetings, remove private parts, and give the transcripts to the copywriter.
  <br>*Why it works:* It gives the writer real opinions and stories without extra time from the founder.

### Common mistakes

- Ideas scattered across apps you never open again (Koe).
- Studying hooks from average posts or a different format (Mino Lee only studies 50K+ like videos in his own format).
- Copying the content instead of the hook or format (Codie).
- Writing problems in your words instead of the audience's (Codie).

### Try it

Create one doc with three headings: hooks that worked (from others), questions people asked me, my real stories. Spend 10 minutes filling each with at least 3 entries, saving 3 to 5 bullets on why each saved hook worked.

## 9.4 Filtering ideas: funnels and kill meetings

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet: "the best creators aren't more creative, they kill bad ideas faster." Paddy Galloway's idea funnel and Sam's ideation meetings turn a big pile of ideas into the few worth making.

### Galloway's idea funnel

- Three sources feed it: internal ideas (what worked on your channel before), external ideas (what worked for competitors and other channels), and innovation (blue ocean: look across YouTube and other industries for something new).
- His illustrative numbers: start with 100 ideas. Fit with your core, casual and new audience (CCN) cuts about 40.
- Feasibility cuts about 20. "I swam covered in blood with 10 great white sharks" would go viral but might kill the creator. "Get MrBeast on the podcast" needs a contact, convincing and a bigger show first.
- Can you come up with the title and a rough thumbnail sketch right now? About half survive, leaving 20.
- Is the creator truly excited to make it? Even a view maximizer needs this, or they burn out. About 10 left.
- One-pager for each of the 10: rough thumbnail sketch, rough title, a "why" square (1 to 3 reasons it will work) and a log line. He claims he can do 10 in an hour or two; ideas drive views, so they deserve it.
- Show the one-pagers to employees, peers or friends ("here's the idea, here's the log line, what do you think?"). He even asks a brand-new editor. Without other opinions you are isolated and lead with ego. End with 3 to 4 ideas to develop. He calls the peer group a "YouTube council".

### Sam's ideation meetings

- Abundance: present 12 YouTube ideas, make 4. The other 8 were not wasted, you picked the best of the best.
- Every idea is pitched in 3 parts: topic (AI tools), angle (these AI tools will make you money), format (a tier list ranked S, A, B, C by how much money each makes you).

### Split ideas from execution

- A MrBeast employee asked Sam whether the person coming up with ideas was the same one making them. Sam said yes. The answer: "Don't do that", because you will pick the easiest thing to make, not the one that performs best.

### Give perfection a time limit

- Galloway: spend an hour or two on a title, not a week, then pick one and stick with it.
- Every video leaves views on the table; he has never got 100% of possible views. "Perfection needs to have a time limit."
- Small creators especially must publish, because time passing means less learning. His analogy: searching for the perfect spouse until you are 65.

### Frameworks

**Idea funnel** (Paddy Galloway)

1. Collect about 100 ideas from internal, external and innovation sources
2. Cut for audience fit (about 60 left)
3. Cut for feasibility (about 40 left)
4. Can you write a title and sketch a thumbnail now? (about 20 left)
5. Is the creator excited? (about 10 left)
6. One-pager each: thumbnail, title, why square, log line
7. Pitch to peers and pick 3 to 4

**The 3-part pitch** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Topic: the subject
2. Angle: the take on it
3. Format: the container it is delivered in

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, the AI tools pitch:** Topic: AI tools. Angle: these AI tools will make you money. Format: a tier list ranked by how much money each makes you.
  <br>*Why it works:* Pitching all three parts makes ideas easy to compare and kill.
- **Paddy Galloway, the shark idea:** Swimming covered in blood with 10 great white sharks would likely go viral, but fails the feasibility filter because it could kill the creator.
  <br>*Why it works:* Virality alone does not pass the funnel.

### Common mistakes

- Remaking one outlier instead of finding 20 to 30 topics and killing most (Sam).
- Brainstorming from zero inside the meeting (Sam).
- The same person picking and making ideas, so the easiest one wins (Sam, via a MrBeast employee).
- Deciding alone and leading with ego (Galloway).
- Overthinking a title for a week (Galloway).

### Try it

Write 20 ideas in 10 minutes. Then, in a separate sitting, run them through Galloway's filters (audience fit, feasibility, can I title it now, am I excited) and keep the best 3, each pitched as topic, angle and format.

## 9.5 Measuring what matters

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Views are a leading signal, not the goal. Sam Gaudet tracks 4 numbers, Matt Gray says cash in the bank is the ultimate content metric, and EXM7777 judges every channel on sales and signups.

### Sam's 4 metrics

- Views or impressions: the biggest leading indicator. Track it, do not optimize for it.
- Followers added: people saw you and liked what you said. Lots of views and no followers means people see you and do not like what you say.
- Leads: emails, SMS numbers, even addresses captured on every platform, moving people from earned traffic to owned traffic.
- Revenue generated: without it you cannot know the return on paying for a team.
- He tracks them daily with an automated Slack message showing yesterday's views, followers, leads and revenue, and tracks his and Dan's followers daily.
- Per piece of content he narrows it to views and followers added: "I ignore all the metrics" otherwise (50 Hacks).

### Look past vanity numbers

- Matt Gray: do not chase vanity metrics. Chasing likes and engagement pushes founders toward viral, beginner-level content. Cash in the bank or profit per month is the ultimate content metric.
- 0xfJuan: look past views to trials, DMs and calls. VibeMarketer: use metrics to diagnose, and track what people link you with (the association layer).
- Caleb Ralston: report work with data, for example a 2.5x or 3x outlier against the average video on TikTok or LinkedIn, engagement, average view duration, leads, and qualified leads that converted.
- Dan Koe: feedback from reality. If you are not growing or making money, reality says your actions are wrong or something is missing; learn, try new tactics daily, iterate.

### Decide with data, not feelings (Sam)

- At Sam's company anyone making a decision has to show him the data.
- His example: a new format got lots of views, but if you do not check how many followers it brought, you will chase views instead of followers. Know which number you are optimizing for.
- The skill is to collect data, clean it and decide from it.
- Clear ownership: scorecards, KPIs and an owner for each KPI. "You can only manage what you measure."

### Reading single posts

- Codie Sanchez: "the data isn't a judgment of your worth, it's a map." On X watch replies: a 1 to 3% reply rate is excellent (her figure).
- Paddy Galloway: use reflection prompts and a data checklist (demographics, device type, new vs returning viewers, view trend, traffic source) rather than a 3-second conclusion. Use first-hour and first-24-hour click-through rate as a predictor; ignore lifetime click-through rate.
- Logan Smith uses the audience-active times in YouTube Studio to choose posting times.
- MrBeast: study other creators' A/B thumbnail tests, check where a video ranks out of 10 for its channel, and watch title patterns above a views threshold.

### Frameworks

**The 4 metrics** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Views: leading indicator, track but do not optimize for it
2. Followers added: did they like what you said

## 9.6 Review rhythms: learning from your winners

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Data only helps if you look at it on a rhythm and turn it into instructions. Sam Gaudet's daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly reviews, and Kallaway's winners vs losers analysis, show how.

### Sam's reporting rhythm

- Daily: report the numbers on every platform (followers added). Owners post a daily update.
- Weekly: a scorecard with all the numbers and notes, reviewed in a weekly meeting.
- Monthly: review all content posted in the last month against its results, write a full report, review the strategy. Change things monthly, not daily, to avoid "knee-jerk reactions".
- Quarterly: plan which formats, angles and topics to test. Check whether positioning or content buckets need to change.
- Matt Gray runs a weekly "level 10 meeting" with the leadership team: scoreboard and key metrics first, then issues, opportunities, challenges and constraints.
- Caleb Ralston: a weekly call recapping content performance. If average view duration on YouTube is going up, you are doing the right things; if it is going down, rethink how scripts are structured.

### Kallaway's winners vs losers method

- Execution differs by niche ("whatever works for a dentist is not going to work for a financial adviser"), so use your own data. Not using your own data for topics, scripts and formats is "playing the game on hard mode."
- 1 Set the date range to only the period since you started using a framework (for example 2 months).
- 2 Sort videos by most views.
- 3 Get transcripts and stats for all of them, and break transcripts into script sections (over time these should map to the framework's steps).
- 4 Manual way: watch and read from the best performer down, asking what you did, and whether there is a pattern across the winners (for example in the reveal, the proof, the urgency).
- 5 AI way: AI is "extremely good at identifying patterns, arguably the best thing that it is at." Export the data (for example as CSV) into an LLM, ask it to compare the 10, 20 or 30 videos, find what the winners did differently in the storytelling, and give very specific instructions going forward. Also feed it the framework doc so it maps transcripts to the framework.
- Output: the words, sentence structure, grammar and topics that separate winners from losers. He says he does this every day.

### Other review habits

- Matt Gray: audit the top 20% vs bottom 20% of posts (topics, hooks, words). That is how he learned his audience wanted systems over mindset, and that specific numbers in hooks did "10x better" (his claim).
- Dan Koe: review your highest performing content monthly and rewrite the winners.
- Ryan: track metrics, review weekly, make 5 more versions of outliers, kill what is not working.
- Oren Meets World: check content pillars monthly. If saves, views and shares are going down, drop, replace or fix a pillar. Keep testing new things even when the old ones work: "it's not going to work forever."
- XCreators: connect analytics from every platform, see where retention drops and why one video has a higher skip or share rate, form hypotheses, and put each lesson straight into the next script. "Every video should make your next one better."

### Review as a system

- Kallaway reviews dozens to hundreds of business owners' videos weekly; the recurring hook mistakes he teaches (delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest) came out of that loop.
- Mino Lee: daily iteration with feedback, where members post content and get reviews from others, beats one big "unlock" class.
- Kallaway: always gut check a hook before moving on, and rework it if it is hard to understand.

### Frameworks

**Reporting rhythm** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Daily: numbers per platform
2. Weekly: scorecard and meeting
3. Monthly: content vs results, full report, strategy review
4. Quarterly: plan tests, check positioning and buckets

**Winners vs losers analysis** (Kallaway)

1. Limit the date range to the framework period
2. Sort by views
3. Pull transcripts and stats, split into script sections
4. Read from best to worst looking for patterns
5. Or give the data and framework doc to an AI and ask for specific instructions

### Examples

- **Matt Gray, top vs bottom 20%:** Comparing his best and worst posts showed his audience wanted systems over mindset, and that hooks with specific numbers did about 10x better.
  <br>*Why it works:* Comparing extremes shows the pattern faster than looking at averages.

### Common mistakes

- Changing strategy daily from knee-jerk reactions (Sam).
- Using other niches' rules instead of your own data (Kallaway).
- Reviewing without writing instructions for the next piece (Kallaway, XCreators).
- Relying on old winners forever without testing (Oren).

### Where the sources disagree

## 9.7 Simplify before you automate

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet uses Elon Musk's 5-step routine on content: question, delete, simplify, speed up, and only then automate. Automating an unfinished process just repeats the mess faster.

### Elon's 5 steps, with Sam's examples

- 1 Question every requirement: if it has been done that way for 5 years, ask why (especially with AI now). Sam asked why color grade and why fancy gear, and went iPhone-only.
- 2 Delete parts: delete until it breaks, then add back. "If things don't break when you delete them, you haven't deleted enough." Sam deleted everything, found bad audio does matter, and added a cheap mic.
- 3 Simplify and optimize, only after deleting, and combine work. Short-form Saturdays and long-form Sundays became all on Saturday. Dan's separate editor, project manager, copywriter and designer became fewer roles (the editor also does thumbnails and copy). Every extra person is "another communication line to manage."
- 4 Speed up the cycle: do it more often so you learn faster. Content went from weekly to twice a week, editing from 3 hours to 1, and he ideates almost daily.
- 5 Automate last, not first. Automating an unfinished process gives you "AI slop" on repeat. Automate the 5-step content flow once it exists.
- "Simple scales" is one of Martell Media's core values: making things complex is easy, making them simple is hard.

### Find the one constraint

- Sam's theory of constraints example: a car factory makes 10 bodies, 10 engines and 4 wheels a day. It is limited by wheels, so time spent on more engines is wasted.
- Find the one constraint in your content engine and only work on that.

### Protect focused time

- Matt Gray's claim (credited to Shopify founder Tobi Lutke): most CEOs can do their core work in four focused hours a day.
- Split them into 1 strategic decisions, 2 content creation and systems, 3 team growth and strategy, 4 revenue generation.
- Batch them at the right time of day. His is 7am to 11am, uninterrupted, or "I'm probably screwed that day."
- Revenue deserves about an hour of daily attention or something blows up in sales or customer success (Gray).
- Ryan: overload your calendar, because work expands to fill the time you give it.

### Obsess over volume and speed

- Sam: also obsess over volume and speed. If an edit takes 10 days, push it to 5.
- Sam: "volume negates luck" is one of his lines; speed lets you learn faster.

### Frameworks

**5-step optimization routine** (Elon Musk, as used by Sam Gaudet)

1. Question every requirement
2. Delete parts until something breaks, then add back
3. Simplify and optimize what is left
4. Speed up the cycle
5. Automate last

**Four focused hours** (Matt Gray (credited to Tobi Lutke))

1. Strategic decision-making
2. Content creation and systems
3. Team growth and strategy
4. Revenue generation

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, deleting until it broke:** Sam dropped colour grading and fancy gear and went iPhone-only. When he deleted everything, bad audio turned out to matter, so he added back a cheap mic.
  <br>*Why it works:* Deleting too much shows you what is truly needed.
- **Sam Gaudet, the wheel factory:** A factory making 10 bodies, 10 engines and 4 wheels a day can only build 1 car per 4 wheels. More engines do nothing.
  <br>*Why it works:* Work only on the step that limits output.

### Common mistakes

- Automating before the process works, which repeats "AI slop" (Sam).
- Simplifying before deleting (Sam).
- Adding people, and with them communication lines, instead of cutting steps (Sam).
- Improving steps that are not the constraint (Sam).

## 9.8 AI as a helper: speed plus taste

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** AI is fast and average by default. Shann Holmberg says AI is the production engine and taste is the steering wheel; Sam Gaudet says augment people, do not replace them; Dan Koe says never expect AI to hand you the perfect idea.

### Why AI defaults to generic

- Shann: LLMs are probability machines that default to the most common patterns.
- He reports an Amplifying.ai study: Claude Code on 2,430 real repos with open questions picked shadcn/ui 90.1% of the time for UI, Tailwind 68.4%, Vercel 100% for JavaScript deployment, Zustand over Redux every time and Express zero times. Great tools, but when 90% accept the same defaults, 90% of products look the same.
- EXM7777: ask AI for a viral post with nothing else and you get "the average of everything it has read, and the average is boring."
- Shann: AI gets you about 60% of the way fast. Most people call it done, so "60% is now the new mediocre."
- Shann: avoid "build it in ONE prompt" tutorials. "One-prompt wonders produce one-prompt results."

### Shann's 80/20: 80% AI, 20% taste

- Let AI do research, first drafts, boilerplate, structure, formatting and speed. The last 20% is yours: what stays and what is cut, rewriting the safe hook, swapping default parts, applying what you know is good in your field.
- Most people invert it: 80% of their energy on prompting and tweaking, running the same prompt fifteen times, and almost no time on judgment. "Productivity without quality is just motion."
- The difference is what happens after the AI finishes and before a human hits publish.
- Leon Lin's "taste skill" (via Shann): about 400 tokens of explicit rules (specific fonts instead of Inter or Roboto, specific colours instead of blue-purple, rules for motion, composition and backgrounds) and, critically, a "what to avoid" list that stops the AI falling back to defaults.
- "AI is a production engine. Taste is the steering wheel." (Shann)

### Sam's split: augment, do not replace

- The industry joke: "I replaced my whole content team with AI." Reply: "we can tell." The worst case he has seen is CEOs posting HeyGen AI avatars and expecting them to beat themselves on camera.
- First 10% (discovery, finding outliers) uses AI tools they built, because they were spending 5 to 10 hours a week on ideation. Middle 80% (scripting, shooting, editing) is real people. Last 10%: AI reviews and points out blind spots.
- Sam writes most of his scripts by hand. Use AI to widen options (for example 50 angles to choose from), not to write.
- Automate last: AI added before a system exists "will just default to whatever the internet says", which he says is mostly wrong.
- Ryan: use AI for research and ideation, not for 100% of your copy. He says 90%+ of his article copy is still written by hand, and his writer bot "actually wasn't that good, I still prefer to write copy manually." AI will not replace taste but takes the grunt work: research, ideas, first drafts, analytics, tracking.

### Useful ways to prompt

- Jenny Hoyos (V.I.R.A.L., via Philipp Humm's interview): when you get ideas from AI, give it the hook shape ("is it possible to...", "what happens if...") or it hands you flat statements. Collect 100 ideas, pick the 1 that fits the framework.
- Dan Koe's prompts: break down why a post worked and how to repeat it; pull your story and beliefs into one document; list subtopics for a broad topic (but write your own first); guide you through a content map by pasting in the article; research viral posts as product angles.
- Koe: "Don't expect any AI to give you the perfect idea." Treat the output as inspiration, have a conversation, and steer it toward what feels true to you.
- Koe's micro product prompt: "Research top performing YouTube videos in [topic], specifically ones that help people improve their lives in some way. Then, help me brainstorm my own unique product idea based around that." His example: a "30 Day Life Reset" where an AI interviews the buyer and builds a custom plan.
- Koe's micro service prompt: outline a pack of 4 one-on-one calls priced at \$500 to \$1,000; have the AI interview you one question at a time, then write where clients are now, a 1-month outcome, the steps grouped into 4 calls, homework, 5 onboarding questions and 3 name ideas.
- heyDominik uses AI image generation to mock up a better first frame before filming.

### Frameworks

**The new 80/20** (Shann Holmberg)

1. 80% AI: research, drafts, structure, formatting, speed
2. 20% taste: cut, rewrite the hook, swap defaults, apply domain judgment

**AI in the content flow** (Sam Gaudet)

1. First 10%: AI for discovery and outliers
2. Middle 80%: people script, shoot and edit
3. Last 10%: AI reviews for blind spots

### Examples

- **Leon Lin's taste skill (via Shann Holmberg):** A 400-token rule file naming exact fonts, colours, motion and composition rules, plus a list of what to avoid.
  <br>*Why it works:* The avoid list is what stops the AI sliding back to the average default.
- **Sam Gaudet, 50 angles:** Sam uses AI to produce many angles on a topic, then picks one and writes the script himself.
  <br>*Why it works:* AI widens options; the human chooses and writes.

### Common mistakes

- One-prompt content with no context (Shann, EXM7777).
- Stopping at the AI's 60% draft (Shann).
- Spending your energy on re-prompting instead of judgment (Shann).
- Replacing the person on camera with an AI avatar (Sam).
- Expecting AI to hand you the perfect idea (Koe).

### Where the sources disagree

How much AI should do: Shann Holmberg says 80% AI, 20% taste. Sam Gaudet keeps the middle 80% (scripting, shooting, editing) with real people and uses AI only at the start and end, and Ryan writes 90%+ of his article copy by hand. They agree the human judgment at the end decides quality.

## 9.10 Team levels: who to hire and when

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet's 5 levels of a personal brand team show how the team changes as the audience grows, from solo creator to separate media and marketing. Matt Gray, domschats and Ryan add their own versions of the roles.

### Sam's 5 levels (followers across all platforms)

- Level 1, solo creator (0 to 100K): no team. Make the best content yourself, set a cadence you can sustain, get reps. "You can't learn to surf from a book."
- Level 2, content manager (100K to 500K): hire one creative director or content manager who approves everything. The founder never looks at another video.
- Level 3, channel owners (500K to 1M): the content manager cannot be the bottleneck. One owner per platform, or per pair of similar platforms.
- Level 4, split ideation from production (1M to 3M): the people with the ideas are not the people who make them. Weekly ideation meetings.
- Level 5, separate media from marketing (3M to 10M): media grows the audience, marketing makes the money.

### The content manager's 5 jobs (Sam)

- 1 Ideation: owns 100% of topics, angles, scripts and frameworks, pulled from the founder's own views.
- 2 Capture: the one holding the camera. 3 Production: cleans it up for every platform. 4 Distribution: gets it on every platform. 5 Analytics review: runs the reporting rhythm.
- Let them own it. Dan Martell's brief to Sam: "here are my social platforms, you figure it out."
- His claim: 90% of CEOs get stuck here because they cannot find someone they trust.
- His story: Dan called Sam in 2023 to go all in. Sam moved 4,000 km, set up a studio, and they started with 2 people (Sam and one editor). It grew to 17 full-time.

### Channel owners

- Sam: one person runs each platform daily: ideas, production, distribution, analytics, notes on what to change.
- Start by pairing similar channels. Sam's first 4 owners: Instagram + Facebook; YouTube Shorts + TikTok; YouTube long form; LinkedIn + Twitter + email. Today one owner per platform.
- Each owner gets clear weekly KPIs and owns the calendar, topics and packaging. If they miss KPIs, the content lead has a serious talk. "The result on the other end is their doing, not yours."
- Sam's view: with AI, one person per platform can run the whole cycle in 40 hours a week.
- Caleb Ralston's platform ownership: instead of three editors all making Instagram content, give one person Instagram as their full-time job, including ideas and strategy. You stay a sounding board and focus on how that platform fits the whole brand, where you have the most context. Nobody is the expert on every platform; it is "pretty audacious" to think you should run everything.
- Ryan: teams now hire a head per platform (Instagram, YouTube, X).

### Other team maps

- Sam's founder phases: phase 1 a VA and a video editor (where Sam is with his own brand; he writes scripts himself). Phase 2 a creative director or content manager who owns the engine; hire them as soon as possible, ideally in the first year, so you are not the bottleneck. Phase 3 workflow owners (production, Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn, email, SMS, ads).
- Matt Gray's team multiplication system, built around each stage from content to revenue: creators (writers for X and LinkedIn, video editors for YouTube, Instagram, TikTok); distributors (the social media manager, the "chief distribution officer"); strategists (data, titles, packaging, thumbnails, conversion sources, sales team input on what to make next, shoot planning).
- Gray: do not hand it all off and ignore it. The founder stays as "chief detail officer": final green light on topics and a veto on what does not fit the brand.
- domschats' team evolution: founder as face of the brand (phase 1), then a copywriter or Head of Content, a short-form editor and a creative director (phase 2), then pods of 10 to 20 per channel (phase 3).
- Ryan: lean teams with 3 key roles and automations for most of the rest. A solid content team costs about \$40k a month (his number). His "follow-the-sun" idea: drafts reviewed in Australia's morning, visuals in Asia, edits in Europe, writing and posting in the US.
- Tristan Kermode: at Iman's peak, about 30 core team and about 150 in the creative team, but "more didn't always equal better"; a smaller team performed better.
- Paddy Galloway built a small team of strategists; young strategists are often very good but do not understand limits and feasibility. Creators can hire editors and thumbnail designers earlier now, but should still have the basic skills, or a team member covering each one. Noah Kagan had the foundations first (team, editing, thumbnails) before his topic strategy changed.

### Specialists or many roles?

- Sam (10M Followers): split the team into short form, long form and "word" (tweets, LinkedIn, email) so people become specialists.
- Sam (50 Hacks, No Time): train each person for several roles. With AI the thumbnail designer can also edit and schedule. "One role per person" is a mistake he sees in media companies; fewer roles means fewer communication lines.
- Both are his. The overlap: clear owners, but each owner can do more with AI.

### Frameworks

**5 levels of a personal brand team** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Solo creator, 0 to 100K
2. Content manager, 100K to 500K
3. Channel owners, 500K to 1M
4. Split ideation from production, 1M to 3M
5. Separate media from marketing, 3M to 10M

**Team multiplication system** (Matt Gray)

1. Creators: writers and editors
2. Distributors: the chief distribution officer
3. Strategists: data, packaging, what to make next
4. Founder: chief detail officer with final green light

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, the first 4 channel owners:** Instagram + Facebook, YouTube Shorts + TikTok, YouTube long form, and LinkedIn + Twitter + email. Later each platform got its own owner.
  <br>*Why it works:* Pairing similar platforms lets a small team cover everything before it can afford one owner each.

### Common mistakes

- The founder or content manager becoming the bottleneck that approves everything (Sam).
- Never finding someone to trust, so the brand stalls at level 2 (Sam's claim: 90% of CEOs).
- Handing everything off and ignoring it (Gray).
- Assuming a bigger team is always better (Tristan).
- Hiring before you have basic skills to judge the work (Galloway).

### Where the sources disagree

How involved the founder stays: Sam's level 2 says the founder never looks at another video, and Dan's brief was "you figure it out". Matt Gray says the founder must stay as chief detail officer with the final green light on topics. Team size also splits: Sam (17), Caleb (18) and domschats (pods of 10 to 20) built big teams, while Ryan argues for lean teams of 3 key roles and Tristan saw a smaller team perform better. Sam himself teaches both specialist splits and one person in many roles.

## 9.11 Hiring and running team operations

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Hire for taste and potential through paid tests, then run the team on one hub, clear definitions of done and quality, fast reviews and frequent touch points. Ryan, Tristan Kermode, Sam Gaudet and Ernesto each describe how.

### Hiring

- Ryan: your team is your ceiling. Hire for taste and potential, train for skill. Always run a paid test task. One A-player beats 3 B-players.
- Ryan in the AI era: he asks "how good were they before AI came out", says his company spends five figures a month on tokens, and will not hire someone unwilling to learn AI tools.
- Sam: hire editors, designers and copywriters through test projects, hold the standard, run clear one-on-ones and deadlines, and hold people to numbers. Move good people up and others down or off the team.
- Sam: at scale you "hire someone who could hire someone".
- Sam: "the best personal brand team is built, not bought." Dan did not find Sam "in some magic pond"; he hired a hungry kid without the playbooks and invested in him.
- Caleb: if you do not trust your team to decide for you, maybe you hired wrong or lack good onboarding and training. Audit why.

### Tristan Kermode's ops system (from Ryan's interview)

- One central hub (Notion), which is "only as good as the team using it", with a view showing each editor's % completion.
- Slack for day-to-day, WhatsApp for urgent.
- Several touch points a week (from 3 meetings a day to 3 every other day). One weekly call is "fighting an uphill battle".
- Everyone knows who to go to when something breaks, so the head of content stops putting out fires.
- A clear "definition of done" and "definition of quality", then editor, peer review, founder review, and version 3 ships. This fixed a client's review loop that had stalled for months.
- Fast feedback ("speed to review"). Long 1-on-1 calls early on pay off later.

### Run the work on paper

- Ryan: SOPs for ideation, writing, editing, packaging and posting, plus a voice doc and a swipe file; the pipeline from ideas to production, review, publish and report on a kanban board.
- Sam: production's only job is to help the viewer understand. Any production that does not is wasted, and the best editing and design often go unnoticed because they do not distract.
- Sam's record and direct: for short-form shoots, sit down with all the short-form scripts and use question-based prompting (ask the talent questions to lead them) instead of giving lines or a teleprompter.
- Caleb's tools: frame.io for notes on videos, Google Drive and a shared slide deck for hackathon entries, Zoom, Slack for owning flops publicly.

### Ernesto's UGC content machine

- He hired 60+ creators at once through a creator marketplace (about \$299/month), with no calls and no portfolios, just the brief.
- He spent close to \$100,000 in 3 months, his claim of about a 5x return. He fired 5 to 10 creators a week until 15 "killers" remained; top performers show themselves when formats are proven and instructions are followed.
- His claim: 100M+ views from that team and 150M views in 3 months overall.
- Beginner version: 1 to 2 creators at about \$600/month each, fire quickly, have replacements ready.
- Hand creators everything: daily scripts, all assets, examples from other creators, a content calendar. "Your creators don't owe you anything, you owe them everything"; your job is to find viral formats and show exactly how to copy them.
- Culture keeps creators: he says studios with \$100M+ funding tried to poach them with more pay, and they stayed.

### Check below your managers (Caleb)

- When scaling, Caleb handed management to managers he trusted and walked away with no formal check-in. Some gave poor feedback, came late to 1:1s or were not present. Issues stayed hidden until boiling, and he lost trust with people he had promised it would be great.
- Fix: skip-level 1:1s. Meet the people below your managers and ask what their managers do well, what confuses them and what is not going well. Filter natural complainers, but act on red flags ("my manager never shows up to my 1:1s").
- Loyal teams will not "rat" on a manager, so earn trust through regular check-ins; bi-weekly or monthly is enough. New hires under managers have fewer touchpoints with you, so build trust before they would ever raise an issue.
- Pro tip: managers who only report good news are a red flag; go a layer deeper. Managers who tell you about weird interactions or questions they could not answer are the ones not to worry about.
- Over-reliance on top-down feedback creates blind spots.

### Frameworks

**Review chain** (Tristan Kermode (via Ryan))

1. Define done and define quality
2. Editor makes version 1
3. Peer review
4. Founder review
5. Version 3 ships

### Examples

- **Tristan Kermode, the stalled review loop:** A client's review loop had stalled for months. Writing a definition of done and of quality, then a fixed editor, peer, founder chain where version 3 ships, fixed it.
  <br>*Why it works:* Clear standards and a fixed number of rounds stop endless revisions.
- **Ernesto, 60 creators to 15:** He hired 60+ UGC creators with only a brief, fired 5 to 10 a week, and kept 15 top performers.
  <br>*Why it works:* With proven formats and clear instructions, the best performers show themselves fast.

### Common mistakes

- Hiring without a paid test task (Ryan, Sam).
- One weekly call as the only touch point (Tristan).
- No definition of done or quality, so reviews loop forever (Tristan).
- Blaming editors when the system is the problem (Sam).
- Delegating to managers and never checking below them (Caleb).
- Giving creators a vague brief instead of scripts, assets and examples (Ernesto).

### Where the sources disagree

Meetings: Tristan Kermode wants several touch points a week and calls one weekly call an uphill battle, while Caleb Ralston warns that every 30-minute meeting can cost a creative 1.5 hours and protects maker days with no meetings. Both want fast feedback; they differ on how much meeting time it takes.

## 9.12 Lead creatives, do not control them

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Caleb Ralston says the more you control the process, the more you stifle ideas. Leaders set the vision and the what, and let the team own the how.

### Who is teaching (Caleb's claims)

- Caleb Ralston says he built an in-house media team from zero to 18 full-time people in two and a half years, producing 350 pieces of content a week. He says that brought "billions and billions of impressions every single year" and that he made 5,000+ videos himself. He worked at VaynerMedia and at Logos Bible Software.
- His opening line: your media team probably does not hate deadlines, data and structure. They hate how you are managing them.
- His course has three parts: mindset shifts, a creative leadership philosophy, and a tactical blueprint for training and leading, then how to be a leader worth following.

### Shift 1: training is not about control

- Caleb: the more you control the process, the more you stifle creativity and innovation.
- His story: a brilliant manager came up with every long form idea, did the research, title, thumbnail and outline, and pitched everything. Results were good at first, then the team plateaued. No new ideas, only slight iterations, and numbers dropped.
- Caleb told him to pitch no new ideas for two weeks and teach the team to do the brief, concept, title, thumbnail and outline. The result was many new formats that worked, a team that could now do it, and a manager freed for bigger picture projects.
- Caleb does not believe in a fully top-down strategy. He believes in equipping the team for a bottom-up one.
- The qualifier: this is not "Kumbaya by the fire" and not every idea is good. Some people pitched every time and worked hard, but ideas were not their skill (they were a better videographer or editor). Know each person's strengths, use them, and do not waste time on weaknesses. Not everyone should contribute ideas, but enable the ones who are good at it.
- The top-down manager thinks their role is to control. Caleb: the leader's job is to empower and equip the team to do things on your behalf. Alone you cannot scale, so build a system, process and culture that amplify you.
- Make it safe to keep pitching, even after one idea is rejected. People should come back again and again.
- Nobody is the subject matter expert on every platform. Caleb calls it "pretty audacious" to think you should run everything.

### Shift 2: managing is not leading

- Caleb: managing means reaching the goal by telling people exactly what to do. Leading means casting a big vision, being clear on deadlines and expectations, sharing the mission, vision and values of the project, and giving people tools to take ownership instead of just doing tasks.
- His story: a high urgency, high importance, high risk campaign for a large platform company. His instinct was to control the concept, shot list and script. Instead he brought in his creative director, director of photography and lead editor, shared the vision, importance, budget and timeline, and let them decide the concept, shot list, gear and talent.
- Result, in his words: "a far better piece of creative than I ever would have come up with on my own". People commented on the ads asking for more ads.
- He still checked in along the way (for example, "did we factor in the wind?") but did not micromanage. The message to the team was: I trust you.
- Caleb's phrase: "checking on, not checking in". Ask how they are doing, the project status, problems and roadblocks you can remove. Do not ask about the lighting, the framing or the frame rate.

### Shift 3: from doer to leader is an identity shift

- Caleb calls this possibly the most difficult. You have to change where you get your sense of value. At VaynerMedia he was known as a high output individual contributor (8 shorts in a day, a video edited in 24 hours). As a leader his output became the team's output and the shoutouts stopped. He felt down for a while.
- His reframe: take internal credit when your team gets the shoutouts, because you built the environment that allows it.
- Let go of perfectionism and rely on coaching, mentoring and training. People will not do it your way. On creative choices he calls most of it "subjective" and unless he has a strong preference he defaults to his editor's choice.
- Caleb's tactic: end each day by asking not "what did I get done?" but "what did my team get done, what roadblocks did I knock out of their way, what context did I give that led to a breakthrough?"

### Shift 4: ownership grows people

- Caleb's story: he was reviewing every short form video, a couple of hundred a week, which became absurd. An editor, Trevor, was emerging as a leader who understood shorts as well or better and could train others. Caleb delegated short form review to him.
- Trevor's engagement rose, he became a leader, and a few months later he was promoted to manager with reports at 22 years old. Caleb: "The more ownership you give to the right individuals the more they will give you in return." It also freed Caleb to think 3, 6, 12 and 36 months ahead.
- Ownership can be chunked up or down: a whole campaign, a whole shoot, or just the sound (watching audio levels) or the lighting.
- Tactic: have the team come up with strategies, the game plan and the agenda and bring them to you for review. Caleb calls that a perfect moment for informal training.

### Shift 5: creatives do care about business results

- Caleb names this a myth: that creatives only care about artistic integrity, not conversions or revenue. His view: they care deeply once they are aligned and know how their work ladders up. If they do not care, you probably have not shown them how their work drives revenue.
- His story: a company pushed monthly seminars. Before there was reporting, the team did not care. For a documentary piece, they first worked with marketing and sales to add tracking from organic content to sales. The video sold out a whole month of seminars. The editor felt hugely fulfilled and the rest of the team started thinking about how their content could drive seminar sales.
- His claim: "99% of you" only report platform analytics, not the bigger business picture.
- Tactic: share the data. Show how organic content drives profitability and how paid campaigns raised sales, so the team thinks about outcomes and not only likes, views and comments.

### Caleb's five leadership principles

- 1 Lead with empathy, not authority, and balance structure and freedom. Set clear expectations and allow autonomy inside them. "You define the what and let your team define the how."
- 2 Encourage ownership and agency: "don't build a checklist army, build an army of problem solvers." Assign ownership of key projects, let the creative set the goal, strategy and solutions, and you guide.
- 3 Operate in humility and give credit to the team. Credit hoarders stifle motivation. Praise publicly on team calls and give constructive criticism privately.
- 4 Less debating, more doing. When Caleb and a creative disagree, unless it hurts the brand he goes with their idea, because his view is likely subjective, then sees what the audience thinks. "Was I wrong? More often than not, yes." He still states his opinion on record, so if he turns out right the team trusts his opinion more next time. A/B test both ideas instead of debating and use the results for future decisions.
- 5 Love data, do not fear it. Many creatives distrust data because it was only used to tell them what they did wrong. Be the one who shows data is empowering, for example with a weekly call recapping watch time and average view duration trends and what they mean for script structure.

### Frameworks

**Caleb's managing vs leading** (Caleb Ralston)

1. Cast the vision and share the mission, budget, timeline and why it matters
2. Let the people closest to the craft decide the how
3. Check on how they are doing and what blocks them, not on the lighting or the framing
4. State your opinion on record, then A/B test instead of debating
5. Share business data so the team sees how the work ladders up

### Examples

- **Caleb Ralston, the manager who pitched everything:** One manager came up with every idea and the team plateaued. Caleb made him stop pitching for two weeks and teach the team to pitch.
  <br>*Why it works:* Result: new formats that worked, a team that could do it and a freed manager.
- **Caleb Ralston, the big campaign:** Instead of writing the concept and shot list, he gave the creative director, camera lead and editor the vision and budget and let them decide.
  <br>*Why it works:* The ads were so good that people commented asking for more ads.

### Common mistakes

- Pitching every idea yourself and calling it leadership (Caleb).
- Checking lighting and framing instead of checking on the person.
- Asking everyone to pitch ideas when ideas are not their skill (Caleb).
- Only reporting platform analytics and never the business result.

### Where the sources disagree

Control versus freedom: Caleb says the leader defines the what and the team defines the how, and goes with the creative's idea when it is subjective. Sam Gaudet, in the same module, says he critiques the keyframes in his editors' videos because he cares more about the outcome than their feelings. Caleb also says to let go of perfectionism. Both run big teams, so the line sits between what must be exact and what is taste.

## 9.13 Safe to fail: flops, hackathons and training

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Caleb Ralston says fear kills creativity and honesty. He builds a culture where flops are celebrated and trains the team by doing, not by lecture.

### Fear is the silent killer

- Caleb: leaders who use fear and micromanagement to force compliance get the exact opposite.
- When creatives fear failure, they stick to the same thing, the work looks uninspired and engagement drops. Build a culture where failure is a stepping stone, not a career-ending mistake.
- Fear also kills transparency. Afraid people still fail but hide it, and you want to know about big failures early, not when the project is done.
- Many leaders accept experiments only when they win. If a failed experiment brings disappointment, people stop experimenting and make "safe" content they know works. No innovation.
- If he had micromanaged his big campaign, Caleb says, the result would have been worse, the team would have felt he does not trust them, and they would have made "the best work to please Caleb".

### "We love a good flop"

- Caleb's saying: "we love a good flop". It tells talent and team that it is okay to experiment and fail. Everyone in leadership must buy in.
- He models it. He is the first to post in the team chat "this one flopped big time" about his own ideas. His team started calling out their own flops, for example an editor who spent a full day on one high energy short with graphics, sound design and music that flopped.
- No finger pointing ("you didn't get a good clip") and no blame. Morale went up and the team got tighter.
- The limit: this is not a week of flops. Repeated underperformance is something to investigate. Performance matters and data tracking stays.

### Content hackathons (Caleb's method)

- A hackathon is a whole day devoted to making things the team does not normally make. The goal is innovation, not performance. It is a way to run the 10% experiments of his 70/20/10 rule.
- His first version had a misaligned incentive. The team posted everything over the week and the winner had the most impressions in the first 48 hours, with a \$150 to \$200 gift card prize. That rewarded safety: competitive people made what they thought would get impressions, not the most innovative thing.
- How he runs it now: usually a Thursday or Friday, quarterly (monthly is hard for high volume teams). A fully blocked day with no normal projects.
- About 10 days before, message the team to reschedule meetings and clear their workload with their manager, "almost like it's a vacation". Letting one or two people work on tomorrow's edit during the hackathon creates a strange dynamic and signals it is not a priority.
- The Monday huddle explains what it is and the vision. The pitch: make what you have wanted to make but feared because you did not know how it would perform. "This could be our lowest performer ever, we might not even post it, and that's fine."
- The day: 9:00 a.m. kickoff call going over the rules, then 9 to 4 (7 hours) to work on anything. He sends food gift cards or orders lunch so nobody thinks about lunch.
- At 4 p.m. everyone stops creating and has 15 minutes to upload to Google Drive and embed in a shared slide deck, one slide per person.
- Each person presents their piece and explains why it is a compelling new form or format. It is also valuable when someone explains "three quarters of the way through I realized this wouldn't work" and what they learned.
- The winner is chosen by an anonymous live team vote on the most innovative and experimental piece that is still relevant to the brand (an educational tax brand does not need an unrelated music video). Peers know best what is different from their usual work.
- Team members often have an "itch" they are afraid to scratch. The hackathon removes the lingering thought.

### Feedback loops and training

- Real-time feedback loop: give feedback throughout, not only at the end. Caleb used to give total autonomy with no check-ins and kept finding big issues near the final edit. On a sensitive four-week vlog he set four weekly check-ins and in week 2 caught a moment the talent would not want, which the editor was making the crux of the story. His lesson: more frequent check-ins.
- Hands-on training beats passive presentations. Creatives learn best by doing, and 15 to 20 minutes into a Zoom training they squirm. A workshop works: give a team of short form editors the same footage, then go through each edit one by one, with everyone saying what was good, what could change and what was missed.
- Do not answer problems immediately. Ask "how would you approach this?" If they say "I don't know, that's why I'm asking", ask what they would do if you were on vacation or got hit by a bus. Instead of "use this transition", ask "what transition pairs scene one with scene two best?" This builds problem solving and confidence, and they start bringing solutions, not problems.
- Give others a chance to lead: a monthly team training where a rotating team member gives a 30 minute talk on something they love and are expert in (storytelling, sound design, motion graphics). They learn more by teaching, the team levels up, you find hidden skills (people often do not tell leadership what is in their "utility belt") and you invest in their communication skills.
- Caleb's claim: communication is the most important creative skill. "Creative is a commodity at this point, there's a million incredible editors better than you", but if you are better at communicating your ability to craft a story, you win.
- If someone says "I don't do public speaking", do not allow opting out, but give ample notice (a week is "awful"). Do not remove their fear for them, train them: show how you craft a talk and how many times you rehearse. Do it 20 times and you are less nervous.

### Frameworks

**Caleb's content hackathon** (Caleb Ralston)

1. Quarterly, a full blocked day with no normal projects
2. 10 days ahead: clear workloads and explain the goal is innovation, not performance
3. 9 a.m. kickoff, 7 hours to make anything
4. 4 p.m. upload to a shared deck, one slide per person
5. Each person presents, then an anonymous team vote on the most innovative piece

### Examples

- **Caleb Ralston, the flopped short:** An editor spent a full day on a high energy short with graphics, sound design and music. It flopped, and the editor called it out in the team chat.
  <br>*Why it works:* Caleb modeled it first by announcing his own flops.
- **Caleb Ralston, the four week vlog:** Four weekly check-ins caught in week 2 a moment the talent would not want as the crux of the story.
  <br>*Why it works:* Without the check-in the problem would have shown up at the near-final edit.

### Common mistakes

- Rewarding the most impressions in an innovation contest (Caleb's first version).
- Letting people work on normal projects during the hackathon.
- Pointing fingers at a flop.
- One big review at the end of a sensitive project.
- Long passive presentations as training.

## 9.14 Being a leader worth following

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Caleb Ralston says a team follows a leader who gives credit, shields them, cares about them as people and lets them grow without forcing them into management.

### Brand your team inside the company

- Caleb: "make them the gangsters, not you". Leaders give credit and make the team look better. Hype them in company-wide huddles and in 1:1s with the CEO or founder.
- Do not say "my team is killing it". Name who made what impact. His example: "Sarah went above and beyond on this edit, we had last minute revisions Thursday night for a Friday morning upload, she didn't complain, got it done, one of our greatest videos, far less handholding."
- Otherwise executives with little visibility see them as names on a page, "another cell in Google Sheets". It is your job, since you meet the CEO more often.

### Be their advocate and shield

- At Logos Bible Software, Caleb says, his creative director absorbed all the candid executive feedback. None reached Caleb as the editor, while counterparts elsewhere had CEOs yelling at them.
- Executives are not sinister. They have high expectations, move fast and communicate their own way. Absorb the tough feedback and relay it in a way that excites and empowers rather than frightens.
- Caleb's observation: creatives thrive with consistency and psychological safety. Constant pivots or aggressive pushback cause whiplash and disengagement. Tough feedback is fine, mean delivery is far less effective.
- His tactic: if an executive walks into the edit bay with an aggressive tone, say "can we walk to the break room to talk about this video?" That creates space so the creative spends energy on the work and not on "am I going to get fired?".

### Care beyond the work

- Caleb: people do their best work when valued as humans. Pay attention to what they care about. If they love pickleball, buy a racket or a coffee gift card for a birthday or work anniversary.
- For sick days he sends soup, bread or juice if budget allows, or a remedy by message. It removes the anxiety, especially for remote workers, that the boss thinks they are slacking. Payoff: excitement, loyalty, longevity and continuity.
- Empathy as one of his three principles: earn respect, do not demand it. Be intentional with check-ins, not only about deadlines but about how they are doing, what they struggle with, what they enjoy and want more of, and how things are outside work (within boundaries). A chippy person may have had a rough morning. That is not an excuse, but it is room for empathy.
- His story: a team member told him about a death in the family and said they needed no time off. Caleb said they should take time off, unless continuing to work was how they processed. He sat with them in the office for two hours, no work.
- You must actually care, he says, because faked care is sniffed out. Against what he calls the "anti-empathy movement": high turnover is lost productivity and continuity improves the bottom line.

### Elevate without forcing management

- Many creatives (designers, editors, motion designers, videographers, audio engineers) want more ownership, autonomy and responsibility in their lane, not management. Give them a seat at the table: ownership of post-production from ingest to upload, or writing the editing playbook for onboarding new editors.
- Offer two career tracks: an individual contributor path (for example video editor level 1 to 5, like developers) and a management path. In most organizations the only route to a raise is management. Two tracks give a road map without forcing 1:1s on people who hate meetings.
- His example: an editor kept bringing process refinements, not shiny ideas. They realized he did not want management. He liked being the go-to person for processes and playbooks. He became senior editor, built all post processes, oversaw edits and took first-pass reviews. Caleb: "Leadership is not just about who manages people, it's about who sets the standard and who influences the team."

### Lead by example and be transparent

- Lead by example: show, do not tell. During a two-month stretch when Caleb was checked out and lazier, the team made simple careless mistakes too. His youth pastor told the worship band: people will not do what you do, they will do what you do "times 10". Show up 5 to 10 minutes late and the team may show up 30 to 60 minutes late.
- Transparency is your biggest asset. Leaders who pretend to have all the answers create disconnect. Nobody respects a manager who covers a mistake by claiming it was intentional.
- Caleb aims to be the first to call out his own mistakes. If a project underperforms, do not shift blame: analyze, learn, apply it next time and share the learnings with the team.

### Caleb's three closing questions

- Am I empowering and leading my team or just managing them?
- Am I creating a culture where people feel safe to take risks?
- Am I leading by example and holding myself to the standard I hold the team to?
- His close: leadership is not about control, it is about creating the right conditions for the team to thrive. The team needs clarity, trust and space. Great leadership is constant iteration.

### Frameworks

**Three principles of a leader worth following** (Caleb Ralston)

1. Lead with empathy: earn respect, do not demand it
2. Lead by example: the team does what you do, times 10
3. Be transparent: own your mistakes first and share the learnings

### Examples

- **Caleb Ralston, naming Sarah:** Instead of saying his team is killing it, he tells the CEO exactly what one editor did and how she handled last minute revisions.
  <br>*Why it works:* Named credit makes people visible, not just names on a page.
- **Caleb Ralston, the process editor:** An editor kept bringing process fixes. Caleb realized he wanted to be the standard setter, not a manager, and made him senior editor.
  <br>*Why it works:* Leadership is also who sets the standard and influences the team.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the team is doing great without naming who did what.
- Passing executive anger straight to the creatives (Caleb).
- Faking care.
- Making management the only way to grow.
- Covering a mistake by calling it a test.

## 9.15 Burnout, focus time and creative structure

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Caleb Ralston says burnout comes more from unclear expectations and inefficiency than from heavy workload, and that creatives need protected maker time and clear but flexible structure.

### What burnout is and its warning signs

- Caleb: burnout (or "work fatigue, career fatigue") happens to everyone. Creatives love a challenge, so push them right to the edge, not over.
- His claim: the biggest cause is unclear expectations and misaligned incentives, more than a heavy workload. His example: rewarding views while punishing people because conversions and sales are not rising. Passionate creatives given too little work also check out.
- His definition: burnout is a mix of lack of autonomy, unclear expectations and an unsustainable pace.
- Warning signs: declining creativity, disengagement (camera off on Zoom more, or on camera but not contributing), avoiding challenges and emotional exhaustion (less control, quicker frustration, crying, high anxiety).

### Normalize rest

- If you as a leader never pause, your team will not. A boss once asked Caleb whether his workload would ever go away. It will not, so take time off even in the craziness.
- His story: an editor worked nine months without a day off, uploading to frame.io at 2 a.m. on weekends. He encouraged rest, she asked for three weeks in Europe with good notice, and despite a moment of panic they made it work.
- Pushing the red line all the time leaves no energy to innovate. He recommends 7 to 8 hours of sleep and a couple of weeks of vacation a year. He came back from his first proper vacation in 7 to 8 years full of ideas.
- You cannot decide others' personal lives, but younger people may not know how to pace themselves. His picture: a marathon. Sprint the first 5 miles and you are done.

### Fix inefficiency, protect maker time

- Burnout is often inefficiency, not volume, so systematize workflows. Caleb's claim: every 30 minute meeting can cost a creative 1.5 hours (30 minutes winding down or clock-watching before, 30 minutes getting back into flow after).
- Maker versus manager schedule (an idea from Paul Graham's essay, as Caleb credits it): makers move things forward by making, with deep focus. Managers move things by meetings. Most organizations run on the manager schedule, which leaves editors 15 to 30 minute gaps.
- Caleb's own business: Tuesday and Thursday are manager days with most meetings. Monday, Wednesday and Friday are blocked for maker time.
- In a bigger company: Monday is a manager day with back-to-back meetings from about 8:30 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. and one 15 minute buffer. Tuesday, Thursday and Friday are maker time from 7 a.m. to 12 p.m. with no calls unless emergency, then 12 to 5 p.m. bookable, with meetings pushed as late as possible (avoid a 12:30 meeting then another at 4:00). Wednesday is a team-wide maker day: 1:1s allowed for feedback or jamming, no meetings with more than two people, so nobody feels guilted into dropping work.
- His claim: most creatives he managed loved it and took it with them.

### Shiny objects and structure

- Shiny object syndrome: chasing every idea leads to scattered, inconsistent execution. Teach discernment through paths, not orders.
- His story: the number one shorts editor, a year in, wanted to move to YouTube long form (it looked "sexy" and the CEO valued it more). Caleb laid out two paths. Path 1: stay in shorts, quickly become senior editor and likely manage people young. Path 2: go to YouTube where three strong editors are ahead, start at the bottom with slim chances of a senior role. Or climb in shorts first and move later as a leader. Within a day or two the editor chose shorts, understanding focus brings bigger outcomes faster. Caleb was willing to give an A player 6 months to try something to keep them engaged.
- Structure versus chaos: too much structure stifles innovation, too little leads to disorder. Caleb: "the enemy of creativity is confusion". The more clarity, the more energy goes to creating. Set clear objectives, share mission and vision, give creative space on the how.
- It is not one size fits all. A videographer crew around a fast-paced entrepreneur lives in chaos (at Gary Vee: last-minute flights, setting up a podcast in an office in 5 minutes). A big YouTube editing function needs more system and process to hit deadlines. Chaos should be organized: "you need rules in place in order to break the rules". Hold systems fluidly, not religiously.

### Creative guardrails

- Constraints fuel creativity when used right. Instead of "make this go viral" or "promote my new six-hour video on building a brand on YouTube", brief "a mashup of me talking about how to build a brand, only using podcast moments".
- His constraint example: the talent cannot film for a month and there are no new videos for four YouTube slots, so the team defaults to thematic mashups from past footage.

### Frameworks

**Maker and manager days** (Caleb Ralston (idea credited to Paul Graham))

1. Pick manager days and stack meetings back to back
2. Block the other days or mornings for maker time with no calls
3. Push meetings as late in the day as possible
4. Keep one team-wide maker day with no meetings over two people

### Examples

- **Caleb Ralston, the nine months without a day off:** An editor worked nine months straight. After Caleb encouraged rest, she asked for three weeks in Europe with notice, and the team made it work.
  <br>*Why it works:* He used it as an example that rest is possible.
- **Caleb Ralston, shorts or long form:** He showed the top shorts editor two career paths. The editor chose shorts within a day or two.
  <br>*Why it works:* Paths teach discernment better than orders.

### Common mistakes

- Rewarding views while punishing the lack of sales.
- Giving passionate creatives too little work.
- Booking meetings in 15 to 30 minute gaps.
- Running structure religiously.
- Briefing "make it go viral" instead of a constraint.

### Try it

## 9.16 Sam's team standards: principles, details and values

*6 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet says a good team is led with principles, not taste, and held to small details and three core values at Martell Media.

### Lead with principles, not preferences

- Sam calls this "probably the single biggest piece of advice". Give lots of notes, but teach principles, not your taste.
- "I don't like this, change it" teaches nothing, and they will keep bringing problems back to you. A principle teaches them to think for themselves next time.
- His Martell Media examples: show don't tell, progressive density and "thumbnails clear, not clever".
- Bonus: the same principles become content, which he calls "super meta".

### Obsess over the details

- Sam's picture: Olympic gold versus silver is milliseconds. He critiques the keyframes in his editors' videos because "I care more about the outcome than I care about their feelings".
- He also obsesses over volume and speed.
- On the podcast set with Dan Martell, Sam saw Chris Williamson's team obsess over the shot, the questions, the audio and guest quality (only A+ guests). "The war is won in the small battles."

### Martell Media's three core values

- Build the people: the only way up is to get replaced, so train people to replace their manager.
- Be the example: you cannot ask for detail you do not show yourself.

### Credit your team

- Dan Martell shines light on his team instead of hiding them from poachers. Sam says it has the opposite effect: people whose brands you grow are less likely to leave, because of reciprocity. He says it is why he has not left.

### Frameworks

**Martell Media's three core values** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Build the people: train them to replace their manager
2. Be the example: show the detail you ask for
3. Simple scales: make it simple, not complex

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, notes as principles:** Instead of "change this", he gives principles like show don't tell, progressive density and thumbnails clear, not clever.
  <br>*Why it works:* A principle teaches the editor to decide next time.

### Common mistakes

- Giving notes as taste ("I don't like it").
- Asking for details you do not show yourself.
- Hiding the team from view.

### Where the sources disagree

How much to defend feelings: Sam says he critiques keyframes because he cares more about the outcome than editors' feelings. Caleb Ralston says to praise in public, criticize in private, and be careful how tough feedback is delivered. Both aim for high quality but disagree on how much feelings should shape the delivery.

### Try it

Take one note you gave recently and rewrite it as a principle the person can reuse.

## Cheat sheet: Systems, teams and AI

The whole module on one page.

- Fix the process before blaming creativity (Sam).
- Put every process on paper as an SOP, checklist or template.
- Time-block each step of the weekly content flow.
- Keep one place for ideas, swipe files and real stories.
- Bring ideas to the meeting, then kill the weak ones (Sam).
- Track views, followers added, leads and revenue.
- Review daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly; change things monthly, not daily.
- Simplify and delete before you automate (Sam).
- Let AI do speed, keep taste for yourself (Shann).
- One agent per job, same notes, logged work, human approval.
- Hire in order: first a content manager, then channel owners (Sam).
- Hire for taste and potential, train for skill, run a paid test (Ryan).
- Define the what and let the team define the how (Caleb).
- Check on people, not on the lighting and framing.
- Make flops safe: announce your own first (Caleb).
- Give credit by name, correct in private.
- Protect maker time and watch for burnout signs.
- Teach principles, not taste (Sam).
