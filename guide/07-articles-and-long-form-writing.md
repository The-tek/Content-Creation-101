## 7.1 Why long content builds trust

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Short posts help new people find you. Long pieces like articles, newsletters and long videos are where people spend real time with you, and that time turns a stranger into someone who trusts you

### Time builds trust

The more time people have spent with your long content, the more they trust your short posts. Every minute someone reads or watches you adds to that trust

Say a famous author of a habits book posts "habits are good" and gets thousands of likes. A random account posts the same line and people reply "yeah, obviously". The idea is the same. The only difference is how much time people have already spent with the writer

The credit also goes to whoever wrote the long version. If you want your name linked to an idea, write the long piece about it

### Short and long do different jobs

A short post holds someone's attention for about 10 seconds. A video, podcast or newsletter holds it for 15 to 60 minutes. The person who gets the most attention over time gets the most trust

Short posts are still useful, because they bring new people to your long pieces. So keep both, and turn your best short ideas into longer content

Long content also lasts. One good piece keeps getting found for years, while a short post is forgotten in a day

### A simple setup

You only need 3 things to start:

1. One short-form platform to reach new people
2. One long-form platform to go deeper, like YouTube or X articles
3. One place you own to stay in touch, like an email list or a private community

A good pace is about 7 short pieces and 1 to 2 long pieces a week. Expect it to take hundreds of posts before it pays off

### What the numbers on X show

One creator tracked his first 11 days on X. His 2 articles got a median of 194,000 views and 3,700 bookmarks. His 38 plain text posts got a median of 2,400 views and 13 bookmarks

These are his own numbers and nobody has checked them, but they show how much more attention an article can get than a short post

### Mistakes to avoid

- only posting short content and expecting the deep trust that long content builds
- dropping short posts completely, when they still bring new people to your long pieces

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say text does not spread as well as video, and that readers now wonder whether AI wrote it. Others say long writing is where trust and customers come from

### Try it

List the 3 ideas you have posted about most in short form. For each one, write down what a reader would need to learn in 15 minutes to really understand it. Pick the idea with the longest answer as your first long piece

## 7.2 Choosing what the article is about

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The topic does most of the work. Build the article from ideas that already did well as short posts, and pick a big, fresh topic that a specific group of people wants right now

### Build from ideas that already worked

Think of your article as a best-of album. Find your posts on one theme that did better than usual, and make each one a section of the article. Every idea inside has already given people a reason to read

Most of your followers never saw what you posted 6 months ago, so reusing a winner is fine

You can also find topics in your messages. When 3 or more people ask you the same question, write about it

### Test small, then expand

1. Post the idea as a single short post
2. If it does better than usual, expand it into a longer piece
3. Pull the best line from the long piece and post it on its own

### Pick a big, fresh topic

One creator's biggest articles (179,000 to 327,000 views) were each on a new topic. A later roadmap that repeated the topic of his first article got 42,000 views, which is 5 to 10 times fewer. A repeated topic gets far fewer views, so each article needs a fresh topic or a fresh angle

The winning topics promise a big result everybody wants, like money, freedom or being known, and not a narrow skill. A reason to read now helps too, like fear of losing a job to AI

You don't need secret information. You need the right package for the right reader

### Make the promise big, specific and timely

A big promise with a deadline, like "fix your life in 1 day", goes against what people believe, which is that change takes years. That is why people click

Give a system and not loose tips. A full plan for one day, morning to night, is easy to follow and easy to remember

Timing counts. A take on a hyped new app works at the peak of the hype. A month later it would flop

### Only write what you have done

Write only about things you have done and are good at, because readers can tell when you are faking. Teaching something you haven't done yourself is the fastest way to lose trust

**Example: A repeated topic**

Say you wrote a guide on growing on X and got 100,000 views. Two weeks later you write a second guide on the same topic with a new title. Expect far fewer views, because readers already have the first one

*Why it works:* A new topic or a new angle gets read. A copy of your last article does not

### Mistakes to avoid

- writing an article on the same topic as your last article
- starting from a blank page instead of from ideas that already worked as posts
- teaching something you haven't done yourself

### Try it

Sort your last 50 posts by bookmarks or replies. Find 5 to 7 winners that share one theme. Write one promise that ties them together, then list each winner as a section heading

## 7.3 Titles and first lines

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** An article has three hooks in a row: the title, the subtitle and the first paragraph. Good titles promise a big result, show the piece is complete, and make a promise the article can really keep

### Three hooks in a row

In an article, the title, the subtitle and the first paragraph each work as their own hook (the part that makes people keep reading). Each one has a single job: get the reader to the next one

Write the article first and the hook last. Write 5 to 10 versions, then cut the weakest ones right away

### What good titles share

They promise a big result that everyone wants, like money, freedom or being known, and not a narrow skill

They show the piece is complete, with a word like "full guide", "101" or "3 strategies"

They are clear at the first read. The reader never has to think about what the title means

### Title shapes that worked on X

- a deadline: "Everyone using AI has about 12 months to develop these 3 moats" (252,000 views)
- a tool plus a job: "[Tool] as Your [Job Title] (FULL GUIDE)" (159,000 views)
- a skill compared to the best: "How to [skill] like the top 0.01%" (127,000 views)
- a label that promises the complete version: "Distribution 101: A Guide to Reaching Millions" (297,000 views)

These views are reported by the creators themselves

### The first line

The only job of your first line is to get the second line read. Open with a wish or a pain many people have, like "If you want to grow online but don't know where to start...", then get specific

Say who the article is for, and add a TL;DR (a two-line summary) near the top

### Keep the promise honest

A headline is only clickbait when the content doesn't deliver. If the article gives what the title promised, nobody minds a strong hook

Getting the click is not the goal. Keeping attention is. If the title promises one thing and the opening talks about another, readers leave

**Example: A title that works**

"Everyone using AI has about 12 months to develop these 3 moats"

*Why it works:* It has a deadline as the reason to read now, a group the reader belongs to, and a numbered promise

### Mistakes to avoid

- a title the reader has to think about
- a headline the article doesn't deliver on
- a narrow-skill title when a big result would bring more readers

### Try it

Take one article idea and write 10 titles. Cut any that need a second read to understand. Then check the last 3: can the article really deliver that promise?

## 7.4 The opening, part 1: earning trust

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** The opening is everything before the main teaching starts. Its first job is to make the reader think "this person gets me and can help me". A study of 27 top article openings found 8 moves that build that trust

### What the opening has to do

The opening has three jobs: build trust ("this person gets me"), earn attention ("I need to keep reading") and make things clear ("this is for me"). This lesson covers trust

The next one covers the other two

Most openings run 150 to 450 words, and the long ones are almost all story

### Moves that show you understand the reader

Start from the reader's life. Describe what they feel, what they have tried and what they fear, with real details like a tight chest or a heavy body

Then say "if you feel lost, this is for you"

Say the doubt out loud. Name the thought the reader is too embarrassed to say, like "was this all a scam?" or "I bet you clicked for a quick way to make money"

Tell them you were where they are. Say you were in their exact situation, failed, and found a way out

Use hard details like a debt amount or a side job, and only a low point that really happened to you

### Moves that show you can help

Show proof before teaching. Put one line of real numbers before any advice, like growing from 400 to 26,000 followers or 6 years of experience

Show the effort behind the article, like 4 years of books and lectures or hundreds of hours of testing

Say what the article is not. For example: "this is not a guide on paid ads, and here is someone better for that"

Saying your limits makes the rest easier to believe

### Giving the other side its due

Admit the common view is partly right before you disagree. For example: social media has obvious downsides, but...

Readers trust a writer who is fair, and not one who attacks everything

**Example: Showing you understand**

An article about feeling overwhelmed describes the reader's body (a tight chest, a heavy body) before it offers any help

*Why it works:* The reader feels understood because of the specific details. Just saying "I understand" would not do that

### Mistakes to avoid

- saying "I get it" with no details from the reader's life, which reads as fake
- inventing a low point or numbers
- claiming proof you can't show

### Try it

Write two different first paragraphs for the same article: one that mirrors the reader's life with real details, and one that opens with proof. Read them aloud and keep the one you would believe as a stranger

## 7.5 The opening, part 2: attention and clarity

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** After trust, the opening has to make the reader want to keep going and make clear what they will get. A few simple moves do both, and almost every top opening uses them

### Moves that earn attention

Say a hard truth about the reader in one blunt line, like "most creators are content factories"

Go against a belief everyone shares, like "posting more is not how you grow". Name the advice they keep hearing and show why it fails

Open with a story and keep the point for after the surprise. For example, two investors where the janitor did better than the Harvard banker. The reader wants to know why

Add a deadline, like "you have 12 months", or a big number about what the reader already has, like "there is \$100,000 in your notes"

### Moves that make things clear

Say who it's for in the first line, or in a list that starts "this is for you if"

Add a roadmap, a short list of what is coming, so the reader knows what they will get

Add a TL;DR on top, the whole point in 2 lines

Promise time or value, like "learn 5 years of lessons in 10 minutes". It makes the article feel like a shortcut

### End the opening with a pull

The last line of the opening should pull the reader into part 1. For example: "Let me help.", a heading that promises a turn, or a story that stops at its lowest point

The opening should not fade out softly. Its last line moves the reader into the main part

### What almost all top openings share

- the reader appears in the first 2 to 3 lines, as "you" or as a named group
- one concrete, true detail comes early, like a number, a name or a debt
- the writer sounds human, with a failure, a fear or a joke about themselves
- the opening ends with a pull into part 1

### A tip from video

Say "you" early and say "I" less. "I've struggled with skin problems my whole life" makes people ask whether they're like you. "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life" makes it about them

### Mistakes to avoid

- talking about yourself before the reader appears
- ending the opening with a soft fade instead of a pull into part 1
- a roadmap or promise that doesn't match the title

### Try it

Take an opening you've written and check it against the 4 shared traits. Mark each one present or missing. Rewrite only the missing ones, then add a pull as the last sentence

## 7.6 Five opening recipes

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** The best openings follow a few set orders of moves. Pick the recipe that fits your article: a pain, an opinion, a how-to guide, one big idea, or a trend you want to question

### Five recipes

Each recipe is a set order of moves from the last two lessons. Pick the one that fits what your article is about

1. The "I get it" recipe, for pain topics like feeling stuck or lost. Describe the reader's situation, say their doubt out loud, tell them you were there too, then say "let me help"
2. The flip recipe, for opinion articles. Start with a blunt claim, explain why the common advice fails, state your main point in one line, then give the roadmap
3. The masterclass recipe, for big how-to guides. Say who it's for, show proof and your honest limits, give the roadmap, then start teaching
4. The story recipe, for an article about one big idea. Tell one person's story, show the surprise, then say "and that's true for you too"
5. The myth check recipe, for trends and fears. Quote the viral claim, say why people believe it, then give your own view

### How to pick

Ask what the reader feels when they find your article. A pain calls for the "I get it" recipe. A belief you want to change calls for the flip. A skill they want calls for the masterclass

Whatever recipe you pick, the opening must keep the promise of the title

### The video version

A long video opening has three jobs. First, reassure: match the promise to the title, show quick proof and answer the doubt "this won't work for me"

Second, make it bigger with a bonus or something new. Third, open a loop (a question left unanswered so people keep watching) by showing the roadmap and hiding the later steps

**Example: The flip recipe**

Say your article starts with "Niche down is bad advice". Next you explain why people believe it, then what they really want, then your main point in one line, then a roadmap of 3 parts

*Why it works:* The reader meets a claim, a reason and a plan in the first 150 words

### Mistakes to avoid

- using a story opening for a big how-to guide, or a masterclass opening for a single idea
- breaking the promise of the title in the first lines

### Try it

Pick one topic and draft two openings of about 150 words each with two different recipes. Ask one person which made them want to read part 1, and why

## 7.7 A 12-part article skeleton, top to bottom

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** One creator's big articles all follow the same 12-part skeleton. It makes a long guide feel like a paid course given away for free: what it is, why to trust it, what's inside, the teaching, then a recap and a close

### The top: parts 1 to 6

The first 6 parts do the selling, so a stranger knows within 20 seconds what the article is, who it's for and why to trust it:

1. A subtitle or TL;DR saying what it is and that it's complete
2. The promise: what's at stake and what the reader gets, said once
3. The roadmap: 4 to 5 section names
4. Trust lines: where the article comes from, and "not written by AI"
5. An early soft ask, like "if you enjoy this, a like and repost go a long way"
6. Who it's for and who it isn't for, like "This is NOT a guide on paid ads"

### The middle: parts 7 to 9

1. 3 to 5 big sections with short headers, like "Packaging" or "Systems"
2. Inside each section, numbered sub-points. Each has a bold label, 1 to 3 short lines, then a list or a text box, then a real example
3. A TL;DR at the end of each big section, 3 lines

### The end: parts 10 to 12

1. Final tips: 5 to 7 one-liners
2. A recap with one line per big section
3. The close: thank the reader, ask for a like, repost or follow, ask what to write next, and finish with a last push like "Now go build it"

### Why it reads easily

The articles run about 2,000 words with 20 to 30 numbered sub-points. Paragraphs are 1 to 3 lines and almost every point ends in a list

They are long overall but easy to read. People trust them because of experience, examples and frameworks, and not because of studies or long stories

### Mistakes to avoid

- writing long paragraphs where a list or a box would do
- skipping the roadmap, so the reader doesn't know what the article covers
- leaving out the "for / not for" block, so readers can't tell whether the article is for them

### Try it

Take an outline you have and map it onto the 12 parts. Write only parts 1 to 6 (about 150 words). Then check: could a stranger tell in 20 seconds what it is, who it's for and why to trust it?

## 7.8 Inside a section: text boxes and teaching moves

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Turn every abstract idea into a box readers can screenshot. Inside each section, explain the idea in simple words, number your points, use real examples and write like a person talks

### How each section teaches

Explain the idea in simple words first. Then give numbered sub-points, each with a bold label, 1 to 3 short lines on why it is important, and a list or an example

Ask questions the reader can answer about themselves, like "What do you stand for? Who is it for? Why should they care?"

Use real brands as examples, one line each. A named example is easier to picture than a vague one

Right before your main idea, put "If you take away only one thing from this article, make it this:"

### Text boxes

X articles show code blocks as clean boxes. Use 5 to 7 per article for frameworks people will screenshot and bookmark. Good shapes are:

- arrows for a flow, like "Who are you? → What you do"
- tables for comparisons, like 7 platforms by what each is best for and how much effort it takes
- pros and cons boxes when you compare two options
- copy-paste prompts, so the reader can act straight away

### Make it easy to take in

People scan, they don't read every word. If a post looks like too much work, they scroll away. Formatting is part of the hook, because it makes starting feel easy

Use one thought per line, paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines, and a mix of short and long sentences. Write simply enough that a 10-year-old could understand. The ideas can be smart, but the words should be simple

**Example: Turning a paragraph into a box**

Say you explain "pick the emotion you want people to feel" in a paragraph. Instead, make a box: Smart ........ "I learn something every time", Inspired ........ "I want to try this"

*Why it works:* An abstract idea becomes a cheat sheet people screenshot

### Mistakes to avoid

- explaining an abstract idea in plain paragraphs with no box, list or example
- vague examples instead of named brands and people
- walls of text, because on a phone 4 lines already look like an essay

### Try it

Take one idea you usually explain in a paragraph and turn it into a text box three ways: dot leaders, arrows and a small table. Keep the one a stranger could understand in 5 seconds

## 7.9 How top article authors structure the whole piece

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Top X article authors build a whole piece in an order they can repeat. Copy the order of the parts and write your own content, and end each part with a push into the next

### An order for an argument piece

Use it when you want to change what people believe. Say your article is about the 3 things that protect you once AI is everywhere. The order is:

1. A deadline claim
2. A story that starts "I learned this the hard way"
3. The problem, with data
4. Your 3 ideas, named
5. One section per idea, with a story, a study and rules
6. An ending that tells the reader what is at stake

### An order for a how-to

Use it when you want to teach a skill. The order is:

1. Proof and who it's for
2. A promise
3. 3 to 5 numbered steps or phases
4. A recap
5. A checklist or a plan the reader can follow

### An order for a story

Use it when the whole article is about one idea. Start with a hard truth, tell a personal story, add someone else's story, then say "what this means for you" and end with a one-line recap

### Headings that keep people reading

A heading that makes a claim works better than a plain label. "Your stories will get ignored if you don't do this" makes a skimming reader stop, and "Storytelling" does not

End each section with a bridge to the next one, like a question that leads into it

### Mistakes to avoid

- copying a structure's topic instead of its order of parts
- ending a section without a bridge to the next one

### Try it

Pick one article you admire and write its order of parts as one line per part. Then use that order for your own topic

## 7.10 Drafting, AI help and your own taste

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Drafting is a process: get everything out messy first, then tighten it. When you use AI, let it do the production work and keep the taste for yourself. Taste means your choices about what's worth saying and how it should feel

### Write messy, then cut

Write your article messy first, with no structure. Don't worry about how bad it is. Then cut it down into something shorter and sharper

Make sure you will be proud of a piece before you post it. Writing long also improves your short posts, because you now know your ideas well

### Let AI do the production

AI can write the structure and first drafts. You rewrite the hook and make the editing choices: what to keep, cut and stress. One creator calls it 80% AI and 20% taste

The reason is that AI output is often okay but generic. Your research and your taste are what make a piece worth sharing

### How to bring your taste

1. Do the research yourself first. Ask what story the data tells and why someone would care
2. Decide the storyline before you ask AI to write anything
3. Give the AI examples to follow, limits and samples of what you like
4. Rewrite the hook yourself

### Mistakes to avoid

- letting the tool pick your hook
- posting a piece you would not be proud of
- skipping the research and expecting the tool to find the story

### Try it

Write one section of an article in one go with no limits, then cut it to half its length. Look at what survived, because that is what you really wanted to say

## 7.11 The newsletter as your home base

*6 min read*

> **The big idea:** A newsletter is a long-form home where you go deeper on the problems your posts bring up. Posts bring people in, and the newsletter keeps them

### Pair short with long

A short post names a problem. The newsletter walks through the full answer

That is how it shows you know your subject

Pair one short-form platform with one long-form place. Substack is one example of a newsletter platform

### Ask your readers what they need

- Put a link to a short questionnaire in the middle and at the end of every newsletter, asking what readers need help with
- The answers tell you what to write next, and readers get a simple way to reply

### Reuse and repeat

Turn long content into other long content. Write a winning video out as an email, or pull its audio out as a podcast

Give the newsletter real time and don't write it only from leftovers. Results come from doing it many times, so plan on a long run

### Mistakes to avoid

- treating the newsletter as a place for leftovers
- writing without a way for readers to reply or tell you what they need

### Try it

Pick one newsletter you write or plan to write. Decide where a questionnaire link will go: once in the middle and once at the end

## 7.12 Pacing and structure borrowed from long video advice

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Several ideas from long video advice also work for writing. This lesson lists the ones that do

### What works for articles too

A hook is also the first few words of an email or a tweet, and not only of a video

Uneven pacing works in writing too: shorter points early and longer ones later. The reader feels progress, because the early sections are quick to finish

Open with proof, promise and plan: what this is, why they should listen to you, and how you will get them there

### Pacing a long piece

Say up front how many parts there are, so readers know what's coming

Start broad and get more detailed. Open with the problem everyone has, and keep the most detailed how-to for the readers who want it, near the end

Cut every story, section or paragraph that drifts away from the promise in the title. A long piece is fine if every part earns its place

The real danger is a piece that moves too slowly

### Inside each section

- Make a point, explain it, give an example, state the lesson, then lead into the next section
- Ask for the like, follow or bookmark about 30% of the way in, and remind people once more at the end

**The section loop**

1. Point: state the idea
2. Explain: say why it works
3. Example: give one example
4. Lesson: say what to do, then lead into the next section

### Mistakes to avoid

- padding a piece with parts that drift away from the title
- using the same pace from start to end

### Try it

Take an outline and cut every section that doesn't help deliver the promise in your title

## 7.13 Pushing an article after it is published

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Treat every article as a small campaign. Publishing is only the start: quote your own article, add a video version, and post it from a few angles over several days

### Quote your own article

After you publish, quote your own article with a hook that asks readers to bookmark it. Do this 2 to 3 times per article, each time with a different hook

Most of your followers will not see the first post, so a second and third one reach people who missed it

### Add a video version

- Make a video version of the article with chapters, and post it a few days later as a quote of the article
- One creator did this for all 5 of his article videos

### Try different angles

Frame the article from a few angles: a quote with a meme, or a quote built around a prompt, like "send this prompt to an AI"

One creator pushed an article over 4 days with a video, a quote of the video, the article, a quote with a prompt angle and a quote with a meme. The article got 461,000 views, by his own report

### Close with a reason to come back

End with a line like "I plan to write 1 to 2 articles a week". It gives readers a reason to follow you for the next one

Each push works best on a fresh topic. One creator's repeated topics got 5 to 10 times fewer views

**The article push**

1. Quote your own article with a hook that asks for a bookmark, 2 to 3 times
2. Post a video version with chapters a few days later, as a quote of the article
3. Add a quote built around a prompt, and a quote with a meme

### Mistakes to avoid

- publishing and then doing nothing more
- repeating a topic you already covered

### Try it

Plan three pushes for one article: a quote that asks for a bookmark, a quote built around a prompt, and a video version

## Cheat sheet: Articles and long-form writing

The whole module on one page

- turn your best short ideas into longer pieces
- pick a big, fresh topic, because repeated topics get far fewer views
- write only about what you have really done
- make the promise big, specific and timely
- write the hook last, with 5 to 10 versions
- a good title promises a big result and is clear straight away
- show proof before you start teaching
- say what the article is not, so readers believe the rest
- name who the article is for in the first line
- end the opening with a line that pulls the reader into part 1
- add a TL;DR, a roadmap and a short summary at the end of each section
- use text boxes people can screenshot
- use short lines and simple words
- write a messy first draft, then tighten it
- let AI do the production work, and bring your own taste
