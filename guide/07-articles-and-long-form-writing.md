## 7.1 Why long form builds authority

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Short posts get you seen, long pieces get you believed. Dan Koe, Kallaway, Sam Gaudet and Ryan all say long form (articles, newsletters, books, long videos) is where trust and authority are built, because people spend more time with you there.

### Time under attention (Dan Koe)

- Dan Koe calls it time under attention: the more time people spend with you in long form (articles, books, videos), the more they read your short posts through everything else you have said.
- Koe's example: when James Clear tweets "habits are good" it gets thousands of likes, while a random account posting the same line gets "yeah no shit". The idea is the same, the time people already spent with the writer is different.
- Koe on his own account: with two books, years of YouTube and 100K+ views per newsletter article, he can post simpler things and people still read them through what they know of him.
- People credit an idea to whoever wrote the long version. Koe: most people who read Atomic Habits think of James Clear when someone posts about habits, not of the person who tweeted it. You hand the authority to whoever chose to write long form.
- Koe says turning your best short ideas into longer content is how your name spreads without you trying, how you build "the roots of your brand deep into the collective psyche", and how you build trust "orders of magnitude higher" than people who only post short content.
- Koe: a short post that holds 10 seconds of attention is nothing next to a video, podcast or newsletter that holds 15 to 60 minutes. "Modern power belongs to those who hold the most attention in their lifetime." He adds that short form still has benefits, so don't drop it.

### Long minutes are stickier (Kallaway)

- Kallaway's "law": the longer the piece, the stickier each minute is. A minute of short form is less sticky than a minute of long form, which is less sticky than a minute of a live stream, because stickiness compounds when someone spends many minutes in a row with you.
- His formula: personal brand value = viewers x content minutes x stickiness. Long form is how you raise the minutes and the stickiness.
- His modern brand stack: one short-form platform for reach, one long-form platform for depth (he names YouTube), and one nurture channel (email or a private community). "That's all you need."
- His suggested cadence: about 7 short pieces a week and 1 to 2 long pieces a week. "This is going to take hundreds and hundreds of posts."

### What the others add

- Sam Gaudet (long video, not writing): a 20 to 30 minute piece gets people closer to who you are than a 30-second clip. In his "seven Rs of influence", long form builds the relationship faster than short clips.
- Sam: one great piece keeps getting found for years. He says Dan Martell has videos still getting views years later because they focused on quality.
- Ryan's "4 boxes": long video (deep trust), short video (raw reach), long written like X articles and newsletters (depth, authority), short written like posts (a playground to test ideas fast). He checks all 4 on X alone.
- GaryVee: spend 5 to 10 hours learning Substack. "Information is now a commodity… yet people will pay for it if they connect with the person. If you are a good writer, it will be lead gen for your business."
- Nicolas Cole: the viral written format keeps moving (Quora answers, then Medium articles, then Twitter threads, then LinkedIn carousels, now X articles). If a format keeps appearing in your feed, it's there for a reason.
- Dan Koe says writing and outlining his book shaped every newsletter and every tweet he wrote after it. Long form also sharpens your own thinking.

### What the numbers on X show

- In a study of Ryan's first 11 days of posting (40 posts), his 2 articles had a median of 194k views and 3.7k bookmarks. Quotes of his own articles came next at 5.9k views. Plain text posts had a median of 2.4k views and 13 bookmarks.
- Ryan's first 26 days (his numbers): 2.9M views over 97 posts. His top post was his Distribution 101 article at 326k.
- Ryan's later algorithm article (461k views) beat all 7 of his earlier articles. These are his reported numbers, not checked.

### Frameworks

**The modern personal brand stack** (Kallaway)

1. Reach: one short-form platform, posted often
2. Depth: one long-form platform, 1 to 2 pieces a week
3. Nurture: one owned channel, email or a private community

**The 4 boxes** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Long video: deep trust
2. Short video: raw reach
3. Long written (articles, newsletters): depth and authority
4. Short written (posts): test ideas fast, winners become long pieces

### Examples

- **Dan Koe, James Clear and habits:** Anyone can post that habits matter. Readers of Atomic Habits still think of James Clear, and his simple tweets about habits get thousands of likes.
  <br>*Why it works:* Koe: the authority goes to whoever wrote the long version, and long form changes how people read your short posts.

### Common mistakes

- Only posting short content and expecting the trust that long form builds (Koe).
- Dropping short form completely: Koe says it is less potent but still useful, and Kallaway's stack keeps a short-form platform for reach.

### Where the sources disagree

Oren (@orenmeetsworld) says text "just does not travel the same way" as video and that people now doubt AI-era text was written by you. Dan Koe, GaryVee and Ryan say written long form (articles, newsletters) builds authority and leads, and Ryan's own articles are his biggest posts.

## 7.2 Choosing what the article is about

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The topic does most of the work. The sources agree on two moves: build the article from ideas that already proved themselves as short posts, and pick a big, fresh topic a specific group urgently wants.

### Build from proven ideas

- Dan Koe's "greatest-hits album": in his most-viewed X article ("How to fix your entire life in 1 day", 226M views, his number), each of the 7 sections was an idea that had already done well as a post. One section title was an old tweet, copied word for word. "Every idea inside the article is a reason it goes viral, not just the title."
- Shaan Puri with Dickie Bush and Nicolas Cole (Ship 30 for 30): validate small, then expand. A single tweet that over-performs becomes a thread, and the best tweet inside a thread becomes its own post. "Most people didn't see what you tweeted 6 months ago."
- Ryan calls short written posts "your playground to test ideas fast". The winners become articles or long videos.
- Ryan's DM rule: when 3 or more people ask the same question in his DMs, he makes content on it. He says his 202k-view crash course came from exactly that.
- Dan Koe's newsletter topic tactic: find a YouTube video that blew up in your topic, use it as your newsletter topic, then write your own take without watching the video. "Good newsletters work a lot like good videos."

### Pick a big, fresh topic

- What Ryan's article numbers show (a reading of his view counts, not his claim): his three biggest early articles (179k to 327k views) were each a new topic (the Head of Content role, branding, distribution). A roadmap that repeated his first article's topic got 42k, and a guide that overlapped two earlier ones got 26k. Repeat topics got 5 to 10x fewer views.
- His two Grok Bot guides (219k and 103k) rode a hot new tool and gave real prompts. His algorithm article (461k, his biggest) was a fresh topic tied to news: X open-sourcing its algorithm.
- The study of Ryan's articles found they spread because each was a big, sharp topic a specific group urgently wanted (content jobs as a "gold rush", "branding is the only moat") and felt like a paid course given away free.
- Ryan admits his own viral article was "not completely mind-blowing, relatively common knowledge, it's all about the formatting and the presentation to a specific audience". You don't need secret information, you need the right package for the right reader.
- From the title study of top X articles: winners promise a big outcome everyone wants (money, escape, being known) instead of a narrow skill, often with AI or job fear as the "why now" and a calm system as the answer.

### Make the promise extreme, specific and timely (Dan Koe)

- Koe's 226M article had an extreme, time-bound promise ("in 1 day") that clashed with the belief that change takes years. He says the promise also forced him to be creative enough to actually deliver it.
- It went against the season: posted in January, the intro attacks New Year's resolutions ("most people won't keep them, here's why").
- It gave a system, not tips: a full protocol for one day (morning, afternoon, night), and he told people to set calendar reminders. "I put myself into their day so they never forget the article." If you change someone's behaviour, they credit you.
- It taught a concept readers had never heard of (the 9 stages of ego development). Koe says new concepts come from books, not from AI chats or scrolling: "that's what makes them follow you, you taught them something they didn't know."
- Timing matters for any piece. Ship 30's example: a take on Clubhouse only worked at peak hype. A month later or 3 months earlier it would have flopped.

### Only write what you have earned

- Ryan: only talk about things you've done and are good at. People can tell when you're faking.
- Tristan Kermode (in Ryan's interview) quotes a creator who switched from "how to" to "how I" because "I shouldn't be teaching anything I haven't done myself".

### Frameworks

**The greatest-hits article** (Dan Koe)

1. Find your posts that already did well on one theme
2. Make each proven idea a section
3. Reuse the best old post as a section title if it fits
4. Wrap them in one big promise

**Validate small, then expand** (Shaan Puri, Dickie Bush, Nicolas Cole (Ship 30 for 30))

1. Post the idea as a single short post
2. If it over-performs, expand it into a longer piece
3. Pull the best line out of the long piece as its own post

### Examples

- **Dan Koe, "How to fix your entire life in 1 day":** 5 to 6k words, posted in January against New Year's resolutions, a one-day protocol with reminders, a concept readers hadn't heard of, and 7 sections that were each proven posts. He reports 226M views and 339k likes.
  <br>*Why it works:* Koe says every idea inside was a reason to spread, not just the title.
- **Ryan's repeat topics:** His Head of Content roadmap repeated the topic of his first article and got 42k views against 179k to 327k for his fresh-topic articles.
  <br>*Why it works:* A reading of his numbers: each article needs a fresh topic or a fresh angle, not a remix of the last one.

### Common mistakes

- Writing an article that remixes your last article's topic (Ryan's numbers).
- Starting from a blank idea instead of ideas that already proved themselves as posts (Koe, Ship 30).
- Teaching what you haven't done (Ryan, Codie Sanchez, Tristan Kermode).

### Try it

Sort your last 50 posts by bookmarks or replies. Find 5 to 7 winners that share one theme. Write a working promise that ties them together, then list each winner as a section heading.

## 7.3 Titles and first lines

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** An article has three hooks in a row: the title, the subtitle and the first paragraph (Ryan). The titles that worked on X promise a big outcome, signal that the piece is complete, and keep the promise the content can actually pay off.

### How the hook works in an article

- Ryan's hooks-by-format table: in an article, the title, the subtitle and the first paragraph are each their own hook.
- Ryan: "The only job of your first line is to get someone to read your second line." Ship 30 says the same about threads: tweet 1's only job is to get tweet 2 read, and most drop-off happens in the first 2 to 3 tweets.
- Ryan's hook process: write the piece first and the hook last, write 5 to 10 versions using different hook types, cut the weakest right away.

### Title formats that worked (title study of top X articles, views as reported)

- Deadline claim "Everyone [doing X] has [time] to [do Y]": Shann Holmberg's "Everyone using AI has about 12 months to develop these 3 moats", 252k.
- "[Tool] as Your [Job Title] (FULL GUIDE)": Ryan, 159k. "How to [skill] like the top 0.01%": Tim Denning, 127k.
- Proof in the title, "How we [big number] and made [result]": Ernesto 114k; domschats "How to go from 0-100M views/month" 103k. "The Psychology of a [X]": wizofecom, 70k.
- Ryan's newer markers: "In Depth", "(Dense Guide)", "(actually)" (promising the real version). His biggest article: "The X Algorithm Explained, In Depth" (461k), a timely topic plus "In Depth".

### What the winning titles share

- A big outcome everyone wants (money, escape, being known), not a narrow skill.
- A bracket or word that promises the complete thing (masterclass, full course, full guide, 3 strategies, 101).
- Often AI or job fear as the "why now", then a calm system as the answer.
- Proof (numbers or screenshots) comes before the teaching.

### First-line formats from top articles

- Threat numbers first: real public numbers that scare the reader before the promise (siddharthwv opened on Amazon, Dell and Oracle layoffs).
- Desire or pain opener for cold readers (Dan Koe): start with some form of "if you want" or "if you don't want". His article example: "If you have multiple interests, you probably have no idea what to do with your life." Koe: most people on social media aren't trying to learn, so open with the broad desire or pain, then get specific.
- Who it's for plus a TL;DR on top (Ryan).

### Keep the promise honest

- Ship 30: "there's no such thing as clickbait headlines, only clickbait content." If you pay off the promise, nobody minds the hook. "Want to know a secret?" followed by "work hard" is a broken promise.
- pmitu: getting the click isn't the goal, keeping attention is. If the headline promises and the content doesn't deliver, people leave. "Write hooks that are strong, but accurate."
- Hussain: good headlines sell the outcome, not the product.
- Sam Gaudet (long video): a mismatch between the packaging promise and the opening is, in his claim, the biggest cause of drop-off today.
- One real study on headlines: Robertson et al. 2023 (Nature Human Behaviour) ran about 105,000 headline variations on Upworthy. For an average headline, each extra negative word raised click-through by 2.3%, with sadness driving clicks most.
- One real study on sharing articles: Berger and Milkman 2012 studied about 7,000 New York Times articles. High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) got shared more, sadness made articles less likely to be shared, and positive content was more viral overall. So a sad hook can get the click, awe or a bit of anger gets the share.

### Frameworks

**Three hooks in a row** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Title: big promise, clear outcome, no confusion
2. Subtitle or TL;DR: what it is and that it's complete
3. First paragraph: its own hook that gets the next paragraph read

### Examples

- **Ryan, "Distribution 101: A Guide to Reaching Millions":** A known label (101) plus a big outcome (reaching millions). 297k views, about 28x his normal.
  <br>*Why it works:* Clear topic, complete-guide signal, big outcome, nothing to decode.
- **Shann Holmberg, "Everyone using AI has about 12 months to develop these 3 moats":** A deadline, a group the reader belongs to, and a numbered promise. 252k views.
  <br>*Why it works:* The deadline is the "why now", the 3 moats are the calm system.

### Common mistakes

- A title the reader has to think about (Ryan).
- A headline the content doesn't pay off (Ship 30, pmitu, Sam Gaudet).
- A narrow-skill title when a big outcome would draw more readers (title study).

### Try it

Take one article idea and write 10 titles, one in each format from this lesson. Cut any that need a second read to understand. Check the last 3 against the content: can the article really pay off that promise?

## 7.4 The opening, part 1: earning trust

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** The opening is everything before the main part starts, and its first job is to make the reader think "this person gets me and can help me". A study of 27 top article openings found 8 trust moves.

### What the openings study looked at

- 27 openings were studied: 12 Dan Koe letters plus his "Full Course" X article (655k), X articles by Shann Holmberg, Hussain Ibarra, Ryan (3), Tim Denning, domschats, Ernesto, beechinour, pmitu and EXM7777, plus Morgan Housel's "The Psychology of Money" essay, Paul Graham's "How to Do Great Work", two Sahil Bloom pieces and Justin Welsh's "Go make some friends".
- Length: most openings run 150 to 450 words. Dan Koe's run 135 to 900, usually about 300. Paul Graham's is about 90. The long ones are almost all story.
- The techniques fall into three jobs: trust ("this person gets me and can help me"), attention ("I need to keep reading") and clarity ("this is for me, and I know what I'll get"). This lesson covers trust.

### The 8 trust moves

- 1\. I was where you are: the writer was in the reader's exact situation, failed, and found the way out. The low point has hard specifics: a debt amount, a side job, switching majors 5 times, being too scared to speak. Used by Koe, Tim Denning (hid his past, broke after payday) and Shann ("i learned this the hard way").
- 2\. Mirror their feelings ("I get it"): describe the reader's life back to them: what they feel, what they tried, what they fear, even body signals (Sahil Bloom: a tight chest, a heavy body). Then "if you feel lost" or "I know you're on the edge". Koe and Sahil.
- 3\. Say their doubt out loud: voice the thought the reader is too embarrassed to say, like "was this all a scam?" or "I bet you clicked for a quick way to make money" (Koe).
- 4\. Proof before teaching: one line of real numbers before any advice. domschats (3B views), pmitu (400 to 26k followers), Koe (\$10K in year one to \$4M), Ernesto (\$1.7M a year studio), Ryan (6+ years).
- 5\. Proof of effort: years, hours or money that went into the article. Koe (4 years of books and lectures), Shann (hundreds of hours), Ryan (thousands of dollars of trial and error).
- 6\. "Not written by AI": a 2026 trust line. domschats: "None of this article was written by AI". Ryan uses it too.
- 7\. Honest limits: say what the article is NOT, or what you don't know, and point to someone better. Ryan ("not a masterclass on paid ads", and recommends another creator for ads), Paul Graham (only for the very ambitious), Koe ("no get-rich-quick here").
- 8\. Give the other side its due, then turn: admit the common view is partly right before disagreeing. Koe (social media's downsides are obvious, but...), Justin Welsh (connections ARE an advantage, so build them instead of resenting them).

### Ryan's trust lines in practice

- "Why listen to me?" section near the top of his newer articles: his numbers and what he says he pioneered, before any teaching.
- Proof first: his Grok Bot guide opens with "Firstly, the elephant in the room: proof of the results" and a screenshot.
- The honest disclaimer on a technical topic: "I am NOT from the X algorithm team. This is purely my interpretation." Saying what you are not builds trust.
- An "Expectations" section: he admits the system still needs taste and that his results come partly from 6 years of experience.
- "I don't have anything to sell you" near the top of his money and organic marketing articles.
- Experience lines: "This comes from 6+ years of experience", "I only speak about things I know".

### The same idea from video creators

- Sam Gaudet opens every video with proof, promise and plan, in any order but all three. Proof answers "why should I listen to you?"
- Sam's long-form opening adds "handle objections": kill "this won't work for me" early (his example script: "the same six phases I took over 10,000 of my coaching clients through").
- Kallaway's trust ladder for the first 10 to 30 seconds, strongest to weakest: a result for someone like them, your own result, a big following or cosign, "I was where you are", common ground, a polished setup. Flash proof fast.

### Frameworks

**The 8 trust moves** (Openings study (Koe, Ryan, Tim Denning, Shann, Sahil Bloom, domschats, Paul Graham, Justin Welsh))

1. I was where you are, with a hard-specific low point
2. Mirror their feelings with details
3. Say their doubt out loud
4. Proof before teaching
5. Proof of effort
6. Not written by AI
7. Honest limits
8. Give the other side its due, then turn

### Examples

- **Ryan, honest limits:** His brand article says it is NOT a masterclass on paid ads and points readers to another creator for that.
  <br>*Why it works:* Saying what the piece isn't makes the rest more believable.
- **Sahil Bloom, mirroring:** His overwhelm piece describes the reader's body signals (a tight chest, a heavy body) before offering help.
  <br>*Why it works:* The reader feels seen through details, not through a claim of understanding.

### Common mistakes

- "I get it" said with no details from the reader's life. The study says it reads fake.
- Fake low points or invented numbers.
- Insulting the reader harder than you would say to a friend.

### Try it

Write three different first paragraphs for the same article: one using "I was where you are" with a real, specific low point, one using proof before teaching, and one using honest limits. Read them aloud and keep the one you'd believe as a stranger.

## 7.5 The opening, part 2: attention and clarity

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** After trust, the opening must make the reader need to keep going and know exactly what they'll get. The openings study found 9 attention moves and 6 clarity moves, plus 5 things almost every top opening shares.

### 9 attention moves ("I need to keep reading")

- Hard truth about the reader: one blunt line that stings a little. Tim Denning ("most people are forgettable"), Koe ("most creators are content factories").
- Flip a belief: a short thesis against what everyone says. Koe (personal brands are no longer personal; the most profitable niche is you), pmitu (posting more is not how you grow, written in caps).
- Attack the common advice: name the advice they keep hearing ("niche down", "just be consistent") and show why it fails. Koe does this in 5 of his 12 openings.
- A viral claim, checked: quote what everyone is sharing, agree with the fear, then reject and reframe it. Koe's "36 months" and "\$1M with AI" letters.
- The window is closing: a deadline or a "you're early" line. Shann (12 months), Koe (36 months), Hussain (the AI era).
- Story cold open: start inside one person's story and save the point for after the surprise. Housel (two investors, the janitor beat the Harvard banker), Tim Denning (Bharat's speech), Sahil Bloom (a split-brain patient), Koe (a teen watching gym creators).
- Famous quote first: borrowed authority. Hussain opens with Sam Altman and Naval; Koe opens sections with Bezos and Altman.
- A big number about what they already have: Koe's "\$100,000 is sitting in your notes", Hussain's "You have a \$100,000 digital product stuck in your head".
- A rhetorical question the reader answers in their head. Housel ("in what other field could this happen?"), Koe ("do you only read about one topic when you scroll?").

### 6 clarity moves ("this is for me, and I know what I'll get")

- Who it's for: name the reader in the first line or in a "this IS for you if" list. Ryan does it in 3 of 3 articles; domschats names "founder, Head of Social, Head of Content, CMO". Ryan's first line names three identities at once so more people see themselves.
- Roadmap: a short list of what's coming. Ryan, domschats (3 phases), Koe (4 bullets, 7 topics), Housel (20 flaws).
- Time or value promise: "learn 5 years of lessons in 10 minutes" (domschats), "if someone had given me this article 6 years ago, it would've been the most valuable thing I'd read" (Ryan). It frames the article as a shortcut.
- TL;DR on top: the whole point in 2 lines before anything else (Ryan).
- The handoff line: the last line of the opening pulls the reader into part 1. "Let me help.", a colon, a heading that promises a turn, or ending at rock bottom. Koe uses one in all 12 letters. Tim Denning's version: "Here's how to articulate your story, so you can do the same."
- Ask for the like early: a light "a like and repost go a long way" (Ryan, every article).

### What almost all top openings share

- The reader appears in the first 2 to 3 lines, as "you" or as a named group.
- One concrete, true detail early: a number, a name, a place, a debt.
- The writer is human in it: a failure, a fear, a joke about themselves.
- The opening ends with a pull into part 1, never a soft fade.
- The "I get it" part is shown with details from the reader's life, never just said.

### Related advice from other sources

- Kallaway: say "you", not "I", early. "I've struggled with skin problems my whole life" makes people ask if they're like you. "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life" makes it about them. His own hook used "you" 9 times and "I" 0 times.
- Kallaway on clarity: a hook needs topic clarity plus "this is for me" curiosity. Confusing wording or doubt about who it's for loses people.
- Sam Gaudet's road mapping (video): show early how many steps you'll take people through, and blur the later ones so they wonder what's coming.
- Oren's "8 Mile" script (video): name the other side's arguments yourself and knock them down, which kills objections while people are thinking them.

### Examples

- **Morgan Housel, story cold open:** His essay opens on two investors, where the janitor ends up beating the Harvard-trained banker, and only then gives the principle.
  <br>*Why it works:* The surprise comes first, the point after it, so the reader needs to know why.
- **Koe, the handoff line:** All 12 of Koe's studied letters end the opening with a pull into part 1, such as "Let me help." or ending at rock bottom.
  <br>*Why it works:* The opening never fades out; the last line moves the reader into the body.

### Common mistakes

- Opening about yourself before the reader appears (the study finds the reader in the first 2 to 3 lines).
- Ending the opening with a soft fade instead of a handoff line.
- A roadmap or promise that doesn't match the title.

## 7.6 Five opening recipes

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** The openings study found the moves come in a few set orders. Pick the recipe that fits the article type: pain, opinion, how-to guide, one big idea, or a trend you want to question.

### The 5 recipes (order of moves)

- "I get it" recipe: mirror their situation, say their doubt out loud, "I was there too" with a real low point, "you're not failing because of you, you're doing the wrong things", then "let me help". Best for pain topics (stuck, lost, no growth). Koe's "beginner hell" letter and his interests article.
- Flip recipe: blunt claim, why the common advice fails, what people really want, a thesis line, then the roadmap. Best for opinion articles. Koe's niche and personal brand letters, pmitu.
- Masterclass recipe: who it's for, proof (numbers plus years), "not written by AI" plus honest limits, roadmap, go. Best for big how-to guides. Ryan, domschats.
- Story recipe: one person's story, the surprise, the principle behind it, then "and that's true for you too". Best for one big idea. Housel, Tim Denning, Sahil Bloom.
- Myth check recipe: quote the viral claim, "it makes sense, here's why people believe it", "but I don't think it's true", then the reframe. Best for trend and AI-fear topics. Koe's "36 months" and "\$1M with AI" letters.

### Dan Koe's own intro shape (from his 226M-view article)

- Start with the problem, what's wrong, or the enemy, said bluntly (there: New Year's resolutions most people won't keep).
- Then talk as an equal: "I'm not here to judge you, I've dropped 10x more goals than I've hit."
- One paragraph that previews the whole piece and the transformation.
- Set expectations: "this is long, save it, grab a notebook". Koe says saves help the algorithm and stop people quitting halfway.

### Koe's "Full Course" article opening

- It opens on a pain ("If you have multiple interests, you probably have no idea what to do with your life").
- It attacks common advice ("choose a target audience", "niche down").
- It shares his story as proof, then states the simple promise, then moves into numbered steps.
- Koe on story openers in general: starting with your own story is how you make a validated topic unique.

### The video version of the same idea (Sam Gaudet)

- Sam's long-form opening, "reassure, elevate, open a loop", is his expanded promise, proof, plan. He says you have the first 30 seconds: "This isn't 2016 anymore", you can't clickbait people into staying.
- Reassure: a promise that matches the title, fast proof ("why you?"), and handle the objection "this won't work for me".
- Elevate: scale the promise with extras or bonuses, and add urgency (time-based, or something new like an AI release).
- Open a loop: show the roadmap with later steps hidden, and name a unique mechanism, something "familiar but unknown" (his example: "the 10-80-10 rule").

## 7.7 Ryan's article skeleton, top to bottom

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan's articles (2,000 to 2,450 words, 179k to 461k views by his counts) all share one 12-part skeleton. It makes a long guide feel like a free paid course: what it is, why trust it, what's inside, the teaching, then a recap and a close.

### The 12 parts

- 1\. Subtitle or TL;DR: one line saying what this is and that it's complete ("TL;DR: The new, updated playbook for growing a massive company and/or personal brand in 2026 and beyond", "A practical playbook for organic reach, starting from zero.").
- 2\. Promise: 1 to 2 lines of stakes and payoff, said once, early ("Branding is one of the only moats left. Nail this, and it could quite literally change your entire business trajectory").
- 3\. Roadmap: "In today's masterclass, I'll teach you:", "This article is quite dense, so here's your roadmap:", or "Your roadmap:", then 4 to 5 section names.
- 4\. Trust lines: where it comes from plus "not AI" ("This article comes from 6+ years of organic content experience. This wasn't written by AI").
- 5\. Early soft CTA: one casual line ("btw, if you enjoy this content, a like, repost, and follow go a long way").
- 6\. Who it's for and not for: "This IS for you if:" with 3 bullets, plus "This is NOT a masterclass on...".
- 7\. 3 to 5 big sections with short noun headers ("BMF (Brand-Market Fit)", "Selecting Vehicles", "Packaging & Formatting", "Operations & Systems").
- 8\. Inside each section: numbered sub-points, each a bold label, 1 to 3 short lines, then a list or text box, then a real brand example.
- 9\. Section TL;DR: a big section ends with "TL;DR of crafting your story:" and 3 lines.
- 10\. Final tips: "Final Tips & Some Advice" with 5 to 7 one-liners ("Double down on outliers. When something pops, make 5 more versions").
- 11\. Recap: one line per big section.
- 12\. Close: thanks, CTA, "I respond to every comment", "tell me what to write next", one proof line, and a last push ("Now go build a massive brand. It will change your life").

### The opening's first 120 words do the selling

- Who it's for in the first line, with three identities so more people see themselves ("If you create content, are looking to hire a Head of Content, or want to become one, this is for you").
- Why trust me: "I've spent my entire career obsessing over building world-class brands", "6+ years", "thousands of dollars and hours of trial and error".
- The wish line: "if someone had given me this article 6 years ago, it would've been the most valuable thing I'd read". It frames the article as a shortcut.
- Honesty markers: "This wasn't written by AI", "I only speak about things I know", "This is NOT a masterclass on paid ads".
- The big promise in one line: stakes plus payoff, once.

### What his newer articles added

- "Why listen to me?" near the top: his numbers and what he says he pioneered.
- "For:" line under every path in his money article ("For: those starting from zero", "For: those with an existing product") so each reader finds their part fast.
- "Expectations" section and "I don't have anything to sell you" near the top.
- The condensed option: "for a condensed version, screenshot this post and watch my video at 2x speed".
- The ask up front (copywriting article): "All I ask for in revealing this information is two things": follow if it was valuable, repost or like so others see it.
- Copy-paste prompts in text boxes in every step of both Grok Bot guides. His best newer articles are the ones with prompts.
- Written for an AI to use: "tag your @bot to digest this article". Push posts say "Send this prompt to Grok" and "The smart ones will inject this prompt". The article is framed as something to feed an AI, not only to read.
- His organic marketing article was about 1,500 words, "more if you count the code blocks", with Venn diagrams and lists. He tells viewers to screenshot the visuals.

### Overall shape and numbers

- Length 2,000 to 2,450 words for his first three, 3 to 5 big sections, about 20 to 30 numbered sub-points in total.
- Mostly lists, little prose. Paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines; almost every point ends in a bullet list.
- Long overall but never dense, because of lists and boxes.
- No studies and no long stories. Authority comes from experience lines, examples and frameworks.
- Every few sections, a one-line punch ("Distribution is everything", "Content is very coachable").

### Frameworks

**Ryan's 12-part article skeleton** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Subtitle or TL;DR
2. Promise
3. Roadmap
4. Trust lines
5. Early soft CTA
6. Who it's for and not for
7. 3 to 5 big sections

10. Final tips
11. Recap
12. Close: thanks, CTA, reply promise, what next, proof line, last push

## 7.8 Inside a section: text boxes and teaching moves

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan turns every abstract idea into a box readers can screenshot. Inside each section he defines the thing simply, numbers the points, names real examples and talks like a person. The box is what gets saved.

### How each section teaches (Ryan)

- Define the thing simply first: "What is a Head of Content?", "Brand PMF is when a specific audience instantly understands what you stand for...".
- Then numbered sub-points, each with a bold label, 1 to 3 short lines of why, then a list or an example.
- Questions the reader answers about themselves: "What do you stand for? Who is it for? Why should they care?"
- Real names as examples, one line each, named and tagged: Duolingo's owl, Polymarket and Kalshi, @fomo, Wispr Flow, Tesla's 2006 master plan, OpenAI Dev Days, Iman Gadzhi.
- Pros and cons boxes when comparing two options (founder-led vs brand-led).
- Opinions said with confidence: "Hire for taste and potential, train for skill", "Your team is your ceiling".
- Asides that sound like a person talking: "I could make this list very long but you get the point", "etc etc", "For lack of a better term".
- Tiers of skill that push the reader on: "These 5 skills are the bare bones of a 6/10... to be an 11/10 you'll have to go deeper".
- "If you take away only one thing from this article, make it this:" right before the main idea.
- Credits other creators inside the article ("If you want to learn about ads, I recommend watching...").

### Text boxes (5 to 7 per article)

- X articles show code blocks as clean monospace boxes. Ryan uses them as frameworks people screenshot and bookmark.
- Dot leaders: Smart ........ "I learn something every time" (his emotion table), TOF ....... Get discovered.
- Arrows: "Who are you? → What you do" (his 7 story questions), "Chapter 1 → Build an expensive sports car" (Tesla's master plan).
- Tables: Vehicle / Best for / Funnel / Effort for 7 platforms. In newer articles: the algorithm weight table, hooks by format, CTA by action, CTA by funnel.

1. Bold label
2. 1 to 3 short lines of why
3. A list or a text box
4. A real, named example

## 7.10 Drafting, AI help and the taste you add

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Drafting is a process: get it out messy first, tighten later. Bloom and Shann both show a split of work where the tool does production and the writer does taste.

### Bloom's drafting process

- Bloom writes threads unstructured first. He does not worry about how bad it is, word counts or character counts. Then he distills it into something tighter and punchier for Twitter.
- Sometimes he writes broad life-principle pieces just to clarify his own thinking or shift his own mood. Writing is his process, and he happens to share it.
- He makes sure he will be proud of a thread before posting. He never deletes threads, except if someone rightly calls out that he did not think it through. He sometimes deletes single tweets that are not hitting.
- Koe says writing and outlining a book shaped every newsletter and tweet he wrote afterwards, so a long piece also trains the short ones.

### Write the article worth distributing (Shann)

- Shann's split: AI writes structure and first drafts, the human rewrites the hook and makes the editorial calls. He calls it 80% AI, 20% taste.
- His aimarketing.sh example: tools like Gamma, Chronicle and Beautiful.ai gave "the same flavor of fine".
- So he spent days on the research first, asking what stories the data tells, what makes someone care about these numbers, and what narrative arc holds it together.
- He studied presentation design: data density, tension and release in conference talks, visual hierarchy.
- Then he split the work: one model wrote the storyline and copy, another produced the visuals, and he directed both with references, constraints and examples. His line: "The AI did the production. i brought the taste."

### Frameworks

**Unstructured first, then distill** (Bloom)

1. Write it all out with no limits.
2. Cut it into something tighter and punchier.
3. Post only what you will be proud of.

**Shann's 80/20** (Shann Holmberg)

1. Research the story the data tells.
2. Let AI do structure, copy and visuals.
3. Rewrite the hook yourself and make the editorial calls.

### Examples

- **Why Shann did not use the slide tools:** They all gave "the same flavor of fine"
  <br>*Why it works:* Generic output is the reason the writer's research and taste matter.

### Common mistakes

- Letting the tool pick the hook
- Posting a thread you would not be proud of
- Skipping the research and expecting the tool to find the story

## 7.12 Pacing and structure borrowed from long-form video advice

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam never teaches written articles. The sources label some of his video lessons as carrying over to writing. Treat these as video advice applied to writing, and Kallaway's frameworks as general.

### What Sam says applies to writing

- Asymmetric pacing works for "videos or copy or emails" and LinkedIn copy: shorter early points, longer later ones (Brand Training).
- A hook is the first few words of an email or a tweet too (7 Strategies).
- HEIT works on all platforms (Brand Training).
- Kallaway's four-step addiction loop works for YouTube videos, shorts, emails, tweets and sales calls. His hook formula (context lean, interjection, snapback) works in any type of content, in conversation and in life. His sources give no article-specific advice beyond that.

### What carries over from his video advice (an application, not something Sam says)

- Promise, proof, plan to open (what is this, why me, how I will get you there), then a numbered plan.
- Road mapping: say up front how many parts there are.
- Point, explain, illustrate, lesson, repeated per section, with each section bridging to the next.
- Progressive density: broad problem first, the dense tactical part for core readers at the end.
- The sacred timeline: cut every story, section or paragraph that drifts from the title's promise.
- Too slow, not too long: length is fine if every part earns its place (15 Brutal Truths). Information density beats viral reach.
- Long form builds the relationship faster than short pieces (4 Billion Views). One great piece gets found for years (15 Brutal Truths).
- The CTA about 30% in, not only at the end, and remind once at the end (M10).

### Frameworks

**Section loop** (Sam Gaudet (video, applied to writing))

1. Point
2. Explain
3. Illustrate
4. Lesson, then bridge to the next section

### Examples

- **Asymmetric pacing:** Short early sections, longer later ones
  <br>*Why it works:* Readers feel progress.

### Common mistakes

- Treating the carry-over as something Sam said about articles
- Padding a piece that drifts from the title

### Try it

## 7.13 Pushing an article after it is published

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan treats every article as a campaign: videos, quotes with bookmark asks and prompts around it. The sources give his exact order and closers, and the caveat that this is a reading of the views.

### Ryan's push system

- Every article gets pushed: a quote of his own article with a bookmark-ask hook (2 to 3 per article), then a video version of the article with chapters.
- The video goes out a few days later as a quote of the article. The pattern is confirmed in all 5 article videos.
- His algorithm-article push (Nov 1 to 4): video with chapters (393k views), then a quote of the video "Someone reading this is going to blow up" (423k, 8,158 bookmarks), then the article (461k), then a quote of the article with the prompt angle (156k), then a meme quote of the article (340k).
- For the copywriting article: the article, then a prompt quote the same day (30k), then the video with chapters the next day.
- Push posts framed the article as a prompt: "Send this prompt to Grok @bot", "The smart ones will inject this prompt into @bot".
- His organic marketing article was about 1,500 words, with Venn diagrams and lists, and he tells viewers to screenshot the visuals.

### Closers

- Ryan's end line "I plan to write 1-2 articles/week" gives people a reason to follow for the next one.

### A pattern in Ryan's view counts (seen in his numbers, not something he said)

- The big three (179k to 327k) were each a new topic: the role, branding, distribution.
- The HOC roadmap (42k) repeated his first article's topic, and the organic marketing guide (26k) overlapped with two earlier pieces. Repeat topics got 5 to 10 times fewer views.
- The algorithm article beat all 7 earlier ones. It was a fresh topic tied to news, and the video had warmed people up two days before. All numbers are Ryan's reported figures, not checked.

### Frameworks

**Ryan's article push** (Ryan)

1. Quote of own article with a bookmark-ask hook (2 to 3).
2. Video version with chapters, a few days later as a quote.
3. Prompt-angle and meme quotes.

### Examples

- **Algorithm article, Nov 1 to 4:** video 393k, video quote 423k, article 461k, prompt quote 156k, meme quote 340k
  <br>*Why it works:* Figures as reported.

### Common mistakes

- Publishing and walking away
- Repeating a topic you already covered

## Cheat sheet: Articles and long-form writing

The whole module on one page.

- Turn your best short ideas into longer pieces (Koe).
- Keep one short-form and one long-form platform (Kallaway, Koe).
- Pick a big, fresh topic; repeats do worse (Ryan's numbers).
- Only write what you have earned (Ryan, Codie Sanchez).
- Make the promise extreme, specific and timely (Koe).
- Write the hook last, 5 to 10 versions (Ryan).
- Title: big outcome, no confusion (Ryan).
- If you pay off the promise, nobody minds the hook (Ship 30).
- Put proof before teaching.
- Say what the article is not (honest limits).
- Show the I get it with real details, never just say it.
- End the opening with a pull into part 1.
- Name who it is for in the first line.
- Add a TL;DR, roadmap and section TL;DRs (Ryan).
- Use text boxes people can screenshot (Ryan).
- Short lines, simple words (Ryan, Ship 30).
- Write messy first, then tighten (Bloom).
- AI does production, you bring the taste (Shann).
- Quote your own article 2 to 3 times with a bookmark ask (Ryan).
- Close with a like, repost, follow ask and when the next one comes (Ryan).
