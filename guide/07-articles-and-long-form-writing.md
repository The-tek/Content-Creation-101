## 7.1 Why long form builds authority

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Short posts get you seen. Long pieces get you believed. Articles, newsletters, books and long videos are where people spend real time with you, and that time is what turns a stranger into someone who trusts you.

### Time under attention

- The more time people have spent with your long content, the more they trust your short posts. Think of it as time under attention: every minute someone spends reading or watching you adds to your credit.
- Example: when James Clear, the author of Atomic Habits, posts "habits are good", it gets thousands of likes. If a random account posts the same line, people reply "yeah, obviously". The idea is identical. The difference is how much time people already spent with the writer.
- This also means the credit goes to whoever wrote the long version. When someone posts about habits, most people think of James Clear, not of whoever tweeted it. If you want your name attached to an idea, write the long piece about it.
- One creator who has two books, years of YouTube and newsletter articles with over 100,000 views says this lets him post much simpler things, because people read them through everything else they know about him.
- Turning your best short ideas into longer content spreads your name without extra effort. It builds trust far deeper than you get from people who only post short content.
- Compare the numbers. A short post holds someone's attention for about 10 seconds. A video, podcast or newsletter holds it for 15 to 60 minutes. Whoever holds the most attention over time wins the most influence. Short form is still useful, so don't drop it.

### Long minutes are stickier

- One minute of long content sticks to people more than one minute of short content, and a minute of a live stream sticks even more. The reason: the longer someone stays with you in one go, the more each extra minute counts.
- A simple way to think about it: the value of your personal brand grows with how many people watch, how many minutes they spend, and how sticky those minutes are. Long form is how you raise the last two.
- A simple setup for a modern personal brand has 3 parts: one short-form platform to reach new people, one long-form platform to go deep (YouTube is one example, X articles are another), and one place you own to keep in touch (an email list or a private community). That is all you need to start.
- A suggested rhythm is about 7 short pieces a week and 1 to 2 long pieces a week. Expect it to take hundreds of posts before it pays off.

### More reasons to write long

- A 20 to 30 minute piece gets people closer to who you are than a 30-second clip. Long form builds the relationship faster.
- One great piece keeps getting found for years. Some well-known creators have videos that still get views years later because they put quality into them.
- Think of 4 kinds of content: long video builds deep trust, short video gives raw reach, long writing (articles, newsletters) gives depth and authority, and short writing (posts) is a playground to test ideas fast. X alone lets you do all 4.
- Information is free everywhere now, but people still pay for it when they feel connected to the person giving it. One big creator advises spending 5 to 10 hours learning Substack (a newsletter platform), because if you write well, a newsletter brings customers to your business.
- The popular written format keeps changing: Quora answers, then Medium articles, then Twitter threads, then LinkedIn carousels, now X articles. If a format keeps showing up in your feed, it is there for a reason.
- Writing long also sharpens your own thinking. One writer says outlining a book shaped every newsletter and tweet he wrote afterwards.

### What the numbers on X show

- One creator tracked his first 11 days of posting on X (40 posts). His 2 articles had a median of 194,000 views and 3,700 bookmarks. Plain text posts had a median of 2,400 views and 13 bookmarks. These are his own reported numbers, not checked by anyone else.
- Across his first 26 days he reports 2.9 million views over 97 posts, and his top post was a beginner guide article with 326,000 views.
- His later article about how the X algorithm works (461,000 views) beat all 7 of his earlier articles.

### Frameworks

**The simple personal brand setup**

1. Reach: one short-form platform, posted often
2. Depth: one long-form platform, 1 to 2 pieces a week
3. Stay in touch: one place you own, like an email list or a private community

### Examples

- **James Clear and habits:** Anyone can post that habits are important. But when James Clear posts it, people listen, because they have read his book. His simple tweets about habits get thousands of likes.
  <br>*Why it works:* Long form changes how people read your short posts, and the credit for an idea goes to whoever wrote the long version.

### Common mistakes

- Only posting short content and expecting the deep trust that long content builds.
- Dropping short posts completely. They don't build as much trust, but they still bring new people to your long pieces.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say text does not travel as well as video, and that readers now wonder whether AI wrote it. Others say written long form is where authority and leads come from, and point out that their own articles are their biggest posts.

### Try it

List the 3 ideas you have posted about most in short form. For each one, write a line: what would a reader need to learn in 15 minutes to really understand this idea? Pick the idea with the longest answer as your first long piece.

## 7.2 Choosing what the article is about

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The topic does most of the work. Build the article from ideas that already did well as short posts, and pick a big, fresh topic that a specific group of people urgently wants.

### Build from ideas that already worked

- Think of your article as a greatest-hits album. One writer's most-viewed X article, "How to fix your entire life in 1 day" (he reports 226 million views), had 7 sections, and each section was an idea that had already done well as a post. One section title was an old tweet copied word for word. Every idea inside was already a proven reason for people to read.
- Test small, then expand. A single tweet that does better than usual becomes a thread. The best tweet in that thread becomes its own post. Most of your followers never saw what you posted 6 months ago, so reusing a winner is fine.
- Treat short posts as your playground for testing ideas fast. The winners become articles or long videos.
- Use your messages as a topic source. When 3 or more people ask you the same question, make content about it. One creator says a crash course that got 202,000 views came from exactly this.
- For a newsletter, find a YouTube video that blew up in your topic, use its subject as yours, and write your own take without watching it. Good newsletters work a lot like good videos.

### Pick a big, fresh topic

- The view counts of one creator's articles show a pattern. His three biggest early articles (179,000 to 327,000 views) were each on a new topic: the Head of Content job, branding and distribution. A roadmap that repeated the topic of his first article got 42,000 views, and a guide that overlapped two earlier ones got 26,000. Repeated topics got 5 to 10 times fewer views.
- His two guides about the Grok bot (219,000 and 103,000 views) rode a hot new tool and gave real prompts people could copy. His biggest article, about how the X algorithm works (461,000 views), covered a fresh topic tied to the news: X had just released its algorithm's code.
- Those articles spread because each had a big, sharp topic that a specific group urgently wanted (for example, content jobs as a gold rush, or branding as the only thing AI can't copy), and each felt like a paid course given away free.
- The writer of the 226 million view article admits it was not mind-blowing, and says much of it was fairly common knowledge. What made it work was the formatting and the way it was presented to a specific audience. You don't need secret information. You need the right package for the right reader.
- A study of top X article titles found the winners promise a big outcome everybody wants, like money, escape or being known, instead of a narrow skill. They often add a reason to read now, like fear of losing a job to AI, and offer a calm system as the answer.

### Make the promise extreme, specific and timely

- The 226 million view article promised something extreme with a deadline: a change in 1 day. That clashed with what people believe, which is that change takes years. The writer says the promise also forced him to be creative enough to really deliver it.
- It matched the moment. It was posted in January, and the intro attacked New Year's resolutions: most people won't keep them, and here is why.
- It gave a system, not loose tips: a full plan for one day (morning, afternoon and night), and it told readers to set calendar reminders. The writer says this put him into their day, so they never forgot the article. When you change what someone does, they credit you for it.
- It taught a concept most readers had never heard of (9 stages of ego development, a theory of how people grow). Concepts like that usually come from books, not from AI chats or scrolling. Teaching people something new is what makes them follow you.
- Timing matters for any piece. Say a creator wrote a take on a hyped new app. It would work at the peak of the hype. A month later, or 3 months earlier, it would flop.

### Only write what you have earned

- Only write about things you have done and are good at. Readers can tell when you are faking.
- One creator quotes a person who switched from "how to" posts to "how I" posts, because they decided they shouldn't teach anything they hadn't done themselves.

### Frameworks

**The greatest-hits article**

1. Find your posts on one theme that already did well
2. Make each proven idea a section of the article
3. If an old post fits, use it as a section title
4. Wrap them all in one big promise

**Test small, then expand**

1. Post the idea as a single short post
2. If it does better than usual, expand it into a longer piece
3. Pull the best line from the long piece and post it on its own

### Examples

- **"How to fix your entire life in 1 day":** An article of 5,000 to 6,000 words, posted in January against New Year's resolutions. It gave a one-day plan with reminders, taught a concept readers hadn't heard of, and had 7 sections that were each proven posts. The writer reports 226 million views and 339,000 likes.
  <br>*Why it works:* Every idea inside was a reason to share it, not just the title.
- **A repeated topic:** One creator's roadmap article repeated the topic of his first article and got 42,000 views, against 179,000 to 327,000 for his articles on new topics.
  <br>*Why it works:* Each article needs a fresh topic or a fresh angle, not a remix of the last one.

### Common mistakes

- Writing an article that remixes the topic of your last article.
- Starting from a blank page instead of from ideas that already worked as posts.
- Teaching something you haven't done yourself.

### Try it

Sort your last 50 posts by bookmarks or replies. Find 5 to 7 winners that share one theme. Write one promise that ties them together, then list each winner as a section heading.

## 7.3 Titles and first lines

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** An article has three hooks in a row: the title, the subtitle and the first paragraph. The titles that worked on X promise a big outcome, signal that the piece is complete, and make a promise the article can really keep.

### How hooks work in an article

- In an article, the title, the subtitle and the first paragraph each need to work as their own hook. Each one has a single job: get the reader to the next one.
- The only job of your first line is to get someone to read your second line. The same goes for threads: tweet 1 only has to get tweet 2 read, and most people drop off in the first 2 to 3 tweets.
- Write the article first and the hook last. Write 5 to 10 versions using different hook types, then cut the weakest ones right away.

### Title formats that worked

- These come from a study of top X articles, with views as the creators reported them.
- A deadline claim, "Everyone [doing X] has [time] to [do Y]". Example: "Everyone using AI has about 12 months to develop these 3 moats" (252,000 views).
- A tool plus a job title, "[Tool] as Your [Job Title] (FULL GUIDE)" (159,000 views). Or a skill compared to the best, "How to [skill] like the top 0.01%" (127,000 views).
- Proof inside the title, "How we [big number] and made [result]" (114,000 views), or "How to go from 0-100M views/month" (103,000 views). Another shape is "The Psychology of a [X]" (70,000 views).
- Newer labels that promise the complete version: "In Depth", "(Dense Guide)" and "(actually)". The biggest article in the study was "The X Algorithm Explained, In Depth" (461,000 views), a timely topic plus "In Depth".

### What the winning titles share

- A big outcome that everyone wants, like money, escape or being known, instead of a narrow skill.
- A word or bracket that promises the whole thing: masterclass, full course, full guide, 3 strategies, 101.
- Often a fear of AI or of losing your job as the reason to read now, followed by a calm system as the answer.
- Proof, like numbers or screenshots, comes before the teaching.

### First-line formats from top articles

- Scary numbers first: real public numbers that worry the reader before the promise arrives. One article opened with layoffs at Amazon, Dell and Oracle.
- A desire or a pain, for readers who don't know you yet. Start with some version of "if you want..." or "if you don't want...". One example: "If you have multiple interests, you probably have no idea what to do with your life." Most people on social media aren't there to learn, so open with a broad desire or pain, then get specific.
- Say who it's for, and put a TL;DR (a two-line summary) at the top.

### Keep the promise honest

- There is no such thing as a clickbait headline, only clickbait content. If the article pays off the promise, nobody minds the hook. "Want to know a secret?" followed by "work hard" is a broken promise.
- Getting the click is not the goal. Keeping attention is. If the headline promises something and the content doesn't deliver, readers leave. Write hooks that are strong but accurate.
- Good headlines sell the outcome, not the product.
- Video creators say the same thing: when the title and thumbnail promise one thing and the opening talks about another, viewers leave. Some say this is the biggest cause of drop-off today.
- A real study published in 2023 (Nature Human Behaviour) tested about 105,000 headline variations on Upworthy, a news site. For an average headline, each extra negative word raised the click rate by 2.3%, and sadness drove clicks the most.
- A real study from 2012 looked at about 7,000 New York Times articles. Articles with high-energy emotions like awe, anger or anxiety were shared more. Sadness made people less likely to share, and positive content was more viral overall. So a sad hook can win the click, but awe or a bit of anger wins the share.

### Frameworks

**Three hooks in a row**

1. Title: a big promise with a clear outcome and nothing to decode
2. Subtitle or TL;DR: what the article is and that it is complete
3. First paragraph: its own hook that gets the next paragraph read

### Examples

- **"Distribution 101: A Guide to Reaching Millions":** A familiar label (101) plus a big outcome (reaching millions). It got 297,000 views, about 28 times the creator's normal.
  <br>*Why it works:* Clear topic, a signal that the guide is complete, a big outcome, and nothing to decode.
- **"Everyone using AI has about 12 months to develop these 3 moats":** A deadline, a group the reader belongs to, and a numbered promise. It got 252,000 views.
  <br>*Why it works:* The deadline is the reason to read now, and the 3 moats are the calm answer.

### Common mistakes

- A title the reader has to think about.
- A headline the article doesn't pay off.
- A narrow-skill title when a big outcome would draw more readers.

### Try it

Take one article idea and write 10 titles, one in each format from this lesson. Cut any that need a second read to understand. Then check the last 3 against your content: can the article really pay off that promise?

## 7.4 The opening, part 1: earning trust

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** The opening is everything before the main teaching starts. Its first job is to make the reader think "this person gets me and can help me". A study of 27 top article openings found 8 moves that build that trust.

### What the study looked at

- The study looked at 27 openings: 12 letters from one newsletter writer, his long course article (655,000 views), X articles by 9 other creators, and several famous essays on topics like the psychology of money and doing great work.
- Most openings run 150 to 450 words. The newsletter writer's run 135 to 900, usually about 300. One famous essay's opening is only about 90 words. The long openings are almost all story.
- The moves fall into three jobs: trust ("this person gets me and can help me"), attention ("I need to keep reading") and clarity ("this is for me and I know what I'll get"). This lesson covers trust. The next one covers the other two.

### The 8 trust moves

- 1\. I was where you are. The writer was in the reader's exact situation, failed, and found a way out. The low point needs hard specifics: a debt amount, a side job, switching majors 5 times, being too scared to speak. Say a writer was broke the day after payday, or says "I learned this the hard way". Use only a low point that really happened to you.
- 2\. Mirror their feelings. Describe the reader's life back to them: what they feel, what they have tried, what they fear, even how it feels in the body, like a tight chest or a heavy body. Then follow with "if you feel lost" or "I know you're on the edge".
- 3\. Say their doubt out loud. Voice the thought the reader is too embarrassed to say, like "was this all a scam?" or "I bet you clicked for a quick way to make money".
- 4\. Proof before teaching. One line of real numbers before any advice. Examples from the study: 3 billion views, growing from 400 to 26,000 followers, \$10,000 in year one to \$4 million, a studio making \$1.7 million a year, or 6+ years of experience.
- 5\. Proof of effort. Show the years, hours or money that went into the article, like 4 years of books and lectures, hundreds of hours, or thousands of dollars of trial and error.
- 6\. "Not written by AI". This is a newer trust line, for example "None of this article was written by AI".
- 7\. Honest limits. Say what the article is NOT, or what you don't know, and point to someone better. Examples: "not a masterclass on paid ads" (and recommending another creator for ads), "only for the very ambitious", "no get-rich-quick here".
- 8\. Give the other side its due, then turn. Admit the common view is partly right before you disagree. For example: social media has obvious downsides, but... Or: connections really are an advantage, so build them instead of resenting them.

### Trust lines in practice

- A "Why listen to me?" section near the top: your numbers and what you did, before any teaching.
- Proof first. One guide about a Grok-based tool opens with "Firstly, the elephant in the room: proof of the results" and a screenshot.
- An honest disclaimer on a technical topic: "I am NOT from the X algorithm team. This is purely my interpretation." Saying what you are not builds trust.
- An "Expectations" section where the writer admits the system still needs good taste, and that part of the results come from 6 years of experience.
- "I don't have anything to sell you" near the top of articles about money and organic marketing.
- Experience lines like "This comes from 6+ years of experience" or "I only speak about things I know".

### The same idea in video

- Open every video with proof, promise and plan. You can put them in any order, but use all three. Proof answers the viewer's question: why should I listen to you?
- A long opening should also handle objections. Kill the thought "this won't work for me" early, with a line like "the same six phases I took over 10,000 of my coaching clients through".
- In the first 10 to 30 seconds, use the strongest trust you have. From strongest to weakest: a result for someone like the viewer, your own result, a big following or a cosign from someone known, "I was where you are", common ground, a polished setup. Show the proof fast.

### Examples

- **Honest limits:** One brand article says it is NOT a masterclass on paid ads and points readers to another creator for that.
  <br>*Why it works:* Saying what the piece isn't makes the rest easier to believe.
- **Mirroring:** An article about feeling overwhelmed describes the reader's body (a tight chest, a heavy body) before it offers any help.
  <br>*Why it works:* The reader feels seen because of the details, not because you claimed to understand.

### Common mistakes

- Saying "I get it" with no details from the reader's life. It reads as fake.
- Inventing a low point or numbers.
- Being harsher on the reader than you would be with a friend.

### Try it

Write three different first paragraphs for the same article: one using "I was where you are" with a real, specific low point, one using proof before teaching, and one using honest limits. Read them aloud and keep the one you would believe as a stranger.

## 7.5 The opening, part 2: attention and clarity

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** After trust, the opening has to make the reader need to keep going, and make clear what they will get. The study found 9 moves for attention, 6 for clarity, and 5 things almost every top opening shares.

### 9 attention moves: "I need to keep reading"

- A hard truth about the reader. One blunt line that stings a little, like "most people are forgettable" or "most creators are content factories".
- Flip a belief. A short statement against what everyone says, like "personal brands are no longer personal" or "posting more is not how you grow".
- Attack common advice. Name the advice they keep hearing ("niche down", "just be consistent") and show why it fails. One newsletter writer does this in 5 of his 12 openings.
- Check a viral claim. Quote what everyone is sharing, agree with the fear, then reject it and reframe it. Example: the viral "you have 36 months" and "\$1M with AI" claims.
- The window is closing. A deadline or a "you're early" line, like 12 months, 36 months, or the AI era.
- Open with a story and save the point for after the surprise. Examples: two investors where the janitor beat the Harvard banker, a speech by a named person, or a teenager watching gym creators.
- A famous quote first. It borrows authority from someone well known. You can open the article, or each section, with quotes from known founders and thinkers.
- A big number about what the reader already has: "\$100,000 is sitting in your notes" or "You have a \$100,000 digital product stuck in your head".
- A question the reader answers in their head, like "in what other field could this happen?" or "do you only read about one topic when you scroll?"

### 6 clarity moves: "this is for me and I know what I'll get"

- Say who it's for. Name the reader in the first line or in a "this IS for you if" list. One creator does it in all 3 of his articles. Another names "founder, Head of Social, Head of Content, CMO". A first line can name three identities at once so more people see themselves in it.
- A roadmap: a short list of what's coming, like 3 phases, 4 bullets, 7 topics or 20 flaws.
- A promise of time or value, like "learn 5 years of lessons in 10 minutes" or "if someone had given me this article 6 years ago, it would have been the most valuable thing I'd read". It frames the article as a shortcut.
- A TL;DR on top: the whole point in 2 lines before anything else.
- A handoff line. The last line of the opening pulls the reader into part 1. Examples: "Let me help.", a colon, a heading that promises a turn, or ending at rock bottom. One newsletter writer uses one in all 12 letters. Another version: "Here's how to articulate your story, so you can do the same."
- Ask for the like early, in a light way: "a like and repost go a long way". One creator does this in every article.

### What almost all top openings share

- The reader shows up in the first 2 to 3 lines, as "you" or as a named group.
- One concrete, true detail comes early: a number, a name, a place, a debt.
- The writer sounds human: a failure, a fear, a joke about themselves.
- The opening ends with a pull into part 1, never a soft fade.
- "I get it" is shown with details from the reader's life, never just stated.

### Related advice from video

- Say "you", not "I", early. "I've struggled with skin problems my whole life" makes people ask whether they're like you. "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life" makes it about them. One example hook used "you" 9 times and "I" 0 times.
- A hook needs a clear topic plus the feeling "this is for me". Confusing wording, or doubt about who it's for, loses people.
- Show early how many steps you'll take people through, and blur the later ones so viewers wonder what is coming.
- Name the other side's arguments yourself and knock them down. This kills objections while people are still thinking them. A famous rap battle scene does this: the rapper says every insult about himself first, so his opponent has nothing left.

### Examples

- **Story cold open:** An essay opens on two investors, where the janitor ends up beating the Harvard-trained banker, and only then gives the principle.
  <br>*Why it works:* The surprise comes first and the point comes after, so the reader needs to know why.
- **The handoff line:** All 12 studied letters from one newsletter writer end their opening with a pull into part 1, such as "Let me help." or ending at rock bottom.
  <br>*Why it works:* The opening never fades out. Its last line moves the reader into the body.

### Common mistakes

- Talking about yourself before the reader appears. Put the reader in the first 2 to 3 lines.
- Ending the opening with a soft fade instead of a handoff line.
- A roadmap or promise that doesn't match the title.

### Try it

Take an opening you've written and check it against the 5 shared traits. Mark each one present or missing. Rewrite only the missing ones, then add a handoff line as the last sentence.

## 7.6 Five opening recipes

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** The best openings follow a few set orders of moves. Pick the recipe that fits your article: a pain, an opinion, a how-to guide, one big idea, or a trend you want to question.

### The 5 recipes

- The "I get it" recipe. Mirror the reader's situation, say their doubt out loud, tell them you were there too (with a real low point), tell them they are not failing because of who they are but because they have been doing the wrong things, then say "let me help". It is best for pain topics like feeling stuck, lost or not growing. Example: an article for beginners who feel stuck, or one for people with many interests who don't know what to pick.
- The flip recipe. Start with a blunt claim, explain why the common advice fails, say what people really want, state your main point in one line, then give the roadmap. It is best for opinion articles, like one arguing against "niche down".
- The masterclass recipe. Say who it's for, show proof (numbers plus years of experience), add "not written by AI" and your honest limits, give the roadmap, then start teaching. It is best for big how-to guides.
- The story recipe. Tell one person's story, show the surprise, state the principle behind it, then say "and that's true for you too". It is best when the whole article is about one big idea.
- The myth check recipe. Quote the viral claim, say "it makes sense, and here's why people believe it", then "but I don't think it's true", then give your reframe. It is best for trend and AI-fear topics, like the claims that you have 36 months left or can make \$1 million with AI.

### The intro shape of a 226 million view article

- Start with the problem, what's wrong, or the enemy, said bluntly. In this article, the enemy was New Year's resolutions that most people won't keep.
- Then talk to the reader as an equal: "I'm not here to judge you, I've dropped 10x more goals than I've hit."
- Add one paragraph that previews the whole piece and the change it promises.
- Set expectations: "this is long, save it, grab a notebook". The writer says saves help the algorithm and stop people quitting halfway.

### A "Full Course" article opening

- It opens on a pain: "If you have multiple interests, you probably have no idea what to do with your life".
- It attacks common advice, like "choose a target audience" and "niche down".
- It shares the writer's own story as proof, then states a simple promise, then moves into numbered steps.
- Starting with your own story is also how you make a topic that has already worked feel like yours.

### The video version of the same idea

- A long video opening has three jobs: reassure, make it bigger, and open a loop. It is the same idea as proof, promise and plan, stretched over the first 30 seconds. Clickbait alone can't keep people watching anymore.
- Reassure: make a promise that matches the title, show quick proof so people know why to listen to you, and answer the doubt "this won't work for me".
- Make it bigger: add extras or bonuses, and add urgency, like a time limit or something new such as a fresh AI release.
- Open a loop: show the roadmap but hide the later steps, and name your method. Pick a name that sounds familiar but new to the viewer, like "the 10-80-10 rule". The name makes people curious.

### Examples

- **The intro of "How to fix your entire life in 1 day":** It attacks New Year's resolutions bluntly, then says "I'm not here to judge you, I've dropped 10x more goals than I've hit", previews the whole piece in one paragraph, and ends with "this is long, save it, grab a notebook".
  <br>*Why it works:* The writer talks as an equal, previews the change, and asks for the save so people don't quit halfway.

### Common mistakes

- Using a story opening for a big how-to guide, or a masterclass opening for a single idea. Match the recipe to the article type.
- Breaking the promise of the title in the first lines.

### Try it

Pick one topic and draft two openings of about 150 words each with two different recipes (for example flip and story). Ask one person which made them want to read part 1, and why.

## 7.7 A 12-part article skeleton, top to bottom

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** One creator's big articles (about 2,000 to 2,450 words, 179,000 to 461,000 views by his own count) all follow the same 12-part skeleton. It makes a long guide feel like a paid course given away free: what it is, why trust it, what's inside, the teaching, then a recap and a close.

### The 12 parts

- 1\. Subtitle or TL;DR. One line saying what this is and that it's complete. Examples: "TL;DR: The new, updated playbook for growing a massive company and/or personal brand in 2026 and beyond" or "A practical playbook for organic reach, starting from zero."
- 2\. The promise. One or two lines about the stakes and the payoff, said once and early. Example: "Branding is one of the only moats left. Nail this, and it could quite literally change your entire business trajectory."
- 3\. The roadmap. A line like "In today's masterclass, I'll teach you:" or "This article is quite dense, so here's your roadmap:", then 4 to 5 section names.
- 4\. Trust lines. Where the article comes from, plus "not AI". Example: "This article comes from 6+ years of organic content experience. This wasn't written by AI."
- 5\. An early soft ask. One casual line, like "btw, if you enjoy this content, a like, repost, and follow go a long way".
- 6\. Who it's for and who it isn't for. "This IS for you if:" with 3 bullets, and "This is NOT a masterclass on...".
- 7\. 3 to 5 big sections with short headers made of nouns, like "Selecting Vehicles", "Packaging & Formatting" or "Operations & Systems".
- 8\. Inside each section, numbered sub-points. Each one has a bold label, 1 to 3 short lines, then a list or a text box, then a real brand example.
- 9\. A section TL;DR. A big section ends with a line like "TL;DR of crafting your story:" and 3 lines.
- 10\. Final tips. A part called "Final Tips & Some Advice" with 5 to 7 one-liners, like "Double down on outliers. When something pops, make 5 more versions."
- 11\. A recap. One line per big section.
- 12\. The close. Thank the reader, ask for a like, repost or follow, say "I respond to every comment", ask "tell me what to write next", add one proof line, and finish with a last push like "Now go build a massive brand. It will change your life."

### The first 120 words do the selling

- Say who it's for in the first line, naming three identities so more people see themselves. Example: "If you create content, are looking to hire a Head of Content, or want to become one, this is for you."
- Say why to trust you: "I've spent my entire career obsessing over building world-class brands", "6+ years", "thousands of dollars and hours of trial and error".
- Add the wish line: "if someone had given me this article 6 years ago, it would've been the most valuable thing I'd read". It frames the article as a shortcut.
- Add honesty markers: "This wasn't written by AI", "I only speak about things I know", "This is NOT a masterclass on paid ads".
- State the big promise in one line, with the stakes and the payoff, once.

### What the newer articles added

- A "Why listen to me?" section near the top with the writer's numbers and what he says he pioneered.
- A "For:" line under every path in a money article ("For: those starting from zero", "For: those with an existing product"), so each reader finds their part fast.
- An "Expectations" section, and "I don't have anything to sell you" near the top.
- A short option for people in a hurry: "for a condensed version, screenshot this post and watch my video at 2x speed".
- An ask up front. One copywriting article says "All I ask for in revealing this information is two things": follow if it was valuable, and repost or like so others see it.
- Copy-paste prompts in text boxes at every step of two guides about a Grok-based tool. The best newer articles are the ones with prompts.
- Writing for an AI to use. The article says "tag your @bot to digest this article", and the posts pushing it say "Send this prompt to Grok". The article is framed as something to feed to an AI, not only to read.
- One organic marketing article was only about 1,500 words ("more if you count the code blocks"), with Venn diagrams and lists. The writer tells readers to screenshot the visuals.

### The overall shape and numbers

- The first three articles ran 2,000 to 2,450 words, with 3 to 5 big sections and about 20 to 30 numbered sub-points in total.
- They are mostly lists and little prose. Paragraphs are 1 to 3 lines, and almost every point ends in a bullet list.
- They are long overall but never dense, because of the lists and boxes.
- They have no studies and no long stories. Authority comes from experience lines, examples and frameworks.
- Every few sections there is a one-line punch, like "Distribution is everything" or "Content is very coachable".

### Examples

- **"How to Grow a Massive Brand (Full 2026 Masterclass)":** About 2,000 words with a TL;DR, a promise ("Branding is one of the only moats left"), a roadmap, trust lines, big sections like Brand-Market Fit with numbered sub-points and text boxes, final tips, a recap, and a close with "Now go build a massive brand. It will change your life". The writer reports 315,000 to 327,000 views and about 5,000 bookmarks.
  <br>*Why it works:* It feels like a paid course given away free, and every abstract idea becomes a box people screenshot.

### Common mistakes

- Writing long paragraphs where a list or a box would do. Keep paragraphs to 1 to 3 lines.
- Skipping the roadmap, so the reader doesn't know the journey before starting.
- Leaving out the "for / not for" block, so readers can't tell quickly whether the article is for them.

### Try it

Take an outline you have and map it onto the 12 parts. Write only parts 1 to 6 (about 150 words). Then check: could a stranger tell in 20 seconds what it is, who it's for and why to trust it?

## 7.8 Inside a section: text boxes and teaching moves

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Turn every abstract idea into a box readers can screenshot. Inside each section, define the thing simply, number your points, name real examples and write like a person talks. The box is the part people save.

### How each section teaches

- Define the thing simply first. Examples: "What is a Head of Content?" or "Brand PMF is when a specific audience instantly understands what you stand for...". PMF means product-market fit, so brand PMF means your brand fits what a group of people wants.
- Then give numbered sub-points. Each has a bold label, 1 to 3 short lines on why it matters, then a list or an example.
- Ask questions the reader can answer about themselves, like "What do you stand for? Who is it for? Why should they care?"
- Use real names as examples, one line each: Duolingo's owl mascot, the prediction market apps Polymarket and Kalshi, the voice typing app Wispr Flow, Tesla's 2006 master plan, OpenAI Dev Days.
- Use a pros and cons box when you compare two options, like a brand run by its founder versus one run by the company.
- Say opinions with confidence: "Hire for taste and potential, train for skill" or "Your team is your ceiling".
- Add asides that sound like a person talking: "I could make this list very long but you get the point" or "For lack of a better term".
- Show skill in levels, so the reader is pushed to go further: "These 5 skills are the bare bones of a 6/10... to be an 11/10 you'll have to go deeper".
- Put "If you take away only one thing from this article, make it this:" right before your main idea.
- Point to other creators inside the article when they do something better, like "If you want to learn about ads, I recommend watching...".

### Text boxes: 5 to 7 per article

- X articles show code blocks as clean boxes in a typewriter-style font. Use them for frameworks that people will screenshot and bookmark.
- Dot leaders are a label, a row of dots, then the point, like Smart ........ "I learn something every time". One article used it for a table of emotions, and another used TOF ....... Get discovered. (TOF means top of funnel: the first step, where strangers find you.)
- Arrows show a flow: "Who are you? → What you do" (a list of story questions), or "Chapter 1 → Build an expensive sports car" (Tesla's master plan).
- Tables work for comparisons. One article compared 7 platforms by what each is best for, how much effort it takes and where it sits in the funnel. Newer articles had tables for how much the algorithm weighs each action, hooks by format, and what to ask for at each step.
- Pros and cons boxes: founder-led brand versus company-led brand.
- Mini cards per item, like Funnel → TOF / Effort → Low / Suits → Writers.
- A full grid, like 4 content topics crossed with the 3 funnel steps (top, middle, bottom), with an example post in each cell.
- Copy-paste prompts in boxes, so the reader can act straight away.

### Make it easy to take in

- People scan, they don't read. If a post looks too daunting, people scroll away. Formatting is part of the hook, because it lowers the effort of starting.
- Use rhythm: line breaks, a mix of short and long sentences, one thought per line, paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines, numbers, lists and arrows, and lines that pull people down the page like "Let me explain...".
- Write simply enough that a 10-year-old could understand. One writer says: "If a 12 y/o can't read it, I'm not publishing." The ideas can be smart. The words should be simple.

### Other ways to fill a section

- 10 numbered principles, each with an example, then a "build your own version" worksheet and an AI prompt to apply it.
- One section per idea, each with a story, a study and rules.
- Bullets that say what the reader will learn plus the benefit. Specific bullets sell better than vague ones.
- A step-by-step course format where each step ends with a question that leads into the next ("The question now is: what do I sell?") and includes prompts, examples, scripts and the math at the end.
- A list of resources where each entry says what it is, its most useful part, when to use it, and a link, with honest notes like "the guide includes sponsored tool recommendations".

### Examples

- **The emotion table:** A box with dot leaders: Smart ........ "I learn something every time", and then more rows for feelings like belonging, confident, inspired and rebellious, each with the thought in quotes.
  <br>*Why it works:* An abstract idea (decide which emotion you want people to feel) becomes a cheat sheet people screenshot.
- **Tesla's master plan as an arrow box:** Chapter 1 → Build an expensive sports car, then a cheaper car, then an affordable car, then clean energy.
  <br>*Why it works:* A real example makes "tell your story in chapters" concrete.

### Common mistakes

- Explaining an abstract idea in prose with no box, list or example.
- Vague examples instead of named brands and people.
- Walls of text. On a phone, 4 lines already look like an essay.

### Try it

Take one idea you usually explain in a paragraph and turn it into a text box three ways: dot leaders, arrows and a small table. Keep the one a stranger could understand in 5 seconds.

## 7.9 How top article authors structure the whole piece

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Top X article authors build a whole piece in a repeatable order. Here are nine real orders to copy, from an argument piece to a how-to guide. Copy the order of moves, never the content, and end each part with a push into the next.

### Structures that argue a point

- An article about the 3 things that protect you once AI is everywhere. It goes: a deadline claim, a story that starts "I learned this the hard way", the flood of AI content (with data), the three protections named, a plateau, one section per protection with a story, a study and rules, the closing window, and a close ("everyone else will ship faster and wonder why nobody cares").
- An article about turning what you know into a digital product. It goes: famous quotes first, then "why now" with 3 reasons, then 7 numbered steps, then a P.S.
- An article about telling your story. It goes: a hard truth, a personal story, someone else's story, a famous quote, sections with provocative headings, "What this all means for you", then a one-line formula as a recap.
- Headings in that article that keep people reading: "Your stories will get ignored if you don't do this" and "Why most storytelling advice is wrong (including mine)". A heading that makes a claim works better than a plain label.

### Structures that teach a how-to

- An article about going from 0 to 100 million views a month. It goes: proof, "not written by AI" and who it's for, a line of quick context "so you can decide if you want to spend time reading this", then 3 phases covering team structure, targets and hires, then final thoughts that promise follow-up articles.
- An article about 6,000 videos. It goes: big proof claims first, then a 2-step system (a content machine plus a way to spread it), then recap lists, then "how to automate all of this".
- An article of 16 rules. It goes: a proof line, a belief flipped in capital letters, 16 numbered rules, then "the BIGGEST takeaway".
- An article on engineering virality. It goes: a scene that shows the pain, 5 numbered steps, then a checklist to run before launch.
- An article on automating distribution. It goes: three rules up front, a section per channel, three product playbooks, a week-by-week plan for the first weeks, then approval rules to copy.
- An article of 7 curated guides. It goes: credibility, an honest stance ("everything felt like slop"), 7 hand-picked resources, then a loop of using what you learned to find more.
- A full course article. It goes: a pain ("multiple interests, no idea what to do"), an attack on common advice, the writer's story as proof, a simple promise, then numbered steps: a content map, a software stack, small products and services, and the first client.

### Frameworks

**Pick a shape by the job**

1. Proof-led how-to: proof, promise, numbered steps, recap
2. Argument piece: claim, story, the named ideas, one section each with a story, a study and rules, the window, close
3. Worksheet piece: numbered principles with examples, then a build-your-own section

### Examples

- **A heading that makes a claim:** "Your stories will get ignored if you don't do this"
  <br>*Why it works:* It makes a claim instead of naming a topic, so people who are skimming keep reading.

### Common mistakes

- Copying a structure's topic instead of its order of moves.
- Ending a section without a bridge to the next one. Use a question that leads into it.

### Try it

Pick one article you admire and write its order of parts as one line per part. Don't copy the content, only the order. Then use that order for your own topic.

## 7.10 Drafting, AI help and the taste you add

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Drafting is a process: get it all out messy first, then tighten it. When you use AI, let it do the production work and keep the taste for yourself. Taste means the choices about what's worth saying and how it should feel.

### A simple drafting process

- Write your thread or article unstructured first. Don't worry about how bad it is, or about word counts or character counts. Then distill it into something tighter and punchier.
- Sometimes write broad pieces about life just to clarify your own thinking or shift your own mood. Writing is the process, and sharing it is a side effect.
- Make sure you will be proud of a thread before you post it. One writer never deletes threads, unless someone rightly points out that he didn't think it through. He sometimes deletes single tweets that aren't working.
- Writing a long piece trains your short posts too. One writer says writing and outlining a book shaped every newsletter and tweet he wrote afterwards.

### Write the article worth sharing, with AI doing the production

- The split: AI writes the structure and first drafts, and the human rewrites the hook and makes the editorial calls, meaning what to keep, cut and emphasise. One creator calls it 80% AI and 20% taste.
- His example was an AI marketing project. Slide tools like Gamma, Chronicle and Beautiful.ai all gave him "the same flavor of fine": okay, but generic.
- So he spent days on research first. He asked what stories the data tells, what would make someone care about these numbers, and what storyline holds it all together.
- He studied presentation design: how dense the data should be, how tension builds and releases in conference talks, and how to guide the eye with visual hierarchy.
- Then he split the work. One AI model wrote the storyline and copy, another produced the visuals, and he directed both with references, limits and examples. His summary: the AI did the production, and he brought the taste.

### Examples

- **Why the slide tools were not used:** All the slide tools gave the same kind of okay, generic result.
  <br>*Why it works:* Generic output is the reason your research and taste are worth more than the tool.

### Common mistakes

- Letting the tool pick your hook.
- Posting a thread you would not be proud of.
- Skipping the research and expecting the tool to find the story.

### Try it

Write one section of an article in one go with no limits, then cut it to half its length. Notice what survived, because that is what you really wanted to say.

## 7.11 The newsletter and long-form as a home base

*6 min read*

> **The big idea:** A newsletter is a long-form home where you go deeper on the problems your posts bring up. Pair it with one short-form platform: posts bring people in, and the newsletter keeps them.

### Pair short with long

- Pair one short-form platform with one long-form platform, or use one place for both. Substack, a newsletter platform, is one example.
- Use the newsletter to show that you know your subject and to go deeper on the pain points your short posts mention. A short post names a problem. The newsletter can walk through the full answer.
- Put a link to a short questionnaire in the middle and at the end of every newsletter, asking readers what they need help with. The answers tell you what to write next, and readers get a simple way to reply.

### Practice and reuse

- One creator says that after about 100 newsletters he was proud of, his list grew to over 182,000 people. That is his own claim and nobody has checked it, but the lesson is that results come from many repeats.
- Turn long content into other long content. Transcribe a winning YouTube video into an email, or pull the audio out of it as a podcast.
- Treat written content as its own workflow: tweets, LinkedIn posts, emails and carousels all come from the same ideas. Don't treat the newsletter as something you do with leftovers.

### Examples

- **Many repeats:** One creator says about 100 newsletters he was proud of led to 182,000 readers.
  <br>*Why it works:* This is one creator's own claim. The point is that a newsletter grows with repeated work, not that you will get the same number.

### Common mistakes

- Treating the newsletter as a leftover.
- Writing without a way for readers to reply or tell you what they need, like a questionnaire link.

### Try it

Pick one newsletter you write or plan to write. Decide where a questionnaire link will go: once in the middle and once at the end.

## 7.12 Pacing and structure borrowed from long-form video advice

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Most of the advice in this guide about long videos was not written for articles. But many of the ideas carry over, and this lesson lists the ones that do. Treat them as video advice applied to writing.

### What video creators say applies to writing too

- Uneven pacing works for "videos or copy or emails" and LinkedIn posts: shorter points early, longer ones later. The reader feels progress.
- A hook is also the first few words of an email or a tweet, not only of a video.
- Some hook rules are said to work in any type of content, in conversation and in life. Beyond that, the video creators give no advice made specially for articles.

### What carries over (our application, not something the video creators said)

- Open with proof, promise and plan: what this is, why they should listen to you, and how you will get them there. Then give a numbered plan.
- Say up front how many parts there are, so readers know what's coming.
- In each section, make a point, explain it, illustrate it with an example, state the lesson, then bridge to the next section.
- Start broad and get denser. Open with the broad problem that everyone has, and save the dense, tactical part for the core readers near the end.
- Cut every story, section or paragraph that drifts from the title's promise.
- Too slow is the problem, not too long. A long piece is fine if every part earns its place. Information density matters more than going viral.
- Long form builds the relationship faster than short pieces, and one great piece gets found for years.
- Ask for the like, follow or bookmark about 30% of the way in, not only at the end, and remind people once at the end.

### Frameworks

**The section loop**

1. Point: state the idea
2. Explain: say why it works
3. Illustrate: give one example
4. Lesson: say what to do, then bridge to the next section

### Examples

- **Uneven pacing:** Say the first sections are 3 short paragraphs each and the later ones are twice as long.
  <br>*Why it works:* This is a made-up illustration. Readers feel progress because early sections are quick to finish.

### Common mistakes

- Treating this carry-over as something a video creator said about articles.
- Padding a piece with parts that drift away from the title.

### Try it

Take an outline and cut every section that doesn't serve the promise in your title.

## 7.13 Pushing an article after it is published

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** One creator treats every article as a campaign: a video version, quote posts that ask for bookmarks, and posts built around prompts. The order matters, and the same sequence was repeated for every article.

### The push system

- Every article gets pushed. First, the creator quotes his own article with a hook that asks for a bookmark. He does this 2 to 3 times per article.
- Then he makes a video version of the article with chapters, and posts it a few days later as a quote of the article. He did this for all 5 of his article videos.
- Here is one push around his algorithm article, over 4 days: a video with chapters (393,000 views), then a quote of the video saying "Someone reading this is going to blow up" (423,000 views, 8,158 bookmarks), then the article (461,000 views), then a quote of the article using the prompt angle (156,000 views), then a meme quote of the article (340,000 views). These are his reported numbers.
- Here is another, for his copywriting article: the article, then a quote with a prompt the same day (30,000 views), then the video with chapters the next day.
- Some push posts framed the article as a prompt. "Send this prompt to Grok" and "The smart ones will inject this prompt into @bot" turn the article into something people feed to an AI.
- One article was about 1,500 words, with Venn diagrams and lists, and the creator told viewers to screenshot the visuals.

### Closers

- Ending with a line like "I plan to write 1-2 articles/week" gives readers a reason to follow you for the next one.

### A pattern in the view counts

- This is our reading of the numbers, not something the creator said. His three biggest early articles (179,000 to 327,000 views) were each on a new topic: the Head of Content job, branding and distribution.
- His roadmap article got 42,000 views because it repeated the topic of his first article, and his organic marketing guide got 26,000 because it overlapped with two earlier pieces. Repeated topics got 5 to 10 times fewer views.
- His algorithm article beat all 7 earlier ones. It was a fresh topic tied to the news, and the video had warmed people up two days before. All of these are the creator's reported figures, not checked.

### Frameworks

**The article push**

1. Quote your own article with a bookmark-ask hook, 2 to 3 times
2. Post a video version with chapters a few days later, as a quote of the article
3. Add a quote built around a prompt, and a meme quote

### Examples

- **The push around the algorithm article:** Over 4 days: a video (393,000 views), a quote of the video (423,000), the article (461,000), a quote of the article with the prompt angle (156,000), and a meme quote of the article (340,000).
  <br>*Why it works:* Figures as the creator reported them. The point is that one article got five separate pushes.

### Common mistakes

- Publishing and walking away.
- Repeating a topic you already covered.

### Try it

Plan three pushes for one article: a quote that asks for a bookmark, a quote built around a prompt, and a video version.

## Cheat sheet: Articles and long-form writing

The whole module on one page.

- Turn your best short ideas into longer pieces.
- Keep one short-form platform and one long-form platform.
- Pick a big, fresh topic. Repeated topics get far fewer views.
- Only write about what you have actually done.
- Make the promise extreme, specific and timely.
- Write the hook last, with 5 to 10 versions.
- A good title promises a big outcome and is instantly clear.
- If the article pays off the promise, nobody minds a strong hook.
- Show proof before you start teaching.
- Say what the article is not, so readers believe the rest.
- Show that you understand the reader with real details, never just say it.
- End the opening with a line that pulls the reader into part 1.
- Name who the article is for in the first line.
- Add a TL;DR, a roadmap, and a short summary at the end of each section.
- Use text boxes people can screenshot.
- Use short lines and simple words.
- Write a messy first draft, then tighten it.
- Let AI do the production work, and bring the taste yourself.
- Quote your own article 2 to 3 times, asking readers to bookmark it.
- Close by asking for a like, a repost and a follow, and say when the next one comes.
