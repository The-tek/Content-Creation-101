# Module 4: Psychology and attention

People scroll without thinking, so learn what makes them stop, what makes them care and what makes them act

This module explains the psychology of attention. You will learn why the brain ignores what it knows, how curiosity and surprise make people stop and stay, and what the real studies behind popular claims say. Then it covers emotion, trust, identity, influence and persuasion, and ends with one creator's reading of how X picks which posts to show. Creators' claims are labelled as claims, and real studies keep their limits

## Lesson 4.1: Why people stop scrolling, and why they stay

### Big idea

People scroll without thinking and decide what to look at in a split second. Your first job is to make them stop. Your second job is to make your post so easy to understand that they stay

### How it works

#### People scroll without thinking

Swiping becomes a habit of the thumb. People keep flicking until something makes them think "wait, what was that?" Sometimes they swipe past, then swipe back, which is a strong sign the post caught them

Your hook should cause that tiny freeze, like seeing a wolf in your backyard. The body does react automatically to something new, but using that idea to think about hooks is only a helpful way to look at it, not a proven rule

While you scroll, your brain ignores what it already knows and only stops for something new

#### What a hook has to answer

To make someone freeze, your first line has to answer 2 questions fast:

1. What is this about? This is the subject
2. What do I get if I keep reading or watching? This is the stakes, what the person gains or loses

Then keep it short and make it clear. Eight words are better than 28, and there should be only one way to read it

#### How fast people decide

People decide in a split second, much faster than most creators think. One creator got to this by watching people scroll and running small tests, so treat it as a rough guide and not a proven result

The brain first takes a very fast, automatic look, about a fifth of a second. It picks up bright colours and movement before you understand anything

That is why the picture is the most important piece of your hook. People also read faster than they listen, so a line of text on screen works well

#### Catch attention, keep it, give value

Everything you publish has to do 3 things: catch attention, keep it, and give something useful back. Think of it as a trade. You give value, and people give you their attention, likes and views

People scan, they don't read word by word. If a post looks heavy, they scroll on. So make it look easy, with short lines, simple words and a clear image

Before you post, imagine you are the reader. Will I read this? Will I keep reading? Or will I leave?

#### Being understood is the hidden hook

You can't hook someone who doesn't understand you. If the picture says one thing and the words say another, people feel lost and leave

Curiosity only works when people follow the facts. They can't guess the answer to something they can't follow

You can't control what people do with your advice. You can control how clear it is, so put your effort there

### Steps

The 4 S's of a hook that stops people:

1. Subject: it is clear who or what the hook is about
2. Stakes: it gives a reason to care, like the result they want (views, clients, money, time)
3. Speed: subject and stakes in as few words as possible
4. Super clear: only one way to read it, no wordplay

### Examples

A hook with a hidden clue:

Say a video opens on a lean person with the text "how does she stay lean eating carbs?" The drink in her hand is blurred, so nobody can tell what she is drinking from that frame alone

Why it works: The question stops the scroll, and the hidden clue gives people a reason to keep watching. If they could work it out from the first frame, they would leave

### Mistakes

- thinking people look at your thumbnail or first line for seconds, when it is a split second
- polishing the words while the picture says something different
- posts that look too packed with text to start reading
- clever wording that can be read two ways

### Try it

Pick 5 of your own posts or videos. Look at the first line or frame for one second only, then look away. Write down what it was about and why you would keep going. If you can't answer both, rewrite the opening so the subject and the stakes come first

## Lesson 4.2: The attention span myth

### Big idea

You have probably heard that people now focus for only 8 seconds, less than a goldfish. No research backs that up. People can still focus. They just have endless choices and no patience for boring content

### How it works

#### Where the goldfish stat came from

It appeared in a 2015 report from Microsoft Canada, which named a website called Statistic Brain as its source. A BBC journalist later traced it and found no study behind it

A 2022 survey from King's College London found that half of UK adults still believe it

#### What actually changed

People don't have short attention spans. They have little patience for boring things, and that is why the hook decides so much

Most people on social media aren't trying to learn anything. If they were, they would search for a video, course or article

They are just browsing, so you have to earn their attention before you can teach

#### Long content still works when it isn't slow

People leave when content moves slowly, and length alone doesn't make them leave. Pack as much value as you can into the time you use, whether that is a 90-second video or a 6-hour course

The brain can only hold a small amount at once, so fewer words help. There is less to carry

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say attention has sped up because we all got used to fast content. Others say attention itself hasn't changed and there is just far more competition for it

Both lead to the same advice: get to the value fast

### Mistakes

- repeating the 8-second goldfish stat as if it were a fact
- blaming your audience's attention span when your opening is slow
- thinking short content always wins

### Try it

Find one claim about attention or psychology that creators keep repeating. Spend 10 minutes looking for the original source

Then write one line: is it a real study, a single study, or no study at all?

## Lesson 4.3: Dopamine: why not knowing the ending keeps people watching

### Big idea

Dopamine is a chemical in your brain that is about looking forward to a reward, not just enjoying it. Not knowing what comes next is what makes content hard to put down

### How it works

#### Dopamine is about looking forward

Most people think dopamine is the feel-good chemical you get when you win. A better way to see it is that it fires when you expect a reward, not when you get it

Think of blackjack. The excitement comes while you decide whether to take another card and wonder what the dealer is hiding. The win itself feels smaller

That is why nobody feels happiest the second after reaching a goal. The looking forward was the high

#### Why surprise keeps people watching

Compare a vending machine with a slot machine. You do the same thing with both, but a vending machine gives the same snack every time. A slot machine might give nothing or a jackpot

Only the slot machine hooks people, and the only difference is that you can't know the result. Content you can fully predict is a vending machine

In content, a big open question gives a steady, gentle pull. A twist that breaks what people expected gives the biggest jolt

#### What the real science says

A 1997 study recorded dopamine cells in monkeys. Once a reward became predictable, the cells fired when the signal for the reward appeared, not when the reward arrived. So the idea that looking forward drives attention has a real base

Be careful with the wording. That research was on monkey brain cells and was about learning. Don't say "dopamine makes people addicted to your video", because nobody has shown that

#### The 4 steps of the addiction loop

1. Stakes: give people someone to root for, something at risk and a deadline. Say "I had 48 hours to fix the ad budget or lose it". Nobody has to die, it just has to feel important to your viewer
2. Big question: give just enough detail that people can guess the answer. "Something crazy happened" is too vague to guess
3. Twist: break what they predicted, but make sure it makes sense when they look back. Random is confusing. Surprising and logical is what works
4. Re-hook: close one question and open the next in the same breath, with lines like "which would have been great, except..." or "and that is when I realized"

### Examples

The prince and the dragon:

Told flat: a prince goes to a tower, fights a dragon and saves the princess. Told with a twist: the dragon was not guarding the tower, it was chained to it, and it pulled back and looked scared

Why it works: The twist is surprising and makes sense when you look back, so it gives a jolt without confusing anyone

### Mistakes

- saying "dopamine makes people addicted to your video"
- questions too vague to guess, like "something crazy happened"
- random twists that make no sense afterwards

### Try it

Take a story you plan to tell. Write down the question the viewer will have, what they will probably guess, and one twist that is surprising but makes sense when they look back. Then write one line that opens the next question

## Lesson 4.4: The 6 steps from first glance to loyal viewer

### Big idea

A viewer goes through 6 steps, from a split-second pause to seeing you as someone who reliably helps them. The first 4 steps are about your message. The last 2 are about you as a person

### How it works

#### Step 1: stimulation

This is a jolt for the eyes in the first 1 to 2 seconds. Colour, motion, brightness and contrast that look different from what the viewer usually sees make them pause for a split second

It is the weakest and shortest reward, but without it nothing else gets a chance. If everyone copies the same look, viewers stop reacting to it, so build your own colours, motion and style

#### Steps 2 to 4: the message

1. Captivation: a question pops into the viewer's head. A bigger, less obvious question pulls harder, but only if the viewer cares about it. A question about aliens won't hook someone who doesn't care about aliens
2. Anticipation: the viewer starts guessing the answer. Give facts that bring them closer, then send them in a new direction, or answer and immediately raise a second question. They can only guess if they understand the facts
3. Validation: you close the question with an answer they didn't expect. In entertainment, finish the story instead of ending on a cliffhanger. In teaching, actually give the tip, with real value

#### Steps 5 and 6: you as the messenger

1. Affection: the viewer starts to like and trust you, and gives you more patience than a stranger would get. Likability carries from video to video, as long as people recognise you

What builds it: how you look, your overall vibe, smiling, and most of all actually solving their problem

1. Revelation: the viewer realises you will keep giving them value. Now just seeing your name or face makes them want to watch

Content with no face can reach steps 1 to 4 easily, but steps 5 and 6 are hard because there is no person to like

#### How to use the steps

Use the 6 steps as a checklist. For each piece, ask: am I creating the right curiosity?

Does each step lead to the next?

Teaching reaches step 6 far more easily than entertainment, because entertainment has to compete with Netflix and sports

### Steps

The 6 steps:

1. Stimulation: colour, motion and contrast in the first 1 to 2 seconds
2. Captivation: a question the viewer cares about forms in their head
3. Anticipation: they guess, you bring them close, then twist or open a new question
4. Validation: you close the question with an answer they didn't expect
5. Affection: they like you
6. Revelation: they see you as a reliable source of value and want to watch you by default

### Examples

Failing at step 2:

Say a video has an attractive person and a moving red and orange graphic, so people pause (step 1 works). But the line "here's how you do your makeup for the holidays" raises a question the viewer doesn't care about, so they leave

Why it works: Winning the first glance isn't enough. The question has to be one the viewer cares about

### Mistakes

- winning the first glance, then raising a question the viewer doesn't care about
- copying the same look as everyone else until people stop reacting to it
- ending on an open question with no answer

### Try it

Pick one educational post or video that hooked you. Write one line per step: what caught your eye, what question you had, what you guessed, how it closed, and what made you like the person

Then do the same for one of your own pieces and mark the weakest step

## Lesson 4.5: Curiosity: the gap and the open loop

### Big idea

Curiosity is the feeling that one piece of the picture is missing. People need to know a little first, and they stay to the end only while the question is still open

### How it works

#### The gap in what people know

A 1994 study found that curiosity needs some knowledge first. If the gap is too big (people know nothing) or too small (the answer is obvious), it doesn't pull

That is why "this one detail about X" works better than "a mystery". People know enough to feel the missing piece

A 2014 study found that when people were very curious about a question, they remembered the answer better

#### Keep the loop open

An open loop is a question you have raised and not yet answered. Questions make people stay to the end to get the answer, so keep it open as long as you can, and when you close one, open a new one

Giving the answer too early kills the pull. Say a short video about "the secret to success" reveals the secret at second 5

Everyone leaves

If the viewer can solve the puzzle from the first frame, there is no reason to keep watching. Hide the clue or blur it

#### Road maps and promises

Early on, show how many steps you will take them through, and blur the later ones so they wonder what is coming

Treat the promise of your title as a fixed line. If you promise "how to make money in 2026" and add an unrelated story, cut it, because it confuses people

Repeat the promise every 30 seconds to a minute, so nobody wonders why they clicked. When you explain, keep both the pictures and the story moving

### Steps

The curiosity loop:

1. Question: plant a specific question they care about
2. Context: give enough facts that they can guess
3. New question: as you answer, open the next one

### Mistakes

- a gap that is too big or too small to pull anyone
- giving the answer too early, or showing it in the first frame
- drifting away from the promise in your title

### Try it

Rewrite one hook two ways: once as a vague mystery with no context, and once as "this one detail about X" with enough context to guess. Then note where in your piece you close the question and where you open the next one

## Lesson 4.6: Newness: why the brain stops for new things

### Big idea

The brain tunes out what it already knows and stops on what is new. But something only looks new next to something old, and too much that is unfamiliar makes people feel lost. So wrap the new idea inside something they already know

### How it works

#### The brain filters out the familiar

While you scroll, the brain ignores anything familiar and only stops for something new. But it can't tell something is new without comparing it to something old

When the brain suspects it might be wrong about something, it freezes for a moment to adjust. That small gap creates curiosity

The more recent something feels, the more attention it gets. That is why "just happened" and "this week" work

#### The purple cow

When you drive, you don't notice most of the cows and cars. But you would spot a purple cow from far away

Your content needs something unique to stand out, and specific details and numbers help

An unusual person can be the new thing too, like a 17-year-old who has figured out something hard in a field full of older men. A fresh point of view, or a big idea explained in a way people haven't heard, gives a small thrill

#### Wrap the new in the known

Hearing something familiar makes people feel comfortable. Too much that is unfamiliar makes them feel stupid, and they leave

So wrap the unknown in something known, such as a familiar face, brand or everyday object

You can also start from something the viewer already half believes. A line like "lists of viral hooks aren't the answer" stops people who have privately thought the same

Theory that people can't picture does worse than examples, stories and comparisons

#### The trap of knowing too much

Once you know something, you assume everyone else does too. This is called the curse of knowledge

Experts keep getting more specific to show something new, and forget that the simple, basic version of their idea could reach far more people

Good content is often saying old things to new people. Most people need reminding more than they need teaching

### Mistakes

- content so familiar that the brain filters it out
- content so unfamiliar that viewers feel stupid and leave
- getting so specific that you forget what beginners don't know

### Try it

Take one idea you think is obvious in your field. Write it for a complete beginner, wrapped in something they already know, like an everyday object or a common belief

Then add one specific number or detail that makes it a purple cow

## Lesson 4.7: Making old ideas feel new

### Big idea

Almost everything has already been said. What works is to take an old idea that has been proven true and make it feel new with a fresh angle, a name, a definition or better packaging

### How it works

#### 4 questions to test an idea

Before you use an idea, check it against 4 questions:

1. Is it new to them, even if only in how you word it?
2. Is it non-obvious, meaning not what they would guess?
3. Is it detailed, more than the generic version?
4. Is it visible, something they can picture through an example, story or comparison?

Obvious ideas get scrolled past

#### Give your ideas a name

Join familiar words into a term people haven't heard, then explain it. They get curious and ask "what is that?" Say a YouTuber wants to teach delegating. Instead of "here's how to delegate", he calls it the buyback principle: you buy back your time

The name is only a label on an idea that already existed, but it makes old advice sound new, and people can repeat it to others

Define the key words in your content too, for example "brand is repeated association". People then see you as an expert on the topic

#### 5 moves that make an old idea feel new

1. New reveal plus result: take an old topic, add a fresh angle, and tie it to what they want
2. Contrast with what they believe: "you've been told 8 glasses a day your whole life..." It has to be a true opposite
3. Urgency, only if it is real. Fake urgency destroys trust
4. Proof close to the viewer: someone exactly like them who got the result works best. A study or statistic works too, but people wonder "was it people like me?"
5. Don't weaken your own point. Don't say "of course, this is basic and well known". Deliver it like a shared secret

#### Packaging counts more than secrets

Many viral pieces are common knowledge. What made them work was the formatting and how they were presented to a specific audience

The easiest way to grow is to take basic truths, the ones that work in any situation, and package them in a new way

### Examples

Naming an old idea:

"Here's how to delegate" becomes the buyback principle: you buy back your time

Why it works: Old advice with a name sounds new and spreads by word of mouth

### Mistakes

- generic messages in a market you call crowded
- fake urgency
- weakening your own point with "of course, this is well known"
- naming things you don't actually teach

### Try it

List 3 things you teach that feel obvious to you. For each one, write a name of 2 to 4 words made of familiar words, a one-line definition, and one contrast with what people already believe

## Lesson 4.8: Emotion: what drives clicks, shares and action

### Big idea

Different emotions do different jobs. Studies show negative and sad words get more clicks, while strong emotions like awe, anger and anxiety get more shares. Pick the emotion for the job

### How it works

#### Clicks versus shares

Clicks: a 2023 study of about 105,000 headline versions found that each extra negative word raised the click rate by 2.3% for an average headline. Sadness pulled the most clicks

Shares: a 2012 study of about 7,000 New York Times articles found that people shared most what made them feel awe, anger or anxiety. Sadness, a low-energy emotion, got shared less

So a sad hook can win the click, but awe or a bit of anger wins the share. Decide whether the piece is for views or for spreading

#### Emotion in your words

Use words that trigger emotion, the way you would talk to a friend. "Quit your bad habits" hits harder than "develop better habits"

Emotion spreads from person to person. Raw thoughts and visible feelings make a story easy to relate to

#### Hopes, dreams, fears and blockers

Behind every title, and every buyer, are 4 things:

- hopes: what they want in the short term, about 90 days
- dreams: long-term wants, 5 to 10 years away
- fears: what scares them
- blockers: beliefs that stop them from acting

Decide which one a piece should hit. On the topic of making money, a hope angle is "how to make \$1M in 2026" and a fear angle is "you'll never be rich until you understand this". Learn what your own audience responds to before you choose

#### Pain, and going one step away from it

Talk about real pain. The 4 core pains are money, time, health and access (being able to get what you want or need)

Don't hit the pain head on, because it can sound like a sales pitch. Go one step away. Videos about "making money" may flop while videos about "growing faster" do well, because viewers make the jump to money themselves

Talk to one person, and show the gap between where they are and where they want to be. "If you struggle with acne, try these 3 things" beats "3 trends in skincare" because people feel seen right away

### Where creators disagree

Studies say negative words get more clicks, yet positive content spread more overall. Negative content can also collect reports and "not interested" taps. So negative words may win a click, but positive, high-energy emotion tends to win the spread

### Mistakes

- using sadness when you want shares
- hitting the core pain so directly that it sounds like a sales pitch
- business jargon with no emotion, like "yield a positive ROI"

### Try it

Take one topic and write 4 hooks: one for a hope, one for a dream, one for a fear and one for a blocker. Then mark which emotion each would trigger and whether it is built for clicks or for shares

## Lesson 4.9: Association, familiarity and liking

### Big idea

People like and trust what they have seen many times, and they link a name to a topic without thinking. Repeating yourself, borrowing links to known names and a recognisable look all do this work for you

### How it works

#### Why association works

When you hear a name and a topic pops into your head without thinking, that is an association. It only forms when you see or hear the same thing many times

You can borrow association. When you team up with someone, you take on what people already think about them

A sports brand teaming up with a famous athlete borrows his "greatness"

Borrowing works in the bad direction too. If you team up with people with a sketchy reputation, you attract a certain kind of audience, and probably not one you want

#### Being familiar is a tool

After buying a certain car, you start seeing it everywhere. The brain looks for patterns it already knows, so people notice what is familiar

That is why a recognisable look works. If a creator wears the same blue shirt in every video, it becomes a visual hook in a row of thumbnails, and people who got value once click again

Repeat a filming style, a way of speaking, even a hairstyle. One test: you don't have a personal brand until somebody can dress as you at Halloween

#### What science says about seeing something often

A 1968 study found the mere exposure effect: the more often students saw a face, the more they liked it. A large review of 208 experiments supports it, though some later attempts to repeat it failed

This is the science behind posting regularly with the same face or format. Trust and liking grow when people know you

### Examples

Borrowed association:

A sports brand teams up with a famous athlete and takes on the "greatness" people already link to him

Why it works: The same transfer works the other way, so who you team up with also decides who your audience is

### Mistakes

- changing your look, style and voice with every piece, so nothing can become familiar
- teaming up with people whose reputation you wouldn't want
- thinking one view is enough, when the link in people's minds needs repetition

### Try it

Write the one-line topic you want people to link to your name. Then list 3 things you could repeat in every piece, like a look, a phrase or a format, so that link forms through repetition

## Lesson 4.10: Trust, weakness and proof

### Big idea

People are more doubtful than ever. Trust comes from real weaknesses, proof that fits the viewer, keeping your promises, and work that clearly has a person behind it

### How it works

#### Trust when people doubt everything

People have learned to distrust anything that feels like a sales pitch. We trust and root for people with real weaknesses, and nobody trusts someone who acts perfect

Fake urgency destroys trust. A lower voice, like you are sharing a secret, creates curiosity instead

Trust means delivering on your promise. If you promise the best tactics, give them, with lots of useful detail. People can also feel when you chase trends or force a topic, so show what really excites you

#### A mistake can make you more likable, but not always

In a 1966 study, a quiz contestant who answered 92% of the questions right and then spilled coffee was rated the most likable of 4 versions

The same spill made an average contestant (30% right) less likable. So a mistake helps only if people already see you are good

One limit: the study used 48 male students, in 1966

#### Winning trust in the first 30 seconds

People decide whether to trust you within 10 to 30 seconds, so show proof fast. From strongest to weakest:

1. A result for someone like them
2. Your own result
3. A big following or someone famous backing you
4. "I was where you are"
5. Something you have in common

Being similar works. In a 2004 study, a shared birthday raised the share of people saying yes from 34% to 62%

#### Believable and real

Aim for something that is only just believable. It is fine to exaggerate something real, but if it looks too fake, people stop trusting it

Clickbait isn't that effective. A hook that lies loses viewers right after

A suggestion gets past people's guard, while a direct command makes them defend themselves. Conclusions people reach on their own create trust faster

#### People reject work with nobody behind it

People tend to reject work that feels like nobody was behind it, even when it is better. They slow down for craft and scroll past defaults

AI-made defaults are correct and well structured but generic, like a landing page with the same gradient and a headline about revolutionising your workflow

To build trust, show yourself doing the thing you do best, for example behind-the-scenes content that shows how your work is made. In a famous story, people feared lifts until the inventor cut the cable in front of a crowd and the safety brake held

### Examples

The lift:

The inventor cut the lift cable in front of a crowd, and the safety brake stopped it

Why it works: Showing it working is stronger than saying it works

### Mistakes

- acting perfect
- admitting flaws before you have proved you are good
- a pushy sales tone and fake urgency
- hooks that lie, so people leave right after

### Try it

Take one claim you make in your content. Write down which proof from the list you could show for it, and one real weakness or mistake you could mention only after the proof

## Lesson 4.11: Identity, sharing, common enemies and disagreement

### Big idea

People share to show who they are, bond over what they are against, and follow people who pick a side. So make content people want to be seen with, and be ready for the pushback that comes with taking a stand

### How it works

#### Why people repost

People repost things they want their friends and followers to see them agree with. Nobody shares something they would be embarrassed to be linked to

A share says "this is me too". The best things to share make the sharer look smart, interesting, successful or self-aware

Before posting, ask 4 questions:

1. Will people share this?
2. Why would they?
3. Do I stand behind what I am saying?
4. Is it bold enough to start a discussion?

#### Common enemies and shared interests

People tend to bond more over something they are all against than over something they all love. A named enemy makes people sort themselves in or out

Say your enemy is the idea that you need fancy cameras, lights and gear. Everyone who has been told that rallies behind you

Pick one idea to stand for and line everything up behind it. The enemy should be a belief or a behaviour, and never a person

#### Confidence and disagreement

Nobody wants to follow someone who sounds unsure or won't pick a side. Stand firm, but be willing to change your mind. You will get hate like everyone else does

Giving people reasons to strongly disagree also gives others reasons to strongly agree. If everyone agrees with you, you aren't really saying anything

A target to aim for is about 95% positive and 5% negative comments. At 50/50, soften the opinion or choose another one

#### Living with comments and fear

Don't give much weight to positive comments. If praise makes you feel great, criticism will hurt you more

Name your own weak points first, so nobody has anything left to say about them

We notice bad things more than good things, so one negative reply can outweigh a hundred positive ones. After a flop, remember that nobody recalls it 30 seconds after scrolling past

### Examples

A common enemy:

Say a carousel (a post with several swipeable images) is about bad bosses. People start listing everything wrong with their own boss in the comments

Why it works: A common enemy gives people something to rally against, and the comments and shares follow

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say to be bold enough to start a discussion. Others say to avoid negativity so you don't collect reports. They agree on a limit: take a real stand on one idea, and don't attack people in a way that makes them block or report you

### Mistakes

- making content nobody wants to be seen sharing
- refusing to pick a side, so nobody can agree or disagree
- going for 50/50 controversy instead of standing for one idea
- fearing that one flop will be remembered

### Try it

Write the one enemy your content stands against, a belief or behaviour and never a person. Then draft a post and ask the 4 share questions. Note who would proudly share it and what sharing it says about them

## Lesson 4.12: Influence: the 7 R's, and persuading people who aren't looking

### Big idea

Views are not influence. Influence is what moves people to act. On social media you are persuading people who weren't looking for you, so describe ideas in a broad way and make them desirable

### How it works

#### What influence is

Influence is the power that makes someone do something. Some creators have lots of followers but can't get anyone to buy or care. That gap is influence

The 7 R's describe what builds it

#### The 7 R's of influence

1. Reputation: good or bad, it decides whether you can influence people at all. Keep doing what you said you would do
2. Relationship: the closer it is, the more people listen. Reply to comments and DMs
3. Resources: people listen to people who have what they want, like time, money or an audience. Show it
4. Reach: 100 times 0 is still 0. Influence needs a big audience, so pick good topics and post regularly
5. Resemblance: people follow people like them, in looks and interests
6. Reasonability: the size of your ask has to fit the trust you have built. "Like this video" is fine, "give me your credit card number" is too much
7. Reciprocity: the more you give, the more people give back

#### Persuading people who aren't looking

Don't open with instructions like how to pick topics or write a bio. That is boring and won't reach new people

Someone ready to buy already knows a lot, so inside a product you can skip the broad intro. On social media nobody is searching for your product, they are just browsing

So use social media to catch attention, and keep the full value for your product. Be persuasive everywhere: DMs, replies, quote posts, emails and videos

#### Growing bigger gets easier

The first followers are the hardest. People see 100 followers and assume it was a one-off post. They see 100,000 and follow without question, even after a weak post

So a big reach multiplies your influence, and posting regularly on good topics builds reach

### Examples

Resemblance:

A husband and wife teach the same thing, yet her audience is much more female

Why it works: Same message, a different kind of person sharing it, a different audience

### Where creators disagree

Some creators say long videos build relationships faster than short clips. Others say social media is for catching attention and your product is where the value lives. Both can be true: short pieces catch people, deeper pieces build the relationship

### Mistakes

- chasing views without influence, with people who follow but never act
- posting and never replying to comments or DMs
- asking for too much too early
- opening with how-to instructions for people who aren't searching for them

### Try it

Score yourself from 1 to 5 on each of the 7 R's. Pick the lowest and write one action for this week. Then rewrite one how-to opener as a broad, desirable idea for someone who isn't looking for it

## Lesson 4.13: Persuasion shortcuts, certainty and the pull of a plan

### Big idea

When people scroll fast, or decide whether to buy, they use mental shortcuts instead of logic. Experts backing you, proof from other people, a number to compare against, concrete words and a clear plan all help people say yes

### How it works

#### Shortcuts instead of logic

When people ask themselves "is this good?", they use shortcuts instead of working it out. Here are 4 that creators use. The figures come from studies and from creators' own claims, so treat them as rough

- authority: when someone else calls you the expert, people believe it. In one study, a receptionist added "Peter, our head of sales, has 20 years of experience", and appointments rose 20%
- social proof: a number about other people works better than a general appeal. In a 2008 hotel study, "75% of guests reuse towels" beat "save the environment"
- scarcity, said in a specific way: "4 slots, 2 are gone" works better than "limited spots"
- priming, a small hint that changes what people think about: a mattress site with clouds in the background made people care more about comfort, and pennies made them care more about price

#### Numbers and language

A number needs something to compare it with. Follow the number rule:

1. Say the big number first
2. Say "compared to what": fixing the roof costs 4k, and not fixing it costs 40k
3. Say the price, then stop. Don't add a list of reasons after it

Cut soft words like "kind of", "I think", "very" and "really". Be concrete too: "there by Friday" works better than "fast delivery"

#### Certainty sells

Say a \$1,000 mystery trip could be terrible, while a \$10,000 fully planned trip to your favourite country sounds great. The second sells more, because it is specific and removes the fear of the unknown

A promise should give people certainty about what they are buying. A question like "what are you struggling with most?", with your offer's pain points as the options, makes people think "that's me"

#### A plan, attention and action

Show a plan that is different from what they have heard, and give it structure, like "6 steps" or "5 tips". Structure makes people feel smart

Hold attention by starting with your most valuable point, teasing that the best one is at the end, and re-hooking with lines like "that was good, but if you don't fix THIS..."

Then make the action easy. Close the loop, give them more than they expected, and recap what you covered

### Examples

The hotel towel study:

In 2008, "75% of guests reuse towels" got more people to reuse towels than "save the environment"

Why it works: A number about people like them works better than a general appeal

### Mistakes

- vague scarcity like "limited spots"
- listing reasons after saying the price
- soft filler words that weaken your claim
- using unchecked percentages as fact

### Try it

Take one offer or claim and rewrite it with a specific number, a "compared to what" number, one concrete detail and one line from someone else calling you an expert

## Lesson 4.14: Faces, constant change, and what people remember

### Big idea

People look at faces and eyes, stay while something keeps changing, and remember the best moment and the ending. Show progress, tell a story, and keep things moving

### How it works

#### Faces, eyes and change

A face with a clear emotion, like an excited open-mouth smile, gives feeling instantly: you are excited, so the viewer is excited. Eyes are the first thing we look at on a face, so brightening them in a thumbnail helps

Something should keep changing on screen, which makes it hard to get bored or distracted. But lots of cuts and fast talking are not enough

There has to be something truly new to look at

#### The best moment and the ending

A 1993 study had people put a hand in painfully cold water. They chose to repeat a 90-second trial over a 60-second one, because the longer trial ended slightly warmer

A 1996 study of patients who went through a medical exam found they judged their pain by the worst moment and the last moment, not by how long it lasted

So every video and post needs one high point and a strong last line

#### Progress keeps people going

A 2006 study on car-wash loyalty cards found people finish what they have already started. Card A needed 8 stamps

Card B needed 10 stamps but came with 2 already stamped, so it was the same 8 purchases

34% finished card B, against 19% for card A. So show progress, and give the viewer a head start when you can

#### What stories do to the brain

In a small 2010 brain-scan study, listeners' brain activity matched the speaker's while they heard a story, and the better they guessed ahead, the better they understood. It was a small group, so treat it as a hint and not as proof

Stories persuade better than lists of claims. Tell your advice through your own story

### Examples

Car-wash cards:

One card needed 8 stamps. The other needed 10 but started with 2 already stamped

34% finished the second card, against 19% for the first

Why it works: It is the same work, but a head start made people finish

### Mistakes

- ending flat after a good middle
- mixing up fast cuts with something truly new to look at
- treating small studies as proven facts

### Try it

Take one piece and mark its high point and its last line. Then write one head start you could give the viewer, like a first step already done

## Lesson 4.15: How X picks what to show: one creator's reading of the algorithm

### Big idea

One creator reads the X algorithm as a system that guesses what each viewer will do and gives each post a score. The main idea is that shares and replies count far more than likes, and negative reactions cost you a lot. This is that creator's reading, and we haven't checked it

### How it works

#### How a post gets picked

In this reading, a model guesses how likely each viewer is to take each action on a post, and each action has a weight (how much it adds to the score)

First, several systems pull in posts a person might like: from people they follow, from people they don't follow yet, and from what is popular in their niche

Then a model scores every post for every viewer. The top posts go through one last filter and land on the For You page, the main feed X picks for each person

#### The weights

One list of weights from late October says, roughly:

- copy link is worth the most, about 20 likes
- reply, quote and share by DM are worth about 5 likes each
- a follow is worth about 4 likes, a share about 2, and a repost about 1
- reports, mutes, blocks and "not interested" are very negative. One claim says a report equals 350 likes

The takeaway is simple: shares and replies count the most, likes add very little, and negative reactions cost you a lot

#### Reputation and myths

Your account has a reputation. Too many blocks and reports compared to likes can get an account labelled, and the quality of your followers counts, not just the size

The creator says these are myths: that profile clicks are a huge signal, that likes are the biggest signal, that big accounts get guaranteed reach, and that small accounts can't go viral

The weights change often without announcement, so treat any list as one reading at one point in time

#### What to do

Make posts people will bookmark, share and comment on. Make them original and full of value, and avoid negative content that collects reports

Before posting, ask whether someone would bookmark it, reply to it or send it to a friend

Don't let AI write and post all of your copy, and spread your posts out, because posting too many in a row lowers reach

### Where creators disagree

One view says the algorithm is so good that no traction means weak content. That is a strong claim and isn't proven. The same list shows weights that changed (like 0.5 in August, 0.66 on October 30). Treat the numbers as one reading at one point in time

### Mistakes

- chasing likes, which are one of the weakest positive signals in this reading
- posting too many in a row, so not every post gets a chance
- content that gets muted, blocked or reported
- treating these weights as proven facts

### Try it

Take one draft. Answer the share test: would someone bookmark it, reply to it or send it to a friend, and why? Then check it for anything that could get it muted, blocked or reported

## Cheat sheet: module 4

- check your first line for one second: the subject and the stakes should both be clear
- check any psychology stat for a real source before you repeat it
- plant a question people can guess at, then answer it with something they didn't expect
- when you close one question, open a new one, and don't give away the answer too early
- wrap a new idea in something familiar, and add a number or detail that makes it stand out
- give old truths a new name and a short definition
- pick the emotion for the job: sadness for clicks, awe or anger for shares
- go one step away from the core pain so it doesn't sound like a sales pitch
- show proof from someone like the viewer before you mention a flaw
- repeat a look, a voice and a format so people recognise you
- make content people want to be seen sharing, and stand against one thing
- match the size of your ask to the trust you have built
- use specific numbers and concrete words
- give every piece one high point and a strong last line
- treat algorithm weights as one creator's reading that can change
