# Module 8: Distribution and growth

*Get eyes on your work, then turn attention into trust, an owned audience and sales.*

Posting alone doesn't grow an account. Creators here say growth comes from getting content in front of people through replies, quotes, collabs and the right platforms, then keeping those people on channels you own. They also say patience and reps matter more than early numbers, and that views only matter if they turn into followers and buyers. Pick one platform, repeat what wins, and match your content to where the audience is.

## 8.1 The one principle: eyes on your work

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Posting alone does nothing. Growth comes from getting your work in front of people, and from making work good enough that people pass it on for you.

### Why posting is not enough

- Dan Koe: you can't post and hope the algorithm saves you. You have to get your content in front of people and shared by people with bigger audiences. He calls this 'the one and only principle for social media growth'.
- Koe's three things for success: 1) what you do, and is it valuable; 2) how many people know what you do; 3) is it valuable to the people who know, and if not, how do you make it valuable (marketing and sales).
- His formula: consistency, effort, improving the thing, and growing how many people know about it. Together that equals success.
- Koe says it's fine to write into the void for practice, but at some point you must put real effort into getting eyes on your content.
- Shann Holmberg: 'If nobody sees it, it doesn't matter.' Distribution is the moat builders (especially technical ones) underestimate most. The gap between 'shipped' and 'someone uses and pays for this' is almost all distribution.
- EXM7777: building got easy, getting noticed didn't. A good product nobody hears about is a product nobody buys.

### Distribution is trust at scale

- Shann defines distribution as 'trust at scale'. AI levelled the building floor but didn't touch the trust floor.
- Taste feeds distribution (Shann): if what you make is really good, people distribute it for you because it made them think. If it's generic, no posting frequency saves it.
- Shann's split: use AI for the logistics (formatting, repurposing, scheduling, analytics) and put your own energy into making the thing worth distributing.
- Ryan (@ryangrowthh) puts it as a story: 'distribution is just other people distributing your story'.

### Koe's big-picture growth steps

- Step 1: write persuasive content.
- Step 2: get eyes on it (replies, quotes, reposts, DMs, remixing, collabs, group chats, covered in later lessons).
- Step 3: iterate on your ideas with data, every month.
- Step 4: create depth and span with your best ideas: long form, and more platforms, so you're not 'a slave to that platform'.

### When views are low, look at the content first

- Sam Gaudet: the algorithm is just a reflection of people's interests. Swap 'the algorithm isn't picking up my video' for 'the audience isn't connecting with my message'.
- Sam: 100 to 300 views is not a shadowban. People just don't want that piece.
- Ryan's hard line: 'the algorithms are so good that if you're not getting traction, it's because your content's not good'. That's his claim, useful as motivation, not proven.

### Frameworks

**The 3 things for success** (Dan Koe)

1. What you do: is it valuable?
2. Reach: how many people know what you do?
3. Fit: is it valuable to the people who know? If not, fix it with marketing and sales

**Big-picture growth steps** (Dan Koe)

1. Write persuasive content
2. Get eyes on it
3. Iterate on ideas with data monthly
4. Add depth (long form) and span (more platforms) with your best ideas

### Examples

- **Dan Koe, digital art account:** Art curation accounts grew by sharing other artists' best work. Koe got featured by using their hashtag, commenting consistently and DMing them. Over about 2 months many small accounts shared his posts, and big accounts (100K to 500K followers) shared them 10 to 20 times. He gained about 2,500 followers.
  <br>*Why it works:* Koe uses it to show growth came from other people putting his work in front of their audiences, not from posting alone.

### Common mistakes

- Posting and waiting for the algorithm to find you.
- Blaming a shadowban when the audience simply didn't want the piece (Sam).
- Trying to fix weak, generic work with more posting (Shann).

## 8.2 Interest media: how algorithms pick who sees you

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Feeds now show people what they're interested in, not who they follow. The algorithm tests each post on strangers, so every post can win, and the wrong kind of views can hurt you.

### From follower feeds to interest media

- GaryVee: five years ago you saw posts from people you followed. Now the feed shows what you're interested in. 'The algorithm changed no one. The algorithm exposed everyone.'
- GaryVee: the only number he watches on every platform is views per post, because 'the creative is the variable'. A brand new account's 4th post can outrun his (he has about 50M followers).
- GaryVee: 'your third post can change the course of your career'. Judge each post on its merit, not on your follower count.
- GaryVee: the platforms finally want what you want (keep people on the app), so relevant posts get reach for free. 'For the first time ever in marketing, creative is creating the reach.'
- Sam Gaudet: since TikTok, social media became 'interest media'. You see what the algorithm picks for you, mostly from people you don't follow.
- Sam: brands built on the old follower feed (2016 to 2018) lost reach. A 10K account and a 1M account compete for the same attention, so small creators grow faster than ever and big ones face more competition. He thinks personal brands as they worked in 2016 are dead.
- Sam's answer: pick one interest your audience wants from you and go all in. Focus on serving people, views and staying relevant.

### Kallaway's model: the algorithm is a matchmaker

- Step 1: it 'fingerprints' the piece: computer vision on the visuals, the audio or transcript, plus caption and creator data, then builds a 'fit score' for who should like it.
- Step 2: it shows it to a first test group (he says about 200 people), mostly non-followers. Strangers liking it is the real signal.
- Step 3: good data pushes it to about 2,000, then about 20,000 and on. Mixed data re-tests another small group. Bad data stops it. '200 view jail' means the first test group didn't like it.
- What it reads: average watch time and completion, engagement rate (likes plus comments plus shares divided by views), and 'watch-time session share' (how much of someone's session was your content, which nobody can see).
- Caveat: the '200 people' and the exact steps are Kallaway's simplified model (he cites outlier data and Instagram's CEO), not published platform rules.

### Audience matching: stay on the same few topics

- Ryan says the same for X: one idea per post helps the algorithm, because mixed topics make it hard to know who to show a post to, especially inside SimClusters (interest groups).
- Kallaway's 'shotgun topics' pitfall: his account mixing tech and marketing got millions of views but didn't grow, because each post only interested half the audience. One topic per account.

### The wrong views hurt you

- Dominic Bair (@domschats, Head of Social at Wispr Flow): 'Not all views are created equal, most can hurt long-term growth.'
- Kallaway's viral trap: a huge viral piece on a broad topic gets stuck with passive scrollers in the middle. A specific piece travels to the people who buy. He says never chase the broadest ('max') ring: a viral max video brings the wrong followers and confuses the algorithm.
- Sam Gaudet: views only count if they turn into followers. Views without follows build 'negative brand association to your face': people saw you, didn't like it, didn't follow.

### Frameworks

**How the algorithm decides** (Kallaway)

1. Fingerprint: read visuals, audio, caption and creator data to build a fit score
2. Test: show it to a small group of mostly non-followers (about 200 in his model)
3. Expand or stop: good data goes to about 2,000 then 20,000, mixed data re-tests, bad data stops

### Examples

- **GaryVee, new TikTok account:** A brand new account ('You're somebody now'), first post, zero followers, a slight re-edit of a clip that had already done well: 8.2M organic views. He says he's heading to 1,000+ niche accounts.
  <br>*Why it works:* His proof that the creative, not the follower count, now creates reach.
- **Dominic Bair, the gorilla video:** Imagine a Claude page posts a Claude-for-marketers reel (50k views, 500 followers), then a gorilla-runs-a-marathon video (5M views, 5,000 followers). The next product video is shown to the gorilla crowd, they don't engage, and the algorithm stops pushing it.
  <br>*Why it works:* Off-topic viral views bring followers who teach the algorithm the wrong audience.

### Common mistakes

- Judging a post by your follower count instead of its own views (GaryVee).
- Mixing unrelated topics on one account (Kallaway's shotgun topics).
- Chasing broad viral hits that bring followers who don't care about your main topic.

### Where the sources disagree

Kallaway says posting time, hashtags and captions 'don't matter' to the algorithm. Logan Smith found posting time did matter in his tests (see the lesson on frequency and timing).

## 8.3 The X algorithm, as creators explain it

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan reads X's open-source code as three stages: Scouts find posts, a Judge scores them by what each viewer will probably do, and a Bouncer filters them. Shares, replies and quotes count far more than likes, and negative feedback is brutal. All of this is creators' reading, not official rules.

### Read this first: whose claims these are

- Everything here is Ryan's (@ryangrowthh) reading of X's open-source code. He says himself he is 'NOT from the X algorithm team', it's 'purely my interpretation', and X keeps some parts closed.
- @pmitu (who says he grew from 400 to 26,000 followers) gives a second reading. Treat both as 'appears to', not fact.
- Ryan: the weights change often without announcement. He says the like weight moved from 0.5 in August to 0.66 by October 30, 2026.

### Stage 1: the Scouts (where posts come from)

- Thunder: posts from people you follow, your 'bubble'.
- Phoenix: posts from people you don't follow yet. Ryan calls it 'where the virality lies': it predicts what you'll want before you've followed the poster.
- SimClusters: posts popular in your niche. Accounts and posts are grouped by who engages with what. Ryan's Head of Content article went viral inside the tech startup, marketing and content niche, not across the whole platform.
- Popular Posts: a fourth, smaller source, a shared list of popular recent posts, first seeded from the most-followed active accounts.

### Stage 2: the Judge (the weights)

- For every post and every viewer, a model (Ryan says built on the same architecture as Grok) predicts how likely the person is to like, reply, share, read the whole thing, follow and so on, then adds up a weighted score.
- Ryan's positive weights (production defaults as of October 30, 2026): share via copy link 20, reply 5, quote 5, share via DM 5, follow author 4, share 2, repost 1, like 0.66, click into post 0.3, open link 0.2, dwell 0.05, photo expand 0.05, profile click 0, video quality view 0.
- Ryan's negative weights: not dwelled (scrolled past) -0.02, block author -31.2, not interested -52.3, mute author -58.8, report -234. In October he summed it up as 'one report equals 350 likes'.
- His takeaways: shares are king (X wants people on the platform), replies and quotes count 7.5x a like, likes 'barely move the needle', negative feedback is 'absolutely brutal'.
- In October Ryan also described a 'user debias' model: likes from people who like everything count less, engagement from picky people counts more, so engagement pods got weaker.

### Adjustments after the score

- Posting too much: if you post too many posts, not all of them get a chance to be pushed.
- Out-of-network discount: posts from accounts the viewer doesn't follow get their score multiplied by a factor below 1.
- Small account boost: small accounts get a boost on fresh posts. Ryan says he had his first viral post 3 days after starting. @pmitu says accounts under 1,000 followers appear to get extra discovery.

### Stage 3: the Bouncer (filters and reputation)

- Filtered out: posts older than 48 hours (X is real-time), posts you've already seen, reposts and replies from people you don't follow, blocked and muted accounts and keywords, spam, flagged accounts and bots.
- Account credibility: a PageRank-style score based on who follows and engages with you. 'Network quality matters (not just size).'
- X tracks inauthentic behaviour over time, and its transparency tool shows labels that limit your visibility, including posts marked as spam.
- Ryan's way to stay safe: don't let AI write and post 100% of your copy, get Premium (and verify your ID if you're comfortable), avoid content that gets you muted, blocked or reported.

### Myths and quirks (Ryan)

- Myth: 'profile clicks are a huge signal'. Weighted 0 now, 'conversions are what count' (a follow is worth 4).
- Myth: 'big accounts get guaranteed reach'. He's seen many big accounts die over about 18 months.
- Myth: 'small accounts can't go viral'. They get a cold-start boost.
- Engagement groups no longer work like they used to.
- Quirks: each feed is built from about 2,000+ candidate posts, the model reads your last ~1,000 actions to predict what you want, and it uses signals like installed apps, inferred gender and location (from IP).
- Ryan: 'Algos are essentially games and math'. The job is to match content with the right people to keep them on the platform, so you reverse-engineer what gets pushed instead of posting whatever you want.

### @pmitu's reading of what counts

- Original posts reach non-followers best. Don't build growth on replies, reposts, copied text or recycled content.
- Sharing is one of the strongest signals, especially copying the link to send to someone. Ask: would someone send this to a friend?
- Replies and quotes are strong because they create conversations. Conversations between people who follow each other appear to weigh significantly more.
- A post that makes someone follow you sends a strong signal, so some posts should give a clear reason to hit follow.
- Time spent inside the post is the major metric. Bookmarks appear to have little or no direct ranking value.
- For video, a tap isn't enough now: if people open it and leave at once it doesn't help much, so start with the strongest part and cut long intros.
- His biggest takeaway: X is getting better at telling real interest from empty engagement. It wants people to read, watch, reply, quote, share and follow. 'Not more content, better content.'

### Frameworks

**The Scout, the Judge and the Bouncer** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Scouts: Thunder (people you follow), Phoenix (people you don't follow yet), SimClusters (popular in your niche), plus Popular Posts
2. Judge: predicts each viewer's likely actions and gives a weighted score
3. Bouncer: filters old, seen, spammy or low-reputation posts before the For You feed

### Examples

- **Ryan, his Head of Content article:** The article went viral inside the tech startup, marketing and content niche, but not platform-wide.
  <br>*Why it works:* Ryan uses it to show SimClusters: posts spread through interest groups first.

### Common mistakes

- Chasing likes, which Ryan calls one of the weakest signals.
- Posting things that collect mutes, blocks, reports or 'not interested', which he weights far below zero.
- Treating any creator's weight table as official or permanent. Ryan says the weights change without notice.

### Where the sources disagree

Window: Ryan's October posts say the first ~2 hours of a post are everything, while his article and @pmitu both describe a 48-hour window (and @pmitu says reply on your own post in the first 30 minutes). Bookmarks: @pmitu says they appear to have little direct ranking value, while Ryan's share test still asks 'would someone bookmark it?' as a sign a post is worth spreading. Both can be true: a save can show value without pushing ranking.

## 8.4 Growing on X: what to do and what to avoid

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** The practical rules creators draw from their reading of X: make posts people share, reply to and quote, stay original and on one idea, and stay away from anything that gets you muted, reported or flagged as fake.

### Why creators take X seriously

- Ryan: creators can post every format on X, the users are smart and rich, and 'most creators still treat it as a side platform'. He calls not taking X seriously 'the most expensive mistake a creator can make'.
- Ryan: X is the best platform for networking and for testing ideas fast, and it suits writers, founders and operators.
- Codie Sanchez describes X as 'the angry middle-aged guy who hasn't become what he wants yet': cerebral, wants a challenge, 'a little smarter and a little ruder than feels comfortable'.
- Codie: on X, contrarian takes, one-liners and predictions that start a small fight in the replies win. Feel-good posts 'trying to be liked instead of right' lose. Posts die in a day.
- Ryan (Oct 31): 'the algorithms are so good now that your account can do a 180 in just a few days'. It used to take weeks to 'warm' an account.

### What to do (Ryan and @pmitu)

- Make content people want to share: easy to scan, easy to send. Ryan's share test before posting: would someone bookmark it, reply to it, or send it to a friend?
- Spark conversation: opinions, observations, frameworks, questions or ideas people can react to (@pmitu). You want people replying in your comments (Ryan).
- Give real value and original insight from your experience, network and job, your 'unfair market advantages' (Ryan).
- Grab attention fast: learn hooks and copywriting. If you mainly make video, invest in a proper camera, mic and light (Ryan). @pmitu adds you can start simple with sincere, self-made founder videos, no special effects.
- One idea per post. If a post has several ideas, split it. Every post needs a clear takeaway, a specific person it's for and a specific call to action (Ryan).
- Test every format X is pushing: threads, articles, media (Ryan). Use several formats.
- Write hooks that are strong but accurate. If the headline promises something the post doesn't deliver, people leave and the algorithm punishes it (@pmitu).

### Getting comments: Kallaway's 5 ways

- Take a hard stance.
- Take the contrarian side.
- Push the framing further ('better than every pasta shop in the world').
- Talk about things people already have opinions on (Nike, not 'shoes').
- Make them feel something.

### What to avoid

- Engagement bait ('like if you agree', 'comment YES to receive it', 'tag 3 friends'). @pmitu says it's punished more than ever.
- Hashtag stuffing: 'hashtags are not a growth strategy, we are not in 2015 anymore' (@pmitu).
- Repeating identical or nearly identical text, which looks spammy. Reuse ideas with a new angle, format or insight instead (@pmitu).
- Negativity and politics. Ryan says reports and mutes 'significantly devalue your account'. Block constant haters. Don't argue with trolls: block or mute them, because arguing risks them muting or blocking you.
- Reply spam and AI slop replies, which Ryan claims 'can suppress your account to the point of irreversible damage'.
- Bots: no bot replies to strangers, no automated likes or follows, no copy-pasting the same post across accounts, or the account gets hidden or banned (EXM7777).
- Downloading and re-uploading other people's content. Credit others and use X's quote and post-media features instead (Ryan).

### Links, calls to action and Creator Rewards (Ryan)

- Ryan says X punishes posts that send people off the platform, so keep only about 10% of posts bottom of funnel and put the call to action in the replies (his example: 'we work with founders struggling with content, DM me this word').
- Creator Rewards: X pays for impressions from people outside your network. Three things block it: AI-written posts or replies, engagement groups ('1M impressions but only 50k verified = the same circle recycling your posts'), and re-uploading other people's content (quote it instead).
- Profile basics he recommends: get verified, show your face, a 2 to 3 line bio of what you do and who it's for, a simple banner, a pinned post that converts.

### Hashtags and keywords on other platforms

- Logan Smith: hashtags and title keywords are 'only the tip of the iceberg'.
- Mino Lee: maybe 10% extra views, not what takes you from 1,000 to 100,000. 'Just make a better video.' He doesn't use hashtags.

### Frameworks

**Ryan's practical advice for the X algorithm** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Make content people want to share
2. Grab attention fast (hooks, copywriting, setup)
3. Post original content and credit others
4. One idea per post
5. Avoid negativity, block or mute trolls
6. Build a dense network of high-quality people
7. Test every format X is pushing

### Common mistakes

- Engagement bait like 'comment YES' (@pmitu).
- Posting the same text again and again.
- Arguing with trolls instead of muting or blocking them (Ryan).
- Letting AI write and post all of your copy and replies (Ryan).
- Putting the sales link in the main post instead of the replies (Ryan).

### Where the sources disagree

Ryan says get verified and show your face, and calls faceless 'hard mode'. He also admits 'if you're good at content you can build a faceless brand, but most people aren't willing to dox'. So showing a face is easier, not required.

### Try it

Go through your last 10 posts and tag any that break a rule above (engagement bait, two ideas in one post, a link in the main post, repeated text, negativity). Rewrite one of them so it has one idea, one reader and a reason to share.

## 8.5 Choosing your platforms

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Win one platform before spreading out. Pick it by where your audience already is, what you're good at (writing, talking, camera), how much time you have, and the format that platform pushes hardest.

### Rented, owned and earned (Ryan)

- Rented: platforms you don't control (X, TikTok, Instagram, YouTube). Best for reach.
- Owned: audiences you reach directly (newsletter, community, website). For trust and sales.
- Earned: other people's audiences (collabs, podcasts). For mindshare.
- Mass distribution needs all three. Use rented platforms to find people and owned channels to keep them.

### How to pick

- Ryan: start with 1 to 2 platforms max. Ask where your audience already spends time, whether you're better at writing, talking or being on camera, and how much time you can really commit.
- Ryan's time rule: low time = 1 to 2 channels, about 8 to 12 hours a week = 2 to 4 channels, high = 5+ with repurposing.
- Kallaway: pick where your buyers spend the most time and the format that platform pushes hardest.
- Sam Gaudet: pick one platform to optimise for over the next 6 to 12 months. 'Focus is an amplifier.' Spread across 7 platforms and you get bad results everywhere.
- Sam: the best platform is the one you already use, the app you open when you pick up your phone. You understand its feel better than anyone who doesn't use it daily, and otherwise you'll hate the process in what is 'a marathon, not a sprint'.
- Codie Sanchez: 'you win by being undeniable in one place', then expand. Each platform is a different person.

### Platform notes from the creators

- Ryan: X is best for networking and testing ideas fast (low to medium effort). YouTube is slow to start but compounds like nothing else. LinkedIn is underrated, with less competition and buyers. A newsletter has the highest engagement right after posting. Podcasts are high effort to run, low effort to guest on. A community is for retention.
- Kallaway's 'hero format' per platform: Instagram, TikTok, Facebook and Snapchat = short video. YouTube = 10 to 30 minute videos. LinkedIn = short text or the new LinkedIn short video (which he calls 'the biggest untapped opportunity right now', his opinion). X and Threads = short text. X is his pick for developer tools, crypto, startups and tech buyers.
- Dan Koe for writers: social media writing is the simplest traffic source. It's free, 'social media is a skill, not luck', you can post every day, test endless ideas, and you don't need video, audio or design skills.
- Koe's options for writers: X has potential and deep ideas. Threads is good for beginners but has lower market sophistication (like Instagram), so more trolling. LinkedIn has the most money but is boring and professional. Substack is his pick, 'where deep thinkers live', and followers are subscribers, so no separate email tool is needed.
- Sam Gaudet: he'd start with Instagram, then add one more. In his view Instagram and YouTube perform best right now, and he grew only on those two.
- Mino Lee: TikTok is better for studying hooks (its search and For You page are more refined). What works on TikTok generally works on Instagram, because both measure engagement and watch time.
- Sam's supply and demand point: his claim is that Facebook has billions of users but less new content than YouTube or Instagram, so they grew Dan Martell's Facebook faster by filling the gap.
- Ryan (Flex call takeaway): YouTube works as an acquisition channel for B2B.

### Don't spread too early

- Kallaway calls it 'failure by suffocation': spreading to new platforms too early. Stay on one platform and one format until a system runs 30 days in a row without breaking. He calls this his own biggest mistake.
- Kallaway's 'violent volume': he stayed only on YouTube for 18 months before touching Instagram or TikTok. Spread over 5 platforms and you have a fifth of your effort in each.
- Ryan: master one platform before going everywhere. Spreading thin causes 'ops headaches and strategy drift'. 'Pour all your water into one cup' instead of half-filling 3 or 4.
- Kallaway's later recipe once one platform works: 1 short-form platform for reach (7 posts a week), 1 long-form for depth (1 to 2 a week), 1 nurture channel (email or community).

### Frameworks

**Rented, owned, earned** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Rented: platforms you don't control, for reach
2. Owned: newsletter, community, website, for trust and sales
3. Earned: collabs and podcasts, for mindshare

**Reach, depth, nurture** (Kallaway)

1. 1 short-form platform for reach, about 7 posts a week
2. 1 long-form platform for depth, 1 to 2 a week
3. 1 nurture channel: email or community

### Common mistakes

- Starting on 5 or 7 platforms at once (Kallaway's failure by suffocation).
- Picking a platform you never use yourself (Sam).
- Picking a format the platform doesn't push (Kallaway's hero format).

### Where the sources disagree

Where to start: Dan Koe says grow on short form first, where it's easier and you control more, then move the audience to long form (he says YouTube and podcasts need about 50 videos before anyone cares). Ryan says start on a long-form platform (X or YouTube) and 'trickle down' ideas to short form after 2 to 3 months. Best platform: Koe picks Substack for writers, Sam picks Instagram and YouTube, Kallaway picks X for tech buyers.

## 8.6 How often and when to post

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Creators disagree on volume and timing. The common ground: a pace you can keep, enough space between posts for each one to run, and quality first.

### Ryan's two routes on X (never the middle)

- Quantity route: up to 24 posts a day, at least 1 hour apart. Only for news accounts or very fast niches (AI, crypto). You accept flops, 'throwing darts and hoping 1 to 2 a day stick'.
- Later (Nov 2) he says space posts 2 hours minimum. In his plans: 3 to 6 value posts a day (90-day plan), 'a few posts a day' when starting.
- @pmitu agrees on spacing: don't post several things back to back, because they compete for the same audience. 'Make every post earn its own distribution before tweeting the next one.'

### Quality versus quantity

- @pmitu: one great post beats six average posts. Posts compete with each other in the same feed. Spend the time making one idea great (he suggests keeping drafts on your phone).
- Shann Holmberg's volume trap: people post 1,000+ AI pieces a day across hundreds of accounts and engagement trends toward zero. 'Quantity without quality is just noise at scale.' What performs is rarely the information, it's whether the reader trusts the writer.
- GaryVee no longer says 'post 40 times a day'. It's a quality to quantity ratio: post as much as you can at your quality.
- Kallaway's 'quality-quantity accordion': get quality to 7/10, raise volume to the platform's normal (his numbers: short video 1 a day, YouTube 1 a week, short text about 5 posts a day), then push quality to 9/10. Then 6 months consistent.
- Dominic Bair (@domschats): reaching 100M views a month needs brute-force volume, likely 20+ posts a day across platforms for brands, founders and employee content programs. Early on, daily posts let you brute-force your way to winning content pillars: make content, iterate, find winners, make more.
- Ernesto (@ErnestoSOFTWARE) scaled his app's content to 6,000 videos a month (more in the founder and team lesson).

### Logan Smith's tests (YouTube Shorts)

- Day of the week didn't matter at all: shorts failed and succeeded on every day.
- Time of day did: his morning posts got noticeably more views. Don't copy his time. Check YouTube Studio, Analytics, Audience to see when your audience is most active and post then.
- Frequency across two channels, from twice a week to four a day: 4 a day was too much (only about half got picked up, and quality dropped). Twice a week worked better but felt unproductive. Once a day was his sweet spot: time for quality plus steady exposure.
- If daily is too much, tone it down. Consistency matters most. Don't go too long without uploading or you lose momentum.

### Consistency is mostly for you

- Paddy Galloway: a consistent schedule doesn't matter algorithmically, but it matters a lot mentally and for building repetition. 'If you're really good you can post whenever you want, but you're not really good at the start.'
- Sam Gaudet's two levers for reach: your best topics and 100% consistency.
- Caleb Ralston: consistency is what creates the association (Michael Jordan wore Nikes every game, not just the finals).

### Galloway's schedule for winning a niche (YouTube)

- Goal: become the biggest channel in your niche. His schedule: 1 long-form video a week (15 to 40 minutes) with 5 skips a year, plus 3+ Shorts a week.
- The week gives a 7-day turnaround to make a good 15+ minute video. The 5 skips cover holidays or a video that isn't good enough: 'I never want to post a bad video just to hit a schedule.'

### Frameworks

**Quality-quantity accordion** (Kallaway)

1. Raise quality to about 7/10
2. Raise volume to the platform's normal pace
3. Push quality to 9/10 at that volume
4. Hold it for 6 months, answer every comment, move people to email

### Examples

- **Logan Smith, frequency test:** He posted from twice a week up to four times a day across two channels. At four a day only about half got picked up and quality fell. Once a day gave him time for quality and steady exposure.
  <br>*Why it works:* His proof that more posts isn't automatically more reach.

### Common mistakes

- Posting several posts back to back so they compete (@pmitu, Ryan).
- Picking the middle route: too many posts for quality, too few for the darts approach (Ryan).
- Copying another creator's best posting time instead of checking your own audience (Logan Smith).
- Raising volume before quality reaches a decent level (Kallaway).

### Where the sources disagree

Volume: Kallaway says about 5 short text posts a day on X, Ryan says 3 to 5 (or up to 24 for news), @domschats says 20+ a day across platforms at scale, while @pmitu says one great post beats six average ones. Sam Gaudet says both 'just post less' and 'volume negates luck' in different videos; the fair reading is a pace you can keep for years. Timing: Logan Smith found morning posts did better; Mino Lee says 'the algorithm does not care what time you post' (he posted half his videos at 1 a.m.); Kallaway also says posting time doesn't matter; Ryan says timing matters less than spacing.

## 8.7 Replies, comments and networking

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Replies and real relationships are the cheapest way to get seen, but only when the reply is interesting, about your own experience, and under posts people actually read.

### Replies that get profile clicks (Dan Koe)

- Replies are the low-hanging fruit everyone recommends, and you have to do them. If your reply captures attention, holds it and delivers value, readers click your profile and follow.
- Mistake 1: boring posts don't make people read replies. A tweet can get huge likes while only 100 people open the replies, if nothing sparks conversation. Reply to posts that spark discussion.
- Mistake 2: boring replies don't get profile clicks. Stop repeating the original post. Talk about yourself and how you experienced it. That drives the most profile clicks.
- Mistake 3: small accounts get fewer impressions. Reply and network with accounts your size, but that won't grow you much on its own. Also reply to bigger accounts with interesting content.
- Keep replying over months. You need your name in people's mouths. It compounds: 'there's that guy again', which leads to DMs, follows, and the creators themselves noticing you.

### Network building on X (Ryan, Matt Gray, @pmitu)

- Ryan: build your network by engaging with others, and only reply-guy when you have real value to add. Ask real questions under other people's posts, not 'nice post'.
- Ryan: 'you can spend under 15 minutes a day writing a few tweets and build an insane network'.
- Ryan: build a dense network of high-quality people in your niche. It helps reach and the account's credibility score.
- Ryan (Nov 4 list): become great first, then expand your network. Reply to and engage with people you find helpful, 'they might give away free sauce'. He also uses networking tools off X (he sent 170+ connection requests on Cosign to people in his city).
- Matt Gray's connections step: follow 50 people in your space, reply to 10 big ones, DM 5 to 10 a day, give first. He says those replies led him to James Clear, who introduced him to his book editor.
- @pmitu: X is a social network, so be social. Ask people, reply, support, build relationships, and follow people you really interact with. Conversations between people who follow each other appear to carry more weight.

### DMs (Dan Koe)

- Manual DMs are slow and hard, but networking helps growth and gets reposts.
- Don't be a corporate robot. His parody: 'great to connect, we should have a virtual coffee and talk about Zapier automations'.
- Make friends to the point where they're happy to share what you ask.
- DMs have diminishing returns for lead gen. Effort is better spent selling a high-ticket service (\$2,500) than a \$150 product, and far better than spending a DM to get one impression on a post. Koe: technically a post is an offer, and its conversion is engagement.

### Talk to your own followers

- Sam Gaudet: recognition is the cheapest loyalty. Reply to comments and DMs, reshare stories and posts that tag you, comment on their videos, run giveaways or free meetups, or just say thank you. People want to be recognised and feel they know you, and then they help the movement.
- Sam: block 30 to 40 minutes in your calendar for it.
- Sam: 'post and ghost' creators who never answer comments or DMs build no relationship and no influence.
- Kallaway: answer every comment, take people to DMs, and give them an owned place to go deeper (newsletter).
- Ryan: stay close to a community by answering questions, going live and sharing behind the scenes.

### Reading replies

- Codie Sanchez: on X, watch replies. A 1 to 3% reply rate is excellent.
- Codie: mine the comments for unanswered questions ('but how do I do this with no money?' is your next piece).
- Ryan: his bot watches his DMs, and when 3+ people ask the same question he makes content on it. His 202k-view crash course came from exactly that.
- heyDominik: in 'my photos 10 years ago vs now', photo quality matters less than you'd think. People argue in the comments, and that helps it go viral.

### Frameworks

**Matt Gray's connections routine** (Matt Gray)

1. Follow 50 people in your space
2. Reply to 10 big accounts
3. DM 5 to 10 people a day
4. Give first

### Examples

- **Matt Gray, replies to a book deal:** Gray says the replies he left on bigger accounts led him to James Clear, who then introduced him to his book editor.
  <br>*Why it works:* Shows how steady replies turn into relationships that open doors.

### Common mistakes

- Replies that repeat the original post (Koe).
- Replying under boring posts nobody opens the replies on (Koe).
- Only replying to accounts your own size (Koe).
- AI slop or spam replies, which Ryan claims can suppress an account.

## 8.8 Borrowing reach: quotes, remixes, collabs and group chats

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** The fastest way to new people is through people who already have them. Quote, remix and credit bigger creators, collaborate with overlapping audiences, and build real creator friendships, all with good content as the base.

### Quote posts and reposts (Dan Koe)

- A quote post is a repost with your commentary on top (on Instagram, share to your story with commentary and a tag). The creator may see it and repost it.
- A bland 'this is great' gives nobody a reason to repost or click. Talk about yourself and write persuasively.
- Quoting a 500K account is a numbers game and luck, they probably won't see it. Make quote posts targeted and worth your time.
- Reposts: your content must be good and shareable enough that people want it on their own feed.
- Ryan: on X, a quote post works when your take adds tension or value, not just 'this'. Credit others and use X's own quote and post-media features instead of copying.

### Remixing what's already big

- Koe: DJs grow by remixing popular songs and borrowing their traffic. YouTubers make videos about popular people. Podcasts book big guests.
- Koe's 4 moves for writers: 1) quote creators in a thread or newsletter, use their ideas to illustrate a point, then DM them that you mentioned them (they may repost without being asked); 2) commentate on their content, like reaction videos; 3) bring them on a podcast (people love being on podcasts and share the episode, and it's a way to befriend bigger people you can ask for something later); 4) talk about their ideas and credit them, for example 'the best idea I've seen this week', how it affected you, and who wrote it.
- Koe: helping spread other people's names makes them help spread yours.
- Kallaway's 'cult hopping': reference known brands, celebrities or movements to borrow credibility while you're small. He also calls showing a face or brand people already know 'speed to relatability'.
- Kallaway: stitching or reacting to content that already went viral is another way to draft off what's big.

### Collabs

- Sam Gaudet: collab only with audience overlap. Podcasts, stages and collab reels only work if the other person's audience looks like yours. The wrong niche brings the wrong audience.
- MrBeast: find channels that make content similar to yours (by comparing titles and content). They're useful as collab partners and for inspiration.
- Ryan: earned channels (collabs, podcasts) give you mindshare. Podcasts are high effort to run but low effort to be a guest on.
- Oren Meets World includes 'a collab every month' in his creative direction for a personal brand.

### Group chats and masterminds (Dan Koe)

- Koe's claim: every creator you know is in group chats with other creators, talking strategy and sharing and commenting on each other's posts. Some comment on each other's posts every morning, others repost what's shared in the group. You can spot tribes of 5 to 10 creators who all talk to each other.
- He prefers the word 'mastermind' over 'engagement group': shared minds work better than one (an idea he credits to Napoleon Hill). It can become lifelong friendships, not just transactions.
- You'll need to DM people and build social skills. Leave a group if it feels purely transactional and start a new one.
- Test all of these mechanisms over 6 to 12 months.

### Paid reposts, Koe's version

- Not running ads for followers, which he calls 'the worst thing you can do' (people lose hundreds of followers a day), and not shoutout pages with 'follow this account' captions.
- What he means: take a truly authentic post you'd write anyway and pay a bigger account (one that offers this as a service) to repost it or reply to it, with no caption and no 'go follow'. People who like it follow, and you nurture them. He calls it 10x more authentic than ads with sleazy direct-response tactics.
- Good content is a must. With bad content a repost does little. He says he tried it at the start, it worked because the content was good, and he no longer does it.
- Large accounts get hundreds of DMs a day and need a filter, so payment gets attention.
- Replies work as paid growth mainly on X, where someone replying to you shows your post to their followers. On Instagram replies don't boost you on their feed.

### Other people as amplifiers

- Kallaway's personal brand amplifiers: clips of you shared by others (podcast clips, reactions, clipping accounts, a 'clipper army') multiply your reach beyond your own time. His example: Goggins clips from Rogan's podcast.
- Kallaway: characters spread you too. Several people with the same interest broadcast the same association (MrBeast's crew, Colin and Samir).
- Caleb Ralston: named concepts and redefined terms spread because other people reference them in their own content.
- Kallaway's speedrun: one 'igniter' video plus a 'super share' from a big account kicks growth off. He calls about 5,000 followers the 'minimum credibility' line.
- Kallaway on Diary of a CEO: no call to action on clips, just the guest's name and the show link in the bio. 'Rising tide lifts all boats', so each clip is made for shares.

### Frameworks

**Koe's 4 ways to borrow traffic as a writer** (Dan Koe)

1. Quote them in a thread or newsletter, then DM them that you did
2. Commentate on their content
3. Bring them on a podcast
4. Share and credit their ideas ('the best idea I've seen this week')

### Examples

- **Kallaway, Goggins on Rogan:** Clips of David Goggins on Joe Rogan's podcast spread across many accounts that weren't his.
  <br>*Why it works:* Kallaway's example of amplifiers: other people spreading your association for you, which he says is why some brands get huge in 3 to 4 years.

### Common mistakes

- Bland quote posts like 'this is great' (Koe).
- Collabs with people whose audience doesn't overlap with yours (Sam).
- Buying follower ads or shoutout-page posts (Koe).
- Paying for reposts of weak content (Koe).
- Group chats that are purely transactional (Koe).

### Where the sources disagree

Koe recommends creator masterminds where members share and comment on each other's posts. Ryan says engagement groups 'don't work like they used to' and block Creator Rewards ('the same circle recycling your posts'), and his Oct reading says engagement pods got weaker. Koe himself separates real friendships from transactional groups.

## 8.9 Repurposing: the waterfall versus the lake

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** One idea can feed many platforms, but creators now split on how. The waterfall cuts one pillar piece into everything. The lake makes content for each main platform first, then moves only the proven winners.

### The content waterfall (Matt Gray)

- One pillar piece, for example a 13-minute YouTube video, becomes 26+ pieces.
- Short video: 3 Instagram Reels, 3 TikToks, 3 YouTube Shorts, cut from the core moments.
- Text from the script: 3 LinkedIn posts, 3 X threads, and a newsletter a few weeks later.
- Audio: distribute it on Spotify and Apple.
- Filming one video fills the next month's calendar for Instagram, TikTok, X and LinkedIn, so you're not on 'some content hamster wheel'.
- Gray: founders without this system cycle through start, give up, restart, give up.
- Gray's 'rule of five': every idea should live in 5 places (X, LinkedIn, YouTube, newsletter and more), native to each.
- Gray's X version: 1 winning post becomes 26 pieces. His '13 lessons at 33' post (4.6M views, his figure) became tweets, shorts, a long video and a newsletter.

### One piece, every platform

- Dan Koe writes the newsletter first, then pastes it to X, then turns it into a YouTube script: one piece, every platform.
- Ryan: turn one idea into many. One article becomes material for other channels. His Nov 4 list says long-form becomes 5+ short forms, and his own route is long first, then cut down.
- Ryan makes video versions of his articles 'for people that don't like reading', raw and unedited, and posts the video a few days later as a quote of the article.
- XCreators: make it once and let a bot post it to X, YouTube, Instagram and TikTok with caption and hashtags.
- But adapt per platform: a different hook and caption per app (EXM7777). Repeating identical text looks spammy on X (@pmitu). Ryan: 'be native', every platform has its own language.

### The lake method (Sam Gaudet)

- Sam's title for it: 'stop repurposing content'.
- The problem with the waterfall: it optimises for one platform, often not the one rewarding you most, and gives bad outputs on the others. His example: a YouTube video with 100 views clipped into Instagram clips that get 1,000.
- Sam says the waterfall worked back in the 'document, don't create' era, and 'omnipresent' posting of everything everywhere 'used to work in 2016'.
- The lake: pick the primary platforms that serve your goals and make content dedicated to each. Everything goes into 'the lake'. Then fish out the best performers and post them on the other platforms (an Instagram winner goes to LinkedIn), but never post everything everywhere.
- 'I'd rather you not post than post something that you know is going to fail.'
- For Dan Martell the primary platforms are Instagram and YouTube. Others still get the reels (the reel also goes on TikTok), they just don't get dedicated content.

### Tristan Kermode on the waterfall today

- In his interview with Ryan, Tristan (Iman Gadzhi's former Head of Content) says Matt Gray's waterfall works less than it did 6 to 12 months earlier.
- Each platform needs its own plan.

### Theme pages and amplification (Ernesto)

- Ernesto (@ErnestoSOFTWARE) splits it into a content machine (creates content) and content amplification (pushes it further than 1 post on 1 account). Amplification can't work without the machine.
- Theme pages are accounts you own that post your content differently: AI replicas of UGC, viral-hook reposts of already-viral videos, AI stories about the problem the product solves.
- One video goes to TikTok, Instagram and YouTube at once through a scheduler, with an agent scheduling every morning. His claim: 10M+ views from videos that had already gone viral once, reposted with a new borrowed hook at the start.

### Frameworks

**The content waterfall** (Matt Gray)

1. Make one pillar piece (a long video)
2. Cut short videos for Reels, TikTok and Shorts
3. Write LinkedIn posts, X threads and a newsletter from the script
4. Publish the audio as a podcast

**The lake method** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Pick your primary platforms
2. Make dedicated content for each
3. Let everything collect in the lake
4. Fish out the winners and move only those to other platforms

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, the 100-view YouTube video:** A YouTube video that got 100 views was clipped into Instagram clips that got 1,000.
  <br>*Why it works:* Sam uses it to show the waterfall spreads weak pieces everywhere instead of moving proven winners.

### Common mistakes

- Posting the exact same text on every platform (EXM7777, @pmitu).
- Cutting up a pillar piece that never performed on its home platform (Sam).
- Posting something everywhere when you already know it will flop (Sam).

### Where the sources disagree

Matt Gray, Dan Koe, XCreators, Ryan's Nov 4 list and Sam's older videos (Creative Director, 7 Strategies, 5 Levels) say repurpose everything: you already did 80% of the work, so pair familiar channels (Instagram to Facebook, TikTok to Shorts, YouTube to podcast audio), and Sam then claimed understanding distribution gets 10x more views. Sam's newer videos and Tristan Kermode say stop: make content for each main platform and move only winners. The honest merge in Sam's own material: make for each primary platform first, then move the proven winners.

## 8.10 Double down on your winners

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Your best growth ideas are usually your own past hits. Spot them with the right numbers, then repost, re-edit, re-record and rewrite them instead of hunting for brand new ideas.

### Spot the winners first

- Kallaway's speedrun: post daily, review in batches of 10, sort the batch by follows gained, and copy what the winner did differently.
- Matt Gray: audit your top 20% against your bottom 20% (topics, hooks, words). That's how he says he learned his audience wanted systems over mindset, and that specific numbers in hooks did '10x better'.
- Matt Gray's profile efficiency ratio = follows divided by profile visits. He says most profiles convert about 5%, his about 20%.
- Shaan Puri (with Dickie Bush and Nicolas Cole): two ratios to read a post. Likes divided by impressions (2 to 3% is a big hit) and reposts divided by likes (20 to 30% means it will spread, because people repost what they want to be seen agreeing with).
- Codie Sanchez's 3 S's for every post: start (did they stop?), stay (did it pay off fast?), signal (did they do anything?). After each post write 3 notes: why they started, where they left, which line got a reaction. 'If you can't name it, you can't repeat it.' 'The data isn't a judgment of your worth, it's a map.'
- Paddy Galloway, when a video overperforms: there's no single data point that tells you why. Look at demographics (a new country or age group), device type (doing well on TV, maybe because it's longer), new versus returning viewers, the view trend (fast spike or slow build) and traffic sources (ranking in search). Data colours in parts of the picture, so zoom out.

### Make more of what popped

- Dan Koe: every month, rewrite your best posts from new angles (short, long, list, better hook).
- Sam Gaudet: what frustrates him most is someone who gets a viral hit and doesn't repeat the topic, format or angle. Iterate on your own winners, not only other people's. A 10x outlier that brought followers goes straight back into ideation.
- Sam: when a format or topic pops, drop everything else and push it. It might only be hot for 30, 60 or 90 days. 'When things get easy, go hard.'
- GaryVee (via Sam): 20 years of one thing. When it's working, don't take your foot off the gas. 'The gains are made in the repetition.'
- Kallaway on signature formats: once one works, 'run it over and over until it stops working'.

### Sam Gaudet's 4 Rs

- Repost: every winner gets reposted after 60 days, then 60 days after that (another video says 60 to 90 days). Find pieces older than 60 days that did far better than your average. On Dan Martell's account anything over 100K goes back on the main feed, and his best videos go out 4 to 5 times. 'You're giving your audience too much credit': most haven't seen it or don't remember it.
- Like a concert: people want to hear the hits again, and you'd be upset if the artist skipped your favourite song. 'Branding is all about associations.'
- Re-edit: change a winner slightly to unlock another repost. Look at your top 30 and ask how better editing could get more. Keep the hook, move things in the middle.
- Re-record: same winning topic, new angle, new shoot. Take your top 5 from the last 6 months and film them again in a new setting. 'You don't need to come up with new ideas.'
- Repurpose: move winners across platforms (see the lake method).
- GaryVee's version: his 8.2M-view new TikTok post was a slight re-edit of a clip that had already done well.

### Instagram trial reels (Sam Gaudet, Instagram only, tactics change)

- Trial reels launched in December 2024 to test videos on non-followers. Sam's team reposted 30 top videos, sometimes 30 times a day, for 1 to 3M views. Then Instagram added duplicate detection and those reposts dropped to about 50 views.
- The double post: post as a trial reel, then 24 to 48 hours later post the same file on the main feed no matter how the trial did. His claim: it doubles reach with no extra work.
- The repost hack: take your top 30 videos and change 4 things (speed 1.1x to 1.2x, music, on-screen text and caption style, flip horizontally). His claim: an 80 to 90% chance of not being flagged. Post 3 to 5 trial reels a day.
- The super hack: cover the old on-screen text with new text using Instagram's own text tool, nothing else. His claim: still working as of October to November 2026.
- His claim: over 100 million views on trial reels for Dan in 2 years.

### Know when to stop

- Oren Meets World: a signature series works at most about 3 times a month or it dies. Check your pillars monthly and drop, replace or fix one whose saves, views or shares fall. Keep testing even when the old things work, 'it's not going to work forever'.
- Kallaway: formulas burn out, so go back to testing.

### Format search with a fast double down (Galloway)

- Galloway: formats grow channels. In channels he has grown from scratch, usually 70%+ of the growth came from 1 or 2 ideas turned into formats. Think of a channel as a show, not a TV channel.
- His loop: try an idea. If it works, do it again as fast as you can. It doesn't have to blow up: 2x your average views is enough signal. If it works twice in a row, you have a winning format. Then milk it.
- 'I don't want to saturate the audience' is a myth in his view. People overestimate viewers' appetite for novelty and underestimate their appetite for more of what they already like. Forrest Galante's channel, biggest in its niche, still has over 50% of uploads in its 2 formats.

### Frameworks

**The 4 Rs** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Repost winners every 60 to 90 days
2. Re-edit them slightly to unlock another repost
3. Re-record the winning topic with a new angle or setting
4. Repurpose winners onto other platforms

**The 3 S's** (Codie Sanchez)

1. Start: did they stop?
2. Stay: did it pay off fast?
3. Signal: did they do anything?

### Examples

- **Matt Gray, top 20% audit:** Comparing his best and worst posts showed his audience wanted systems over mindset, and that specific numbers in hooks did about 10x better.
  <br>*Why it works:* Gray uses it to show that winners tell you what to make more of.

### Common mistakes

- Getting a hit and never repeating its topic, format or angle (Sam).
- Assuming your audience remembers everything you posted (Sam, Shaan Puri).
- Running a winning series so often it dies (Oren).
- Reading one number and guessing why something worked (Paddy Galloway).

### Where the sources disagree

Sam Gaudet says repost the same winners every 60 to 90 days. On X, @pmitu warns that repeating identical or nearly identical text looks spammy and says to reuse the idea with a new angle, format or insight instead, and Ryan says re-uploaded content blocks Creator Rewards. Sam's own re-edit and re-record moves sit closer to @pmitu's advice.

## 8.11 Building an audience that spreads you

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Followers are not the goal. An active audience that comes back, replies and shares without being asked is built by being reliably useful over many pieces, giving generously, and making people feel part of something.

### A passive audience versus an active community

- Shann Holmberg: a passive audience is a commodity, and followers are a vanity metric. An active community (people who reply, share without being asked, and come back daily because you're part of how they think about a topic) is a moat.
- Shann: you earn it by being useful, saying specific things, being honest about what you know and don't, and showing up long enough. Trust runs on a different clock: AI compresses creation from days to minutes, but trust still takes months or years.
- Kallaway's cult fans: casual fans watch and leave, cult fans want to help you grow and share you. That's why cult brands seem to be everywhere.
- Kallaway: citizens (your audience) become advocates and amplifiers, and a dense, similar audience compounds faster.

### Consistency of value is what brings people back

- Kallaway: the first level of obsession comes from a higher signal-to-noise ratio than everyone else in your category, over many pieces.
- Kallaway: the top rung ('revelation', when people see you as a reliable source of value) needs non-obvious, differentiated value delivered again and again. Deep-pain audiences turn into super fans in a few pieces, nice-to-have audiences take many.
- Kallaway: results drive return. Viewers who act on your advice and get results come back 'over and over'.
- Kallaway's watering hole: an elephant goes back to the hole that had water 9 times out of 10, not the one that had it 1 time in 3. Every useful post is water.
- Sahil Bloom: create value and people pay you with attention. Evergreen beats news, something someone can come back to in years.
- Kallaway: bingeability. A follow usually needs about 4 to 5 'content minutes' (his rule of thumb), so make people binge, with hard series (numbered) or soft series (every piece feels like the same show).

### Give generously

- Caleb Ralston worried about giving away too much free value. After day 2 of filming his free 6 hour 22 minute YouTube course (12 to 13 hour filming days, about 30 hours of filming plus hundreds of hours of editing), he told his content director Trevor they could probably sell it for over a million dollars (his estimate). He released it free instead, and within 7 days met three dream brands.
- Caleb's goal now: push the limits of what's acceptable to release for free, following 'share the knowledge, sell the personalization'.
- Matt Gray: be generous to a fault. Give away so much that people wonder what the paid stuff must be.
- GaryVee: 'jab, jab, jab, right hook'. If every post is a sales pitch, people learn to duck. 'If your content is completely selfish, it's not going to work.'

### Make people feel part of it

- Ryan's community playbook: make people feel special (a name, public recognition, a visual identity, shared beliefs and language, a qualification process to get in).
- Give them a home (Ryan): Discord (real-time, younger), Telegram (updates, crypto), Skool (courses plus community), a private group (simple), or a newsletter (the best entry point, but not a community on its own).
- Rituals (Ryan): weekly events, meetups, member spotlights, challenges (like tech companies' 'Dev Days').
- Caleb Ralston: a common enemy makes people self-select. The right people join and the others stay away. Never a specific person or company.
- Sam Gaudet: recognition is the cheapest loyalty. Thank people, reshare them, meet them, and they help the movement.

### Frameworks

**Building a community** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Make people feel special: name, recognition, identity, shared language, a way in
2. Give them a home: Discord, Telegram, Skool, private group or newsletter
3. Stay close: answer questions, go live, share behind the scenes
4. Run rituals: weekly events, meetups, spotlights, challenges

### Examples

- **Caleb Ralston, the free course:** He nearly sold a 6-hour course he thought was worth over a million dollars, released it free instead, and met three dream brands within 7 days.
  <br>*Why it works:* His case for 'share the knowledge, sell the personalization'.

### Common mistakes

- Chasing follower count instead of people who reply and share (Shann).
- Selling in every post (GaryVee).
- Being useful only some of the time, like a watering hole that's often dry (Kallaway).
- Treating a newsletter alone as a community (Ryan).

### Try it

## 8.12 Own your audience: the web between channels

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Rented platforms can change or ban you overnight. Point every channel at the others with a clear reason, and move people onto something you own, an email list or community, from day one.

### Why owned matters

- Ryan: 'they could ban me tomorrow and my entire audience would be gone', so build an owned channel (newsletter, Skool) too.
- EXM7777: if your business lives on someone else's platform, one change can take it away, so the email list comes first.
- Matt Gray: X shows a post to about 15% of your followers, while email reaches 100% (his figures).
- Ryan: start your newsletter on day one, even if you can't publish regularly. For founders: start a contact list (email or text) on day one, the only audience you own.
- Dan Koe: on Substack, followers are subscribers, so you don't need a separate email tool.

### Content is a web, not a funnel (Sam Gaudet)

- People jump between YouTube, Instagram, LinkedIn and X in any order, and may never see your newsletter. That's fine.
- Being on every platform isn't enough, people won't find you by themselves. Point every platform at the others with a reason (short form to long form, long form to short form) so the web catches people, then move them to channels you own.
- Sam's own model: a free template, GPT, SOP or scorecard if you message him a keyword on Instagram, with no opt-in. Going there to DM often means a follow too. On Instagram he points back to new YouTube videos.
- Ryan: point every channel to another (X to newsletter, YouTube to X).
- Kallaway calls it interoperability: build rails between your surfaces (short video to long video to email to community). Keep your beliefs consistent on every surface, though you don't need all of them on day one.

### From social to email (Nicolas Cole and Matt Gray)

- Nicolas Cole: collect emails from day one with a pinned post, one opt-in for one reader.
- Cole's auto-plug: when a post passes a set number of likes, a reply with your opt-in goes out automatically.
- Cole: 'social to email list to nurture to sell'. Selling straight from a post rarely works.
- Matt Gray's flywheel: X to lead magnets to newsletter to offer. His pinned post moves people to the next platform or the newsletter, and his cover photo states the benefit of following.
- Gray's back end: content to an email landing page to a 5-day email course to a 4-day sales series (transformation, outcomes, price and return, objections).
- EXM7777 for courses and communities: every post that does well gets a freebie that grows the email list. A free checklist or community first, the paid product after.

### Point to one clear next step

- Sam Gaudet (YouTube): recommend one next video. Find 'the next logical problem' (this video took them from 0 to 1, recommend the 1 to 10 video), frame it as the next step on their journey, and point to only one. Several options confuse people.
- Kallaway: give people an owned place to go deeper, like a newsletter, after you've answered their comments and DMs.
- Kallaway's recipe ends with a nurture channel: email or community, next to one short-form and one long-form platform.

**Social to email to sale** (Nicolas Cole)

2. Email list (pinned post, auto-plug)
3. Nurture over email
4. Sell

**The authority flywheel** (Matt Gray)

2. Lead magnets
3. Newsletter
4. Offer

- Building your whole audience on one rented platform (Ryan, EXM7777).
- Waiting until you're big to start an email list (Ryan, Cole).
- Trying to sell straight from a post (Cole).
- Offering several next steps instead of one (Sam).

## 8.13 Patience, reps and not quitting

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Most growth comes late. The creators here say the early months look flat, the work still counts, and the people who keep posting through the quiet stretch are the ones who get the jump.

### Galloway's three growth phases

- Paddy Galloway says a channel moves through establishment, improvements and optimization.
- Establishment: work out what and who you are as a channel, pick your niche or sub-niche, and post one or two videos a week until you have about 30 to 50 videos (about 4 to 5 months). Make what calls to you and what you're passionate about. Some will do okay, some terrible, most nothing at all.

## 8.16 From views to money: Kallaway's attention conversion funnel

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Views only turn into money if four things all work, and they multiply. If any one is zero, the result is zero.

### The four steps that multiply

- Kallaway's attention conversion funnel has four steps. They multiply, so one zero means zero.
- 1) Bullseye signal: within the first lines the viewer thinks 'that's exactly my problem' or 'that's exactly what I want'. People sort every video into solution, entertainment or irrelevant, and only 'solution' buys. Tools: name their pain, the emotion of not solving it, the before and after, a named method.
- 2) Trust bank: every useful post is a deposit (GaryVee's give, give, give, ask). Some proof is worth more than others. The more painful the problem, the less trust they need to act.
- 3) Clear ramp: an off-ramp in every piece: a lead magnet (his favourite, matched to THAT video's topic), a sales page, or a call or DM. Talking about New York for 8 hours and then offering an exit to Tulsa doesn't work.
- 4) Aligned offer: the buyer's math is 'how likely am I to get the result for this price?'. Trust lets you charge more before the 'flip point'. His rule: the buyer should get 10x the price back within 30 days.

### Kallaway's trust ladder, weakest to strongest

- A study, then a stranger's testimonial ('Mark from San Antonio'), then borrowed credibility ('I used Hormozi's framework'), then your own result, then several named client results, then showing your skill live in the content, then a big real audience, then a warm recommendation from someone they trust.
- The warm recommendation is the strongest, which is why shares matter.

### Frameworks

**Attention conversion funnel** (Kallaway)

1. Bullseye signal: they feel 'that's my problem' in the first lines
2. Trust bank: deposits of useful posts plus proof
3. Clear ramp: a lead magnet, sales page or call in every piece
4. Aligned offer: the result is worth far more than the price

### Examples

- **New York and Tulsa:** Talking about New York for 8 hours and then offering an exit to Tulsa.
  <br>*Why it works:* Content and ramp have to point the same way.

### Common mistakes

- Making a great video with no ramp (Kallaway).
- A lead magnet that does not match that video's topic (Kallaway).
- Treating the four steps as add-ons when they multiply (Kallaway).

## 8.18 Content that sells: Matt Gray, Codie and promotion frequency

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Chasing millions of views brings beginners with no money. Targeting a specific, affluent audience gives fewer views and more cash, and you need to ask for the sale often enough.

### Boring content makes the money (Matt Gray)

- The affluence paradox: chasing millions of views attracts beginners with no money. A specific affluent audience means fewer views and much more cash. His lifestyle and beach content got likes. His systems breakdowns got 'I shared this with my whole team in Slack'.
- His filter before posting: 'would someone pay \$10,000 for this information?' If not, it's entertainment.
- Proof-led: his best-earning content is live, unscripted 'whiteboard sessions' helping real founders (for example Ali Abdaal's funnel). 'Details create cash': 'build your personal brand' is forgettable, 'how I grew my X from 0 to 214,000 in 12 months with 3 formats' is proof.
- Max 3 repeatable formats, rotated forever. 'Change the input, not the structure.' A known format also makes word of mouth easy.
- Content like an index fund, not day trading: steady value compounds, viral spikes die.
- His SELL formula: Stories, Educate (how-to or how-I), List (any clear structure).
- Ideas come from a 3-question buyer survey: biggest goal this year? what keeps you up at night? magic wand, what would you want my help with?
- His #1 metric: profit per piece of content.

### Codie Sanchez: collect

- 62% of her event tickets came from organic content and 38% from paid ads. Her newsletter sold 30%, YouTube 27%, X about 1%.
- Product ladder: free content, then \$0 to 50 (guide, template), then \$50 to 500, then \$500 to 5k, then a big ticket. Start with free plus ONE paid step. Add the next only when the one below is full. Ship at 85%.

### Promotion frequency (Hussain)

- Products don't sell themselves. You must deliberately send traffic. More traffic means more sales.
- Two levers: promote more often (no change to offer, strategy or funnel; always remind people you sell something; be clear on what you sell, what to do, how and when to buy) and make more content, which gives more in-between moments for calls to action.
- The odds someone clicks your bio link and buys are tiny.
- The math: at 7 posts a week, one weekly promo is 14% of content. At 90 posts a month (3 a day) with 4 promos, it's about 4%. 'More content = more opportunity to sell (without being annoying).' 'Promote your products like you'd promote a sponsorship.'

### Paid boosts (Kallaway and GaryVee)

- Kallaway: never boost posts from an organic account that works. Paid viewers blur who your audience is, they don't engage with normal posts, and reach drops. Boost the winning creative from a separate ad account.
- GaryVee: post every ad organically FIRST, and only pay to boost the ones that beat your normal views. The two views fit together.

### GaryVee's barbell

- GaryVee says the world is splitting into extreme tech (AI) on one side and "1950s grandpa stuff" on the other: call, text or write to every past client just to say hello, with no ask. His advice is to do both.

## 8.19 Content campaigns and the funnel by audience stage

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Stop thinking one post at a time. Pick one narrative, give it a month, and match each piece to where the audience is.

### Campaigns, not single posts (VibeMarketer)

- Pick one important narrative and develop it for a month across four jobs. Topics can look completely different and still point the same way (a hiring breakdown, an agent tutorial, a 'super IC' article, a case study, a meme about giant GTM teams).
- Consistency doesn't mean repeating one sentence 100 times. It means '100 different pieces of evidence pointing toward the same conclusion'. Then repeat the cycle with new evidence, because most of the audience didn't see it all the first time.
- 'Lightning strike' (from Play Bigger): when you have something worth launching, concentrate activity into a short window so real things land together. If someone meets you four times that week from four sources, they should hear roughly the same story. Not to fake momentum.
- 'Most brands stop repeating a message right around the point the market is finally starting to notice it.'

### Funnel by audience stage (VibeMarketer)

- Not everyone is at the same stage. If someone thinks their process is fine, a 20-minute tutorial won't move them. First show why it's worth changing, then the better version, then how, then why to trust you.
- Top of funnel: awareness. Reach new people and introduce the problem, shift or opportunity.
- Middle: belief and trust. Show thinking, proof, process, results and depth.
- Bottom: commercial connection. The offer, who it's for, results, testimonials and next step.
- Don't copy a fixed 60/30/10 ratio. It depends on what you sell. A broad personal brand needs more top of funnel. A high-ticket offer to a specific ICP needs more middle and bottom.
- Diagnose where the audience gets stuck. Read but don't follow = weak positioning or promise. Follow but never use anything = not practical enough. Use your ideas but don't know what you sell = the bottom of the funnel, the commercial bridge, is missing. 'Figure out the gap first.. then make the next piece.'

### Watch platforms and culture (GaryVee)

- GaryVee's 'PAC': platforms and culture. Trends are clues about what people want. The people who hate trends 'are either lazy or tired'.

### Frameworks

**Funnel by stage** (VibeMarketer)

1. Top: awareness, introduce the problem or shift
2. Middle: belief and trust through thinking, proof and process
3. Bottom: the offer, who it is for, results and next step

## 8.20 Launches and creator collabs (0xfJuan)

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A launch works when the right people understand the product, the team is ready to answer, and there is a plan for what comes next.

### Find the right people

- People who deal with the problem daily, specialists who can explain what it changes for their field, and customers who can show results.
- Read their posts and replies first. Who pays attention to them? What questions come up? Would that audience try the product? A big following helps, but you should be able to say why that audience would care.

### The creator brief

- Here's what the product does. Here's what you can test yourself. This is the part I think your audience would care about. Here's the evidence behind the claim. What's your take after trying it?
- Get on a call if needed. Make paid partnerships clear. Let them use their own words. If everyone posts the same paragraph, you've lost the benefit.

- Split conversations (own replies, quotes, comments under creators' posts). Agree who handles product questions and who handles business opportunities. Answer where people commented.

- Which posts and angles brought the right people? Look past views to trials, relevant DMs and sales calls. Ask prospects where they found you.
- Book follow-ups with creators who brought useful conversations, with something new (a deeper walkthrough, a customer use case, an update answering their first post's questions).
- Give recurring buyer use cases more space in demos and on the landing page.

### Pre-launch checklist

- A clear claim with evidence. Different angles suited to the sharers. Creators who understand the product and its audience. A team ready to answer and follow up. An easy way to try the product or make contact.

## 8.21 Growth phases for a company, and founders with zero distribution

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A company's social growth moves in phases, from founder-led to brand-led. A founder with no audience starts small, with one platform and the actual process.

### Dominic Bair's three phases (all his claims)

- Phase 1 (0 to 1M views a month): founder-led content. Get a face of the brand who can make social-first product videos for X and Instagram (bonus if they can also scale paid UGC). Post 1 to 2 launch videos a week (product updates, simple LinkedIn posts or simple animations). His claim: if this doesn't get you to 1M impressions a month, something has gone terribly wrong. People prefer a personal page over a brand one.
- Phase 2 (1 to 10M): scale winning channels ('stoking fires'). Thresholds from zero: LinkedIn above 10,000 average impressions per post, X 50k+ impressions per week, Instagram a growing baseline of several thousand views a video plus 1 to 2 outliers a month at 100k+.
- Phase 2 hires: a Copywriter (runs LinkedIn and X, finds winning angles on LinkedIn and repurposes them natively for X, scales LinkedIn from 50 to 100k impressions a week to over 200k), a Short Form Editor (at least 10M+ organic short-form views, ideally 50M+, turns proven written content into short form, batches, targets 500k views a week on IG and 250k+ on X) and a Creative Director (elite at launches, on the pulse of culture, expensive but worth it).
- Phase 2 AI workflow: record all the founder's meetings with a notetaker and auto-send raw transcripts (confidential parts removed) to the copywriter. His claim: the single most valuable workflow for scaling written impressions, and it feeds every founder channel. End of phase 2: about 10M views a month, with X carrying much of it.
- Phase 3 (10 to 100M): 'crossing the chasm' from founder-led to brand-led. OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX make you think of Sam, Dario and Elon. Google, Microsoft and Apple make you think of products. It needs resources or deep talent, often both. The team grows from 3 or 4 people to 10 to 20, organized in pods that each obsess over one channel.

### Founders with zero distribution (Ryan)

- Make content about the problem, not your product. Spend at least 60 minutes a day on organic content.
- Post the actual process (what broke, what you learned, what you're testing). Nobody cares about launch updates.
- One platform first (ideally X or YouTube). Add short form after 2 to 3 months by cutting it from your long form.
- Split time 50/50 between building and distributing. Most founders are at 95/5.
- Founder pillars: document WHAT you're doing, HOW it solves a pain point, WHERE people can get it. Share real numbers (revenue, users, churn). Show your face: 'nobody connects with a penguin PFP'.

### Micro versus big creators (Ryan)

- The vehicle to your first \$10k a month isn't the vehicle to \$100k+. Micro creators who only post for their offer stay in a 'hustle loop' of closing sales every day.
- Big creators go broader because top of funnel gives them access (private rounds, advisory roles). His advice: offer content for the sale, plus 'trickle in' broad content to grow.
- Matt Gray's note on the role: Gray calls the social media manager 'one of the most valuable players', essentially the chief distribution officer, distributing content across every platform flawlessly, often 7 days a week. Don't treat it as a simple VA job.

### Frameworks

**Domschats' phases** (Dominic Bair)

1. Phase 1 (0 to 1M): founder-led, 1 to 2 launch videos a week
2. Phase 2 (1 to 10M): scale winning channels, hire a copywriter, short-form editor and creative director
3. Phase 3 (10 to 100M): move to brand-led with channel pods

### Examples

- **Wispr's meeting workflow:** Record all the founder's meetings with a notetaker, auto-send raw transcripts to the copywriter.
  <br>*Why it works:* He says this is the most valuable workflow for scaling written impressions.

### Common mistakes

- Posting launch updates nobody cares about (Ryan).
- Splitting time 95/5 between building and distributing (Ryan).
- Hiding behind a logo with no face (Ryan).
- Staying in a hustle loop of daily sales (Ryan).

## 8.22 Go to market: pick channels by how you sell (EXM7777)

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Go to market comes first. If the offer is fuzzy, channels just spread the fuzzy version faster. How you sell picks the channels.

### Start with the offer

- Write what you sell and what counts as a win (signup, booked call, purchase). If the offer is fuzzy, channels 'just spread the fuzzy version faster'.
- How you sell picks channels: a product that sells itself through a free trial or plan needs content, SEO and a great first experience. A person who closes on a call needs outbound, ads and direct outreach.

### Research, then pick two or three

- Research the competitors buyers compare you with: their pages, prices, offers and repeated claims. Look at posts and ads in the niche with real engagement. Note the doubts and words buyers use in comments.
- Make one note per competitor and one on how you're different. Check that note line by line yourself, because every agent reads it.
- Pick two or three channels where buyers already spend time and forget the rest for now. 'A lazy brief gets you confident work in the wrong direction.'
- Judge every channel on sales and signups, never views.

### Three playbooks

- SaaS: with a free trial, content, SEO and trial emails come first. With calls, outbound first then the page. Show the product doing one job for one user type. Connect payment and product analytics to see who uses and who leaves.
- Ecommerce: UGC and Meta ads first, because people buy what they can see. The store is the source of truth. Do SEO on product pages already showing in Google. Put one trusted revenue number in the brief.

### Landing pages and lead capture

- Keep it small: one page that sells one thing to one kind of buyer, plus one page that sends people to it. An agent writes from the positioning note and collected buyer doubts. Rewrite the headline yourself.
- After signup, speed is everything: the form notifies the system, you get a ping, the lead lands in a CRM or sheet, a welcome email goes right away, and follow-ups take over.
- SaaS free-trial sequence: a personal welcome from the founder, a quick 'need help?' email, three emails each showing one way to use the product, and a last email that changes depending on whether they paid.

### First weeks

- Week 1: folder, interview, brief, GTM research, tools connected with approval rules. Week 2: landing page and email sequence. Week 3: first channel.
- Then add one channel at a time, only when the last passes the test: 'did it do its job this morning without me asking'.

## 8.23 Outbound email, SEO and ads (EXM7777)

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Each of these channels works only after the page and emails convert. Keep it small, track one thing, and keep humans on budget and strategy.

### Outbound email

- Workflow: find and filter prospects, send through a sending tool, run a short sequence, sync replies to a CRM.
- First lines get read when you write right after something happened at the company: they raised money, posted a job for the role you replace, hired a new head of marketing, or their website hasn't changed in ages. The daily job is to find the reason, note it, and write a first line about them.
- Deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC on every sending domain. A real one-click unsubscribe. Warm new inboxes at least 2 weeks. About 30 emails a day per inbox, and grow volume slowly. Stop following up the moment someone replies or bounces. Check Google Postmaster Tools daily and send less if things look bad.
- Legal: every cold email needs your real address and a respected unsubscribe. EXM7777 claims this is US law even for business emails.

### SEO

- Find ONE page where ranking higher would make money. A whole-site audit gives a pile of small problems and no priority.
- Check: can Google find and read it, does it answer what people searched, is it better than what ranks above, and does it push toward buying or signing up. Search now means Google plus AI answer tools that cite sites, and the basics work for both.
- Weekly loop: pull search numbers and sales, compare with last week, suggest ONE change on ONE page, approve, wait a week. It's slow but the only way to know what worked.

### Ads

- Open ads last, after the page and emails prove they convert. Paying for traffic to a page that doesn't sell loses money faster.
- Guardrails: new campaigns are created paused. A daily check flags losers. The agent can pause a loser but never raise a budget. Budget and strategy stay human. Pick ONE source of truth when platforms disagree. No wild promises or fake reviews, because platforms can shut accounts without warning.
- AI UGC compliance: a fake person praising your product can count as a fake review (FTC). Label AI content where platforms ask.

**Weekly SEO loop** (EXM7777)

1. Pull search numbers and sales
2. Compare with last week
3. Suggest ONE change on ONE page
4. Approve and wait a week

- Whole-site SEO audits with no priority.
