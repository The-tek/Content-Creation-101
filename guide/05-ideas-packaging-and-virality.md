## 5.1 What makes an idea go viral

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A viral idea passes three quick tests in the viewer's head (do I care, is it new, am I curious) and it works for three kinds of people at once: your fans, casual viewers and total strangers. Gear and editing have little to do with it.

### The three questions every viewer asks

- Without noticing, every viewer asks three things about your idea. Do I care? Is this new to me? Am I curious to see where it goes?
- If any answer is no, they leave. A topic people care about but have heard a hundred times fails the second question. A brand new topic nobody cares about fails the first.
- When a topic is old, you can make it feel new with a fresh angle. Lesson 5 shows how.

### 4 traits of an idea that goes viral

- 1\. It works for three audiences at once: your core fans, casual viewers and strangers (explained in the next section).
- 2\. It has something new in it. The viewer learns something they haven't heard before, which is why it pays to spend real time brainstorming fresh ideas for your niche.
- 3\. You can explain it in a title and a thumbnail. If you catch yourself saying "I have an amazing idea, I just don't know how to make a thumbnail for it", you don't have an amazing idea yet. You have an interesting thing.
- 4\. People get excited when you mention it. Ask a few friends or people in your niche what they think of the idea. If you don't see excitement, change it.

### The three audiences: core, casual and new

- Core: people who know, like and trust you. They watch your content and buy from you.
- Casual: people who kind of know you. They watch now and then but don't buy yet.
- New: people who have never seen you. This is where growth comes from.
- The test: would the biggest creator in your niche want to watch it, and would that creator's mother? If both say yes, the idea is understandable for everyone.
- How to do it: start wide so anyone can follow, then go deeper later in the piece with tactics only your core audience needs. Understandable for everyone, useful in detail for a few.

### Any niche can do this

- Take woodworking, a niche where you'd think nothing can go viral. A boring video is "reviewing a tool". Videos that get big are "testing woodworking hacks", "99% of woodworkers get this wrong", "this tool is so good it should be illegal", or "the most expensive woodworking tool in the world". Same niche, much stronger framing.
- Worried your niche is too small? Search it on YouTube, filter for long videos and sort by most viewed. The top results show your real ceiling. People who say their niche is too small usually get only about 10% of what the best in that niche get, so the niche is rarely the problem.
- Start in your niche, then expand to the neighbouring niches that overlap with it. Most channels that now get 100,000+ views per video started like this, one niche first and then the one next door. Going broad on day one loses your first fans, and the few who went viral with their very first video are the exception.

### Frameworks

**The three questions**

1. Do I care? The topic is relevant to the viewer.
2. Is it new to me? It has a new angle, finding or frame.
3. Am I curious? It raises a question they want answered.

**Core, casual, new**

1. Core: people who know, like, trust and buy from you.
2. Casual: people who watch sometimes and don't buy yet.
3. New: people who have never seen you.
4. Start wide so all three can follow, then go deeper for your core later.

### Examples

- **A niche topic that anyone clicks:** A lecturer in a very niche field of horticulture (growing plants) got about 12 million views with the title "How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert".
  <br>*Why it works:* The topic is narrow, but the framing is not. The UN is a name everyone knows, and "holding back" a desert is a strong picture. A stranger can click without knowing anything about plants.
- **Meetings are the biggest time waster:** Say a video is called "Meetings are the biggest time waster in any company". Business owners agree with it, employees stuck in pointless meetings share it, and everyone knows what a meeting is. At the end it gives tactics only owners need, like clear goals, one person responsible for each, and a scorecard instead of meetings.
  <br>*Why it works:* One idea serves all three audiences. Owners are the core, people in meetings are casual viewers, and everyone else is new. One creator says a video like this got over 3 million views.

### Common mistakes

- Having an idea you can't put into a title and a thumbnail, and calling it a great idea.
- Making content only your core audience understands. It serves the people you already have and misses growth.
- Going broad before you have a small group of fans.
- Saying your niche is too small without checking the most viewed videos in it.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say you should stop niching down: keep your ideal customer, but cover every problem they have. Others say start narrow and widen later. They agree more than it looks. Both want a niche idea framed so strangers also click. Lesson 16 explains the risk of going too broad.

### Try it

Take your last 5 content ideas. For each one, write one line on how a core fan, a casual viewer and a total stranger would react to the title. Give each idea 1 point for every one of the 4 traits it has. Rewrite the lowest scorer so a stranger would want to click, like the Sahara title does.

## 5.2 Topic is king

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The topic you pick decides more than your hook, editing or camera. Choose it first, and test every topic with three filters: do enough people want it, does it fit your audience, and do you care about it.

### Why the topic comes first

- A saying worth keeping: you can't out-edit a bad topic. Great lighting, gear and editing can't save a subject nobody cares about.
- The order of importance, from most to least: topic, then packaging (the angle, the format, the title and the thumbnail), then everything else like camera, lighting and editing.
- Topic is the 20% that gives you most of the results. If you ignored all other social media advice and only picked the best topics, you'd be ahead of most people.
- This holds for short videos too. An interesting topic is the base of every viral short. One creator says researching your niche before choosing a topic makes ideas "10 times better" (an unverified claim, but the logic is sound).

### Filter 1, demand: how many people care

- Say two videos have the same editing, gear and hook. One is "get abs in 90 days", the other is "build a B2B software company in 90 days". The abs video wins, because far more people want abs than want that business. The size of the audience that could want your topic is called the total market.
- To judge demand, ask three questions: who is my audience, what do they want to see, and what kinds of videos already perform well in my space? Study the big accounts in your niche to answer them.
- Let data pick the topic and bring your own take. Find topics the market already proved it wants, by looking at the best posts of top accounts. Then ask what you believe about that topic that most people get wrong.
- This flips the old way. The old way starts with your own perspective and then looks for a hot topic. The better way starts with a topic you know is hot and pours your stories, examples and opinions into it. The blunt reason: most of the ideas we come up with alone don't interest anyone else.
- Tools that help you find topics: 1of10, topicfinder.ai and VidIQ.

### Filters 2 and 3: fit and interest

- Fit: does my audience care, and does it match what they come to me for? A fitness or makeup video on a branding account would flop, even though those topics have big demand. If you chase only demand, you slowly turn into a fitness account without meaning to.
- Interest: do I genuinely care about this? If not, skip it. Viewers can feel when you don't care, and you end up with a brand you hate.
- Aim narrow, all the way down to your ideal viewer. Broad topics chase views, but they don't build a small audience that is obsessed with you.

### Need-to-have beats nice-to-have

- Build topics around pains your viewer already feels, not around trends. "3 trends in skincare" is nice to have. "If you struggle with acne, try these 3 things" is need to have.
- Pick topics with high demand and low supply. If people want something and nobody has made it well, even a rough video gets views.
- The most common mistake is selfish content: talking about yourself instead of what the viewer gains.
- Evergreen content is content someone can find years later and still find useful, unlike news. Building a library of it pays off over time. Not every piece will go viral, and that is fine.

### Frameworks

**Importance of each part, top to bottom**

1. Topic: the thing that decides most.
2. Packaging: angle, format, title and thumbnail.
3. Delivery: style and tone.
4. Camera, lighting and editing: the least important.

**Three filters for a topic**

1. Demand: do enough people care?
2. Fit: does my audience come to me for this?
3. Interest: do I care enough to keep making it?

### Examples

- **Abs vs a business:** Two videos made the same way: one on getting abs in 90 days, one on building a B2B software company in 90 days. The abs video gets more views.
  <br>*Why it works:* Its demand is bigger. But if you run a business account, abs content fails the fit filter, so demand alone is not enough.
- **Skincare vs acne:** "3 trends in skincare" next to "If you struggle with acne, try these 3 things".
  <br>*Why it works:* The second one speaks to a pain the viewer already has, so it feels like a must-watch. The first is just interesting.

### Common mistakes

- Trying to fix a weak topic with better editing, lighting or gear.
- Starting from your own perspective and hoping the topic turns out to be hot.
- Chasing demand outside what your audience comes to you for.
- Picking topics you don't care about and building a brand you hate.
- Making content about you instead of what the viewer gets.

### Try it

List 10 topics you could make content about. Score each from 1 to 5 on demand, fit and interest. Cut anything that scores 1 on any filter. For your top 3, rewrite each from nice-to-have into need-to-have by naming a pain your viewer already feels.

## 5.3 Where good ideas come from

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Good ideas come from a system, not from staring at a blank page. Collect ideas all day, listen to your customers and comments, and sit down regularly to sort what you collected.

### Build an idea engine

- At the heart of a creator's work there should be a system that produces ideas on a regular basis. Think of it as an engine.
- Here is one way it works. Everything you regularly read, listen to or watch (newsletters, podcasts, videos) is your source. Whenever something makes you think "hmm" or "oh wow", you log it on a board, for example in a Notion page.
- When you feel like writing, pick from the board. You'll often drop a piece halfway, and that is fine. The board never runs dry.

### Catch ideas the moment they show up

- Successful creators share one habit. The moment they notice an idea that is both likely to do well and exciting to them, they drop everything and write it down.
- Likely to do well means: will others like it, can it be said in an engaging way, can it perform?
- Exciting means: do you truly want to understand it or say it your way?
- Ideas tend to arrive on a walk, while listening to an audiobook, while reading a real book or other deep content (not mindless social posts), or in a conversation.
- Think of your brand as a collection of notes from ideas like these.
- There are two reasons people struggle for ideas. Their mind isn't trained to put thoughts into words, and they only consume information they already understand. Read and watch things at the edge of what you know.

### Six types of ideas for every subtopic

- Pain points: what frustrates your audience, what they are doing wrong, what keeps them stuck. This makes people feel seen.
- How-tos: step-by-step processes, methods and frameworks they can try today.
- Lessons you learned: what you know now that you didn't a year ago, mistakes you made, what you'd tell a beginner.
- Why it matters: why they should care at all, what ignoring it costs them, what changes when they get it right.
- Harsh truths: what nobody in your space wants to say. These tend to do very well because they cut through the noise.
- Your opinion on popular ideas: common advice you disagree with, or advice that is wrong or incomplete.

### Get ideas from customers and comments

- Ask real customers about their main problems, obstacles, goals and biggest dreams. Their answers give you hundreds of ideas that solve exactly what they struggle with.
- A simple three-question survey: what's your biggest goal this year? What keeps you up at night? If you had a magic wand, what would you want my help with?
- Also ask which of your content they like most. In one case, customers said they loved watching the creator help other founders live. That became a regular "whiteboard sessions" format.
- One creator says how-to videos convert best into customers, because they teach the exact problem people come with.
- A 4-step routine. First, build a swipe file (a folder of saved examples) of the top 100 posts in your niche, saving the hook, the first 30 seconds and the top comments. Second, copy the hook style, never the content, and pour your own knowledge in. Third, read the comments for questions nobody answered: "but how do I do this with no money?" is your next video. Fourth, keep a problem bank written in your audience's exact words ("I don't have the money and I'm scared I'll look stupid asking", not "capital constraints").
- A good filter: don't ask "what should I post?", ask "what have I earned the right to explain?" Here is a test. Could friends grill you on the topic for 30 minutes at dinner without you preparing? If yes, you can make content about it.

### Get ideas from your own day and your niche's news

- Ideas come from outliers (small accounts getting far more views than normal for them) and from your own day. Everything is an idea. Collect 100, then pick the one that scores best on a viral checklist. If you use AI to help, give it the hook shape you want, like "is it possible to..." or "what happens if...". Without a shape, it hands you flat statements.
- Map the touch points of your day: a question someone asked that you had to explain (that is a post), your personal life, media you consumed (react to it), and thoughts in the shower or at the gym. Aim for 3 to 5 ideas a day. For each, ask: do I have a strong opinion, a story, or a relatable breakdown?
- Hold a content meeting twice a month. First, go through news in your niche: do we like it or not, is it an opinion or a tactic? Second, go through your own history: your camera roll from last week, then 1, 3 and 5 years ago. Third, look at outliers in your topic. Then debate each idea out loud with someone ("would this make a good video? what's the hook?"). Record the meeting and sort ideas into priority, middle and backburner.
- One big creator keeps an eye on platforms and culture. Trends are clues about what people want right now, and people who hate trends are usually either lazy or tired.

### Frameworks

**Six types of ideas**

1. Pain points
2. How-tos
3. Lessons you learned
4. Why it matters
5. Harsh truths
6. Your opinion on popular ideas

**4 steps to content ideas**

1. Build a swipe file of the top 100 posts in your niche: hook, first 30 seconds, top comments.
2. Copy the hook style, never the content.
3. Find questions nobody answered in the comments.
4. Keep a problem bank in your audience's exact words.

**The content meeting**

1. Go through news in your niche: like it or not, opinion or tactic.
2. Go through your own history: camera roll from last week, then 1, 3 and 5 years ago.
3. Look at outliers in your topic.
4. Debate each idea out loud, record it, and sort into priority, middle and backburner.

### Examples

- **Whiteboard sessions:** Customers said their favourite content was watching the creator help other founders live. So the creator started a format where they sit down with well-known founders and bring the audience into the room.
  <br>*Why it works:* People see how the creator helps, then join the paid program. The idea came from asking customers, not from guessing.

### Common mistakes

- Only consuming light social posts instead of books and deep content at the edge of your understanding.
- Writing an idea down later instead of the moment it arrives.
- Describing problems in your words instead of your audience's.
- Asking AI for ideas without giving it a hook shape.

### Try it

Pick one subtopic you know well. Write one idea for each of the six idea types. Then open the comments under a big post in your niche and copy 3 unanswered questions word for word into a problem bank.

## 5.4 What an idea is made of: topic, angle, format

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** An idea is more than a topic. Before you write anything, check three parts: the topic, the angle (your take on it) and the format (how you present it). You can swap each part to turn one topic into many ideas.

### Topic, angle and format

- Check all three before you write a script. A great topic with the wrong angle or format still flops.
- Topic: pick a conversation that already exists. That is easier than starting a new one. To check that people care, look inside your own business (sales calls, coaching calls, team calls, your own past posts) or outside it (other people's posts, Reddit, Quora, Google search trends).
- Angle: your take on the topic. The same topic with the wrong take flops. Choose the angle you agree with most and that you think will resonate with the most people. If you are copying a proven idea, don't change its angle too much.
- Once you have a topic, ask an AI to list about 50 angles, then pick one.
- Format: how you package the idea so the viewer understands it quickly. A ranking video works because the viewer can see where you put each thing.
- A simple rule to get volume: 1 hot topic x 3 angles x 3 formats = 9 videos.

### An idea has 7 parts you can swap

- You can split any idea into 7 parts: topic, angle, hook (what you say, what text shows, what the viewer sees), story structure, visual format, key visuals and audio. Think of them as Lego bricks. You can keep five and change two.
- The angle is your take plus the key facts. "Apple's logo history" and "Apple's 2004 ad campaign" share a topic (Apple) but they are different videos.
- Lesson 8 shows how to use the 7 parts when you take apart a post that did far better than normal.

### Common knowledge, better packaged

- You don't need secret information. You need a better package. Keep one idea per post, and use numbers, results and specifics.
- Top brands say the same 5 or 6 things over and over. You can repeat yourself, as long as each version is packaged better.

### Frameworks

**Topic, angle, format**

1. Topic: a conversation that already exists, checked inside or outside your business.
2. Angle: the take you agree with that will resonate with the most people.
3. Format: how the viewer will understand it fastest.
4. 1 topic x 3 angles x 3 formats = 9 videos.

**The 7 parts of an idea**

1. Topic
2. Angle (your take plus key facts)
3. Hook (spoken, text, visual)
4. Story structure
5. Visual format
6. Key visuals
7. Audio

### Examples

- **One topic, different angles:** The topic is meetings. The angle "Meetings are the biggest time waster in any company" is one that one creator says got over 3 million views. A flat angle like "Are meetings a good use of time?" would probably have gone nowhere.
  <br>*Why it works:* Same topic, different angle, very different result. The strong angle takes a side that people react to.

### Common mistakes

- Writing a script before the topic, angle and format are checked.
- Changing the angle too much when you borrow a proven idea.
- Thinking you need secret information instead of a better package.

### Try it

Pick one topic. Ask an AI for 20 angles on it, then choose the 3 you agree with most that you think would resonate with the widest group. Pair each with 3 formats (a list, a ranking, a story, a how-to). You now have 9 ideas from one topic.

## 5.5 Making old topics feel new

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Almost every topic has been covered. You make an old topic feel new by mixing it with a different field, giving it a fresh name or angle, and taking a side that goes against what people expect. This is called the illusion of novelty: it feels new to the viewer even if the idea is old.

### Mix two worlds that don't usually meet

- Much of creativity is joining two fields that don't normally meet. Spend time outside your niche, in a nearby field or a totally different one. See what works there, then combine it with a method you already know.
- Examples: "dopamine ladders" mixes psychology with storytelling, and "the power of suggestion" mixes psychology with content growth.
- Recycled ideas can get views, but they don't build an audience that is obsessed with you. One creator argues that with AI producing endless similar content, only non-obvious, new and unique ideas will stand out.
- Think of a story lens: everyone covers the same news, and your lens is your angle. When a famous singer was at the Super Bowl, most creators talked about her outfit. One creator covered how she affected the NFL's business and says it got 1 million views, because nobody else was making that video.

### 4 ways to make an old topic new

- A new frame: look at the topic from a different side, like the business angle on a celebrity story.
- A new name: give a known idea a name of its own, like "dopamine ladder". A named idea feels like a discovery. One creator even admits that "illusion of novelty" is old persuasion psychology in a new wrapper, and that this is the point.
- A new update: say what changed recently in an old topic.
- A new finding: share something you tested or discovered yourself.

### How to create the illusion of novelty

- Step 1, a new reveal tied to an outcome. Take an old topic, add a fresh angle, and connect it to something the viewer wants. Example: "drinking water at the right time gives you more energy than your morning coffee".
- Step 2, proof that fits the viewer. The strongest proof is someone exactly like the viewer who got the result. Next best is "a lot of people like you". Weakest is a study or a statistic. It still works, but people wonder "does this apply to someone like me?"
- Step 3, protect the illusion. Never undercut yourself by saying "of course, this has been known for decades". Say it like a secret you are sharing ("not many people know this yet"), not like someone shouting it in the square.
- Two more optional steps are contrast (a true opposite of what the viewer believes) and urgency, but only if the urgency is real.

### The contrarian formula

- A contrarian belief is something you believe that goes against what most people in your niche say. Write down 3 to 5 of them first.
- Then use this routine: pick a subtopic in your niche, state the common belief, introduce your contrarian frame (what everyone gets wrong), and explain why your frame is right. Repeat with the next subtopic. One creator says this is all they do in every video and why they grew faster.
- A strong belief you hold does more for a big brand than titles, thumbnails or hooks.
- Harsh truths and your opinion on popular ideas (lesson 3) are the same move in the form of an idea.
- Here is how one creator started. They took the very common topic "how to get more views and followers" and added a take nobody else was saying, a method to pick topics that will perform.

### Frameworks

**Creating the illusion of novelty**

1. New reveal plus outcome: an old topic with a new angle, tied to what they want.
2. Contrast: a true opposite of what they believe.
3. Urgency, only if it is real.
4. Proof that fits: someone like them got the result.
5. Protect it: don't undercut yourself, say it like a secret.

**The contrarian formula**

1. Pick a subtopic.
2. State the common belief.
3. Introduce your contrarian frame.
4. Explain why your frame is right.
5. Repeat with the next subtopic.

### Examples

- **The Super Bowl lens:** A famous singer attends the Super Bowl. Most creators cover her outfit. One covers what her presence did for the NFL's business.
  <br>*Why it works:* The same news with a different lens made that creator a category of one. They say it earned 1 million views.
- **Naming an old idea:** A creator invented the name "illusion of novelty" for old persuasion psychology, then told viewers this at the end of a video.
  <br>*Why it works:* A name makes an old idea feel new, and the creator proved it works on the viewer.

### Common mistakes

- Recycling the same ideas as everyone else, which can get views but not a loyal audience.
- Using a random comparison instead of a true opposite when you contrast.
- Fake urgency, which kills trust.
- Undercutting your own idea by saying it's old news.

### Try it

Write down one common belief in your niche. Then write your contrarian frame and three reasons it's right. Finally, give the idea a name of 2 to 4 words, the way "dopamine ladder" was named.

## 5.6 Copy the format, not the content

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** The best creators learn from what already works. Borrow the format, the topic or the hook shape, never the words or someone else's point of view, then fill it with your own stories.

### Learn from others before you invent

- Musicians play other people's songs before they write their own. Don't try to write your own song too early.
- Look at what works for other people, what worked for you before, and what's trending on Reddit and X. Find which topics, formats and angles perform.
- Take inspiration from the topic, the angle and the format. Never take the words or the other person's perspective. Then add your own stories and experience so it becomes yours and not a remake.
- Study how big creators grew, not where they are today. Look at what they posted 2 to 4 years ago, while they were blowing up, not what they post now.
- If you were starting over, list 5 to 10 creators making the kind of content you want, study their top content and how they grew, and plan to repeat it with your own story, ideas and experience. Also look at what products they sell. Every great artist has references.
- Ideas that make people grow are already out there. Find them, make them your own, and test until growth is steady.

### Copy the format

- A format is the shape of a post: a ranking, a list, a before and after, a thread, a carousel. Look at formats in neighbouring niches and bring them into yours.
- The biggest lever is to copy the viral format of the moment, never the content, and bend it to your niche. Platforms keep changing what wins: Quora answers, then Medium articles, then Twitter threads, then LinkedIn carousels, now X articles. If a format keeps showing up in your feed, it is there for a reason.
- Study outside your niche too: info products, online shops, huge personal brands and luxury brands. Format matters more than niche, so a productivity creator can learn from fitness videos that use the same format.
- One company built a machine that makes about 6,000 videos a month on formats proven to go viral again and again, including some the team invented. The creators just had to carry them out well.

### Scroll like a creator, read like a researcher

- Scroll as a creator, not just a viewer. When a video hooks you, ask why it did, who it was made for, and read the comments. Then bring the idea into your niche.
- Keep a swipe file (a folder of saved examples) of what stops you from scrolling, and work out why. A good bar: save the top 20 posts you couldn't help saving, and hold your own posts to that standard.
- Read the internet as a researcher. You are collecting ideas, not just being entertained.
- If you ask an AI for a viral post and give it nothing else, you get the average of everything it has read, which is boring. Flip the order. First collect what's working on each platform now, then write from that.
- A weekly loop: find what's working in your niche this week, pull the best accounts and posts in full, turn each video into text, spot patterns that fit what you sell, write the batch with the source posts attached, and wait for your approval.
- A text version of a video misses the opening image, the pacing and what is shown as proof, so look at the video itself too.
- When you save a video, break it down with five questions: why did it work, how does the hook grab in the first seconds, how is the pacing, what is the sound (which song), and how can I adapt it to my brand and audience?

### Ads and competitor research

- The Meta Ad Library is a free public tool that shows the ads any company is running. Ask it a specific question, like "which hooks do the best story ads in my niche use this month?", not a vague one like "what are my competitors doing?". Copy the skeleton of an ad, never the script.
- For winning ads, note what they say more than how they look: the hook, the order of beats (hook, problem, how it works, proof, call to action), the type of person on camera and the product angle.
- A similar method works on YouTube: collect thumbnails, transcripts and titles from a list of competitors, check daily which topics are trending, and turn every video into a note (title, thumbnail, transcript, hook, structure). Your scripts then draw on a growing pile of real examples.

### Borrow what is already proven

- One creator opens a video with a clip that already went viral, then teaches something about it. The hook has already proved it grabs people.
- A small account can ride on something bigger. One way is to show a brand or celebrity people already know and build on their popularity. Another is to use clips that already did well with a small re-edit.
- One creator says a brand new account with zero followers got 8.2 million views on its first post, a slight re-edit of a clip that had already done well.

### Warnings

- Don't be a copycat. Never copy someone one for one, and don't react to everything other creators do.
- Just because something went viral doesn't make it true.
- If everyone copies the same motion, colours and contrast, the feed gets numb to it and it stops working. Build your own look.

### Frameworks

**Break down a saved video**

1. Why did it work?
2. How does the hook grab in the first seconds?
3. How is the pacing?
4. What is the sound?
5. How can I adapt it to my brand and audience?

**Weekly research loop**

1. Find what's working in your niche this week.
2. Pull the best accounts and posts in full.
3. Turn every video into text, and also watch the video itself.
4. Spot patterns that fit what you sell.
5. Write the batch with the sources attached.
6. Wait for approval.

### Examples

- **Opening with a clip that already went viral:** A creator opens with a clip that millions already watched, then teaches the lesson behind it.
  <br>*Why it works:* The hook is already proven. The creator borrows its pull and adds their own teaching.
- **Adding your own take to a common topic:** One creator took the very common topic "how to get more views and followers" and added a method for picking topics that will perform.
  <br>*Why it works:* The topic was proven. The take was new, because nobody else was saying it.

### Common mistakes

- Copying the words or someone else's point of view instead of the format.
- Copying what big creators post today instead of what they posted while growing.
- Asking AI for viral content with no real examples attached.
- Studying only a text version of a video and missing the visuals and pacing.
- Assuming something is true because it went viral.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say originality is overrated, so learn from others first and change it later. Others say far too many people are copycats, and warn against reacting to every move other creators make. They meet in the middle: borrow the format or topic, never copy one for one, and add your own take.

### Try it

Save 5 posts or videos that stopped you from scrolling this week, at least 2 from outside your niche. Break each one down with the five questions (why it worked, the hook, the pacing, the sound, how to make it yours), then write one idea for your own niche from the best one.

## 5.7 Finding outliers

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** An outlier is a post or video that did several times better than that account normally does. Studying outliers shows you which ideas people want right now, so you don't have to guess. Creators disagree on the exact cut-off, but all judge a post against its own account's normal.

### What counts as an outlier

- The rule of thumb is a post that gets at least 3 to 4 times that account's average views. Say a channel's videos get about 100 views each and then one gets 100,000. That didn't go viral by accident. Something about its title, thumbnail or idea was special, and you can learn from it. A tool called ViewStats focuses on videos that did 10 times the channel's average.
- On a text platform like X, a good sign is a post with at least 2 times the engagement (likes, replies, shares) of that creator's other posts.
- Another approach skips ratios and only studies hooks from videos with 50,000+ likes, and only in your own format (talking head, faceless, list).
- A stricter filter: divide a video's views by the channel's average (100,000 views on a channel that averages 10,000 is a 10x outlier). Look for 5x or more, 5% or more engagement (this screens out paid brand deals), and posts from the last 7 to 14 days for news topics or the last 3 to 12 months for evergreen topics.
- Only look at the last 6 months, because what people want changes. Prefer smaller accounts. A channel with 10,000 to 100,000 subscribers that has one video with 250,000+ views tells you more than a million views on a million-subscriber channel, where everything gets a lot of views.

### What outliers are made of

- Outliers are rarely about colour grading or a camera upgrade. Those help, but they don't make 10x differences. The cause is usually the idea, the title or the thumbnail.
- Here is how to think about one. A podcast interview with a very young creator got close to 3 million views and a high click rate in the first 24 hours. That is probably less about design details like text placement and more about the topic and the guest: a very young girl who figured out something hard, with "I can make anything viral" as the thumbnail text. The next step is to brainstorm similar subjects, like teenagers making real money from content.
- The reverse also teaches. A video built around an abstract idea (a "generative world model") got 100,000 views or less, while a video about concrete things you can see got millions. Lesson 9 covers why.

### Searching at scale

- Mine other niches. Filter for videos with at least 3 times the average of that channel's last 5 videos, at least 100,000 views, posted in the last year. Then search an unrelated topic and adapt what you find. Example: "I tested 100 years of kids' toys" becomes "I tested 100 years of productivity hacks" or "100 years of morning routines".
- Filter by outlier size. For example: videos over 1 million views, on channels with over 100,000 subscribers, that did at least 10 times the channel average in the last 90 days. A video's rank among that channel's last 10 videos also tells you how well it did for them.
- Track a title pattern. If you plan a video called "I built the world's largest treehouse", set an alert for any long video with over 1 million views that has "I built" in the title.
- Judge long videos and short videos separately. A billion monthly views from Shorts means something very different from a billion views from long videos.
- Reverse-search a viral thumbnail to see whether the concept came from another niche where it already went viral.
- Make a research account: a new Google account that subscribes to every competitor and watches their videos, so its home feed fills with ideas from your niche. Don't copy. Take inspiration from the topics, then write your own title and thumbnail.

### Studying outliers on text platforms: the anomaly exercise

- An anomaly is a post that did far better than that person's normal. This exercise teaches you to write posts that stop the scroll.
- 1\. Write down 5 to 10 writers or creators you admire for how they say things.
- 2\. Spend 1 hour a day for a week scrolling their accounts.
- 3\. Find their anomalies: posts with at least 2 times the engagement of their other posts. Screenshot or save them.
- 4\. Collect 20 to 30 posts that show the way of writing you want.
- 5\. Under each one, write 3 to 5 bullets on why it worked: the psychology, how it grabbed attention, the value, why people care.
- 6\. Optional: paste the post into an AI and ask it to break down why it worked, its structure, and how to repeat it step by step with your own ideas.
- Successful post structures work like training wheels for how you say things. When you write in a way that stops the scroll, growth follows. You don't need to grow by thousands, you just need to keep growing. No growth means no traffic, and no traffic means no income.

### Frameworks

**The anomaly exercise**

1. List 5 to 10 creators you admire.
2. Spend 1 hour a day for a week on their accounts.
3. Save posts with 2 times their normal engagement.
4. Collect 20 to 30.
5. Write 3 to 5 bullets on why each worked.
6. Optional: ask an AI to break down the structure and how to repeat it.

**Mining outliers in other niches**

1. Find videos with 3 times the average of their channel's last 5 videos.
2. Keep only those with 100,000+ views from the last year.
3. Search an unrelated topic.
4. Adapt the concept to your niche.

### Examples

- **From toys to productivity:** "I tested 100 years of kids' toys" becomes "I tested 100 years of productivity hacks" or "100 years of morning routines".
  <br>*Why it works:* A format that already worked in one niche, adapted to another.

### Common mistakes

- Studying average posts instead of outliers.
- Blaming outliers on a better camera or colour grading.
- Treating a big channel's views as a signal when everything they post does well.
- Mixing short and long video views when judging a channel.
- Using old outliers after demand has moved. Stick to the last 6 months.

### Where the sources disagree

Creators draw the line in different places: 2 times the engagement, 2 to 4 times the average, 3 to 4 times as a minimum, 5 times plus 5% engagement, or 10 times. Another approach skips ratios and uses 50,000+ likes. All of them agree you judge a post against its own account's normal.

### Try it

Pick 5 accounts in or near your niche. For each, note their typical views or likes, then find any post from the last 6 months with at least 2 times that number. Save the top 5 outliers, each with one line on what you think made it special: the idea, the title, or the thumbnail or first line.

## 5.8 Breaking down outliers

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Finding an outlier is half the job. The other half is working out why it won and what you can reuse. Ask a few questions about it, split it into its 7 parts, keep the parts that keep winning and change the rest.

### Questions to ask about an outlier

- Screenshot any successful video into a doc or Notion page and write answers to three questions. What made it unique: the topic, the thumbnail? What about its packaging made it an outlier: was the title or thumbnail different from the rest of the space, or was it a different format? Why did this one go viral when similar ones didn't?
- Watch out for survivorship bias. You only see the winners, and the creator may have tried the same video eight other times before it worked.
- Put videos side by side. If others covered this topic and got 10,000 views and this one got 150,000, ask why. Also look at timing and how the views grew over time.
- Don't dismiss an outlier in three seconds with "they have more money" or "they're bigger". Write down what you think made it work.

### Keep 2 parts, change 5

- Step 1: find outliers (lesson 7). Step 2: split each into its 7 parts (topic, angle, hook, story structure, visual format, key visuals, audio) and look for patterns across many winners.
- Step 3: keep 2 parts that keep winning and change the other 5. It's like redrawing cards in poker: you hold the good ones and swap the rest.
- Study the same type of content. Short video should study short video, on any platform. A tweet's topic can inspire a video, but its hook and structure won't carry over.
- A simple routine: keep one doc with two lists. "Daily inspiration" holds topics, angles and visuals worth borrowing. "Long-term patterns" holds hooks, structures and formats that keep showing up over weeks.

### Learn from other people's tests

- When you read how others tested their titles and thumbnails, you save yourself from running hundreds of tests of your own.
- Here is an example. A travel YouTuber made a video called "I tried every seat on the most expensive airline" and tested three thumbnails: one showing the creator in a bed with "24/7 service", one showing a "sleep pod \$54", and one splitting the picture between economy and first class. The split economy versus first class thumbnail won. MrBeast had made a similar video a year earlier where a split thumbnail also won, so the YouTuber could have studied it first.

### Frameworks

**Questions for any outlier**

1. What made it unique?
2. What about its packaging made it an outlier?
3. Why did this one go viral when similar ones didn't?
4. Compare side by side, and check timing and how views grew.
5. Write down your answer instead of dismissing it.

**Keep 2, change 5**

1. Find outliers (5 times the average or more).
2. Split each into 7 parts.
3. Find patterns across many winners.
4. Keep the parts that keep winning and change the rest.

### Examples

- **The airline video thumbnails:** A travel YouTuber tested three thumbnails for one video. The one split between economy and first class won, the same result MrBeast had with a similar video a year earlier.
  <br>*Why it works:* Checking another creator's test result first can save you from running the test yourself.

### Common mistakes

- Dismissing a competitor's outlier as luck or budget.
- Ignoring survivorship bias.
- Borrowing a hook or structure from a different type of content.
- Changing every part at once instead of keeping the ones that win.

### Try it

Take 3 outliers from the last lesson. Fill in the 7 parts for each in a table. Circle any part that repeats in at least 2 of them. Plan one piece that keeps those 2 parts and changes the other 5.

## 5.9 Choosing which ideas to make

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The best creators aren't more creative, they kill bad ideas faster. Collect many ideas, cut most of them with a few simple tests, and make only the best few. Check early that the idea has something clear to show in the first seconds.

### Kill bad ideas faster

- Many people find one outlier and remake it. Better to find 20 to 30 topics and kill 25 of them.
- If you work with a team, hold an ideation meeting once a week for each platform, with 3 to 4 people. Nobody brainstorms during the meeting. Everyone brings 3 to 5 ideas they found during the week.
- Think in abundance. Present 12 YouTube ideas and make 4. The other 8 weren't wasted, because you picked the best of the best.
- Working alone, find 20 ideas in one session, then in a second session kill as many as you can.
- Pitch every idea in 3 parts: topic (AI tools), angle (these AI tools will make you money), format (a tier list ranked by how much money each makes you).
- Keep coming up with ideas separate from making them. A MrBeast employee once asked whether the person who thinks of ideas also makes them. When the answer was yes, the reply was "don't do that", because you'll pick the easiest idea to make and not the one that performs best. Split it by person, or by time: ideas from this week get made two weeks from now.

### The idea funnel

- Start with 100 ideas. Cut the ones that don't work for your core fans, casual viewers and strangers (see lesson 1). About 40 are left. Cut the ones you can't realistically make.
- Ask: can I write the title and sketch the thumbnail? About 20 are left. Ask: am I excited to make it? About 10 are left.
- For each of those, write a one-page plan: a rough thumbnail, a title, 1 to 3 points on why it will work, and a one-line summary of the video. Ask other people what they think. Then make the best 3 or 4.
- Collect ideas from three places: your own past results (what worked for you), what worked for others, and new things nobody has tried.
- Put a time limit on overthinking. Spend an hour on a title, not a week. There are always some views left on the table, but time passing costs more.

### Pick the idea by what the viewer will see

- When an idea arrives, first ask what you will show in the first 3 to 5 seconds. If there is no good visual, reconsider the idea or create one with motion graphics or stock footage.
- Concrete beats abstract. Videos called "future of storytelling" and "generative world model" did badly. A video about life-size floor plans, where you can walk through your exact home design before you build it, did well, because the thing was right there on screen. If your footage can't explain the idea, drop it. There are always more ideas.
- Among the facts or angles of a story, choose the one with the biggest contrast, because it decides the hook. Something people have never seen has contrast built in just by existing.
- Even educational posts need a picture. A classic example: an expert fills a jar with golf balls, then rocks, then sand, and it all fits. In the other order it doesn't. So do the big tasks first. In text, a concrete image beats an abstract claim.

### Final checks before you commit

- Run every piece through five questions. Is it relevant to your ideal viewer (narrow enough that it feels like you read their mind)? Is it a small effort for a big reward? Is it non-obvious? Is it proven? Can you actually do it?
- Do people in your niche get excited when you mention it?
- Is it both likely to perform and something you're genuinely excited to say your way?

### Frameworks

**The idea funnel**

1. Start with 100 ideas.
2. Cut those that don't work for fans, casual viewers and strangers (about 40 left), then cut those you can't make.
3. Can I write the title and sketch the thumbnail? (about 20 left)
4. Am I excited to make it? (about 10 left)
5. Write a one-page plan for each: thumbnail, title, why it works, one-line summary.
6. Ask others, then make the best 3 or 4.

**5-point idea checklist**

1. Relevant to the ideal viewer.
2. Small effort, big reward.
3. Non-obvious.
4. Proven.
5. Actually doable.

### Examples

- **Floor plans vs world models:** A video about an abstract "generative world model" got 100,000 views or less. A video about "life-size floor plans" that let you walk through your home design before building it got millions.
  <br>*Why it works:* The floor plan idea had something concrete to show in the first seconds. The abstract one didn't.

### Common mistakes

- Remaking one outlier instead of finding 20 to 30 topics and killing most.
- Letting the person who makes the content also choose the ideas, so the easiest ones win.
- Picking abstract ideas with nothing to show in the first seconds.
- Spending a week on a title instead of an hour.

### Try it

Write 20 ideas in 10 minutes. Then, in a separate session, run them through the funnel: cut any that don't work for fans, casual viewers and strangers, cut any you can't make, cut any you can't title and picture, and cut any you aren't excited about. Write a one-page plan for the last 3.

## 5.10 Why packaging decides the result

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Packaging is how an idea looks before anyone watches or reads it: the title, the thumbnail and the first lines. The same idea can get 5 to 10 times more views with better packaging, so it deserves far more of your time than most people give it.

### What packaging is

- If they don't click, they don't watch. That is why packaging matters so much.
- On YouTube, packaging is the title, the thumbnail and the first 30 to 60 seconds. On short videos it is the first caption line, the thumbnail and the hook in the first 3 to 5 seconds.
- Packaging is a skill of its own, separate from writing. Good packaging makes people curious, promises a clear payoff, is specific, and fits the platform. It is usually a picture (the thumbnail) plus words (the title).
- Formatting is part of the hook. The shape of a post decides whether people start reading. The goal is to reduce friction. If someone has to study a complex graphic to get any value, they scroll on.
- The first step of any piece is the click. It has three parts: the topic, the title and the thumbnail.

### Same idea, very different results

- A weaker content creator can have an idea flop, while a stronger one can take that exact same idea, package it differently and get 5 to 10 times the results. For example, one gets 10,000 views and the other gets 100,000 from the same idea.
- Often you don't have a content problem, you have a packaging problem. One team's old YouTube videos had great information but unclear packaging, and results improved once they changed it.
- If you use AI to write, its default first line is almost always weak. Rewrite the first 2 sentences yourself.

### Small gains, huge results

- A title that is 20% better can mean double, triple or even 100 times more views. YouTube works like a snowball: being a little better with a very large number of people compounds.
- One creator estimates that with a title only 10 to 20% worse, a drone-vs-race-car video from Red Bull might have gotten 2 to 3 million views instead of over 20 million. These are guesses, since nobody can know the exact numbers, but the point is that small packaging gains have an outsized effect.
- Sometimes a client prefers a weaker title because it feels more on brand. A good answer is to ask: if your title gets 1 million views and mine gets 10 million, would you still feel the same?
- At the hook level, one or two words can be the difference between 100,000 and 1 million views.

### How much time packaging deserves

- Aim to spend about half your time on the content and half on packaging. Most people spend 95% on content and 5% on packaging.
- Most of the result comes from three things: the hook, the packaging and the value. Top creators obsess over the hook and packaging more than the content itself, and they spend far more time planning before they record than beginners do.
- A good principle: don't put 5% of your time into something that is 50% of the game. In one creator's opinion, the idea, title and thumbnail make up at least 50% of a video's performance, while most creators spend about 5% of their time on them. The exact split is debatable, but the direction is clear.
- Scripting, filming and editing will always take the most time. But time spent on the idea and packaging often pays back much more. Some channels make 10 or more thumbnails per video, sometimes 50 or more, and one team comes up with as many as 500 ideas to find one great one. If you do nothing else, start by giving packaging more than 5%.

### How people actually choose

- On YouTube, people glance at the thumbnail (they only see colour and motion), read the title (the shorter it is, the more of it they understand), look back at the thumbnail to understand it, and then click.
- People have a personal trust line. They only click packaging that sits above it. The more painful their problem, the lower the line. Sloppy design pulls the trust level down.
- Your intro must confirm the click: repeat the title's promise in the first seconds.
- On text platforms, the first line of a caption is extra hook space. Write it like a title.

### Frameworks

**What great packaging does**

1. It makes people curious.
2. It promises a clear payoff.
3. It is specific.
4. It fits the platform.

**How a viewer picks a YouTube video**

1. They glance at the thumbnail for colour and motion.
2. They read the title.
3. They look back at the thumbnail to understand it.
4. They click if it is above their trust line.
5. The intro confirms the click.

### Examples

- **The drone vs race car video:** A video called "World's fastest camera drone vs F1 car" got over 20 million views, according to the creator who made it. A title 10 to 20% worse might have gotten 2 to 3 million.
  <br>*Why it works:* Packaging gains are not linear. A small improvement can multiply the result.

### Common mistakes

- Spending 95% of your time on content and 5% on packaging.
- Choosing a weaker title because it feels more on brand.
- Keeping the AI's default first line.
- Posts that look hard to read.
- An intro that doesn't confirm the title's promise.

### Try it

Take one post or video that did badly. Without changing the content, write 5 new titles or first lines and 2 new visual ideas. Pick the strongest using four tests: does it make people curious, does it promise a clear payoff, is it specific, and does it fit the platform?

## 5.11 Thumbnails: simple, clear, one click

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A thumbnail has one job: to get the click. Top creators agree on how. Show fewer things, show a clear emotion, and make sure it can be understood in a split second.

### Keep it simple

- Don't get caught up in design. Show something simple. The only job of a thumbnail is to get the click, and the less it shows, the better.
- Run the glance test. Put the thumbnail on a slide in Google Slides, with a blank white slide before and after it. Keep it small and flick onto it quickly. Can you understand it in a split second? Many creators scroll their own feed at top speed, then make packaging that needs 5 seconds to read, which is probably 10 times more time than viewers will give it.
- Use no more than three things to look at, ideally one to three. A face, another face, an object, text, a logo and more text is too much.
- Beginners fill all the space. Many of the most viral thumbnails have empty space. One example is just an arrow, a saw and a face.
- Simple plus a slight exaggeration, without clickbait, works well. For example, putting gold on the world's most expensive saw is exaggerated a little, but it doesn't mislead.
- Study the thumbnails in your own niche, because culture matters. For example, audiences in countries like Singapore and Malaysia more often click thumbnails with a lot more text than audiences in the West.
- Useful limits: titles under 60 characters (longer only when it really needs it), thumbnail text under 5 words, and 1 to 3 things to look at. Then check how the parts work together, for example text that blends into the background. If it fails the test, keep simplifying.

### A thumbnail recipe

- One creator studied their 6 best videos of the last year and found the same pattern in all of them: three elements.
- Element 1: a big face with a clear emotion. People are drawn to eyes, and a face builds recognition.
- The emotion must match the title. A title like "Stop wasting your life" needs a person who clearly looks upset. A big smile on a negative title feels disconnected.
- Element 2: short text of 3 to 5 words. People scan, they don't read. In that study, the 4 best performers all had exactly 4 words.
- Element 3 (optional): one picture that backs up the idea, such as money flying around.
- Never repeat the title on the thumbnail. You get two chances to make the viewer feel something, so don't waste one. For the title "here's how to grow a business so fast it feels like cheating", the thumbnail text could say "faster than 99% of people" instead of repeating the title.

### Stand out, then check dark mode

- Do the opposite of your niche. If everyone's thumbnails are dark and busy, go light and minimal, and the other way round. The brain notices differences in colour and brightness before it reads anything.
- Check your thumbnail in dark mode. One creator says over 80% of YouTube viewers use it.

### What makes a famous MrBeast thumbnail great

- Take his island thumbnail as a case study. It does 5 things.
- 1\. The person is big. He fills about a third of the frame, because he is the most recognisable part of the brand. He has an excited, open-mouth smile and bright eyes.
- 2\. Colour and contrast. Red headphones sit against a light blue and green island. The colours are strong but not overdone, so it still looks real.
- 3\. Objects that show scale, such as a boat and a volcano.
- 4\. Small details that add luxury and a subtle brand: a roller coaster, a burger building and a giant gold statue. A parachuting figure adds scale and curiosity.
- 5\. It is simple. Even zoomed out, you can still see what is going on. Thumbnails should be instantly understandable at any size.

### Finding and testing thumbnail ideas

- MrBeast looks for inspiration by describing a scene in words, for example "a person standing next to a tractor", to see how others framed the camera to make the object look big. Then he searches for similar thumbnails.
- YouTube lets you test several titles and thumbnails against each other (an A/B/C test). Use it to check which one gets more clicks.
- The click rate (CTR) is fickle. The more people see a video, the lower its CTR usually drops, so the lifetime CTR isn't very useful. But clicking is extremely important. "CTR is useless" does not mean thumbnails don't matter.
- On many established channels, the CTR in the first hour and first 24 hours strongly predicts long-term performance.

### The opening of the same video

- The first seconds of MrBeast's island video work on the same ideas. He shows the scale right away, by zooming out to reveal the whole island or showing 100 people in a line.
- The first challenge arrives by second 17.
- He deliberately uploads in 1080p so the video never feels too polished.

### Frameworks

**Glance test**

1. Put the thumbnail small between two blank slides.
2. Flick onto it. Can you understand it in a split second?
3. Keep it to one to three things to look at.
4. Leave empty space.
5. Exaggerate a little, never mislead.

**The thumbnail recipe**

1. A big face with a clear emotion.
2. The emotion matches the title.
3. 3 to 5 words of text.
4. Optional: one picture that backs up the idea.
5. Never repeat the title.

### Examples

- **The saw thumbnail:** An arrow, a saw and a face, with gold on the world's most expensive saw. The rest of the thumbnail is empty.
  <br>*Why it works:* Three things to look at, a small exaggeration, and no lie.
- **Emotion that matches the title:** A title like "Stop wasting your life" with the creator clearly upset and about 4 words of text.
  <br>*Why it works:* The emotion matches the title, the text is short, and the title isn't repeated.

### Common mistakes

- Filling every inch of the thumbnail with a face, another face, an object, text and a logo.
- A big smile on a negative title.
- Repeating the title word for word on the thumbnail.
- Getting caught up in design instead of one clear thing.
- Judging a thumbnail by its lifetime CTR alone.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say to keep text to 3 to 5 words because people scan and don't read. Others point out that in places like Singapore and Malaysia, thumbnails with more text often get more clicks. Both say the same thing in the end: study the thumbnails in your own niche first.

### Try it

Take one thumbnail and run the glance test: put it small between two blank slides and flick onto it. If you can't say what it is in a split second, cut things until you can.

## 5.12 Titles that get the click

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** A good title makes one clear promise about something people want. Be clear before you are clever, use a famous name or a strong verb, and use four emotions (hope, dream, fear, blocker) to package any topic in different ways.

### Framing a title

- "World's fastest camera drone vs F1 car" is a title that got over 20 million views, by the claim of the creator who made it. It is simple, straight to the point, and you understand it instantly.
- A title format that has worked in many niches: "If I had to start [X] over again, here's what I'd do".
- The same topic can be packaged differently. "Here's how to grow a business" is plain. "Here's how to grow a business so fast it feels like cheating" is stronger, because it adds a feeling and a promise.

### Clear, not clever

- Ask yourself: what is the smallest promise I can make that still gets people interested? Put that in the title and thumbnail.
- The test: if a stranger can't say what the video is about within 3 seconds of seeing the thumbnail, it is too confusing. People scroll, they don't wait for their brain to catch up.
- Many creators repeat the rule to be clear, not clever.

### One topic, four packages: hopes, dreams, fears, blockers

- The same topic, say "how to make money", can be packaged four ways, depending on the emotion you aim at.
- Hope (a short-term win): "how to make a million dollars in 2026".
- Dream (a long-term life): "if I wanted to achieve financial freedom in the next 12 months, I'd do this".
- Fear (what they dread): "you'll never become rich until you understand this".
- Blocker (what they think is stopping them): "how to get rich without talent, luck, or a trust fund".
- Learn this method instead of copying 100 titles. It teaches you how to fish.

### Title formats that worked for long videos

- These come from a study of titles that did far better than their channel's normal. Here are the shapes, with an example of each.
- "How to [big outcome] (Complete 2026 Masterclass / Full Guide / step-by-step roadmap)", for example "How to Grow a Massive Brand (Full 2026 Masterclass)".
- "Full Course: How to turn [what you have] into [money]".
- "[Topic] 101: A Guide to [big outcome]", for example "Distribution 101: A Guide to Reaching Millions".
- "How to become dangerously good at [skill] (use these 3 strategies)".
- A deadline claim: "Everyone doing [X] has [time] to [do Y]".
- "[Tool] as Your [Job Title] (FULL GUIDE)".
- "How to [skill] like the top 0.01%".
- Proof in the title: "How we [big number] and made [result]".
- "The Psychology of a [X]", for example "The Psychology of a Premium Personal Brand".
- The winners have four things in common. They promise a big outcome everyone wants (money, freedom, being known) instead of a narrow skill. They use brackets to promise the complete thing (masterclass, full course, 3 strategies). They use fear of AI or of losing a job as the reason to watch now, with a calm system as the answer. And they show proof, like numbers or screenshots, before the teaching.

### What one channel's own titles teach

- This is a small sample from one creator, so treat it as a hint and not a rule.
- "How to Become Disgustingly Good at Content Creation" did best. It promises an outcome everyone wants, with a strong, odd word.
- "How to Become a Better Storyteller" is a plain, clear promise of a skill.
- "How to Build a Personal Brand (Starting From Zero)" has a clear outcome, plus brackets that call out beginners.
- "If I Wanted to Get 10M Followers (with no time), I'd Do This" is an "if I wanted X" plan, with a big number and brackets that remove the objection.
- "50 Content Hacks I Know at 10M Followers I Wish I Knew at 0" has a number, proof and a "wish I knew" hook.
- "I'm 25. If you want to get rich in your 20s, watch this" uses age as a signal and calls out a group.
- In this sample, the "so fast it feels like cheating" outcome and "the most valuable video you'll ever watch" did best. "Full Guide" in brackets also did well. Numbered lists and "new era" framing did worst.
- The creator ends every video with the same line: "watch this video next if you want to grow on social media so fast it kind of feels like cheating". It reuses the strongest title idea as a call to action.

### Frameworks

**Hope, dream, fear, blocker**

1. Hope: the short-term win they want.
2. Dream: the long-term life they want.
3. Fear: what they dread if nothing changes.
4. Blocker: what they think is stopping them.
5. Write one title for each, then keep the strongest.

**The smallest clear promise**

1. Find the smallest clear promise the video can make.
2. Put it in the title and thumbnail.
3. Check: can a stranger say what it is about in 3 seconds?

### Examples

- **One topic, four ways:** "how to make a million dollars in 2026" (hope), "financial freedom in the next 12 months" (dream), "you'll never become rich until you understand this" (fear), "how to get rich without talent, luck, or a trust fund" (blocker).
  <br>*Why it works:* The topic is the same. Only the emotion changes.
- **The Sahara title:** "How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert" turned a very niche lecture into a clickable video.
  <br>*Why it works:* A name everyone knows (the UN) plus a strong verb phrase (holding back).

### Common mistakes

- A clever title nobody understands in 3 seconds.
- A narrow skill promise when a big outcome everyone wants was available.
- Picking one emotion by habit instead of testing hope, dream, fear and blocker.
- Using numbered lists or "new era" framing just because they sound fresh. In one channel's small sample, they did worst.

### Try it

Take one topic and write four titles: one each for hope, dream, fear and blocker. Cross out any that a stranger could not explain in 3 seconds. Keep the best one.

## 5.13 Viral writing 101

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Viral posts are a craft. Decide which emotion you want the reader to feel, make your first tweet earn the second, write in simple words, and read two ratios afterwards to see what worked. One creator says five threads built from these ideas added 100,000 followers in about 3 months.

### Pick the emotion before you write

- Keep a short list at the top of your document: LOL, WTF, "finally someone said it", aww, wow. Choose one before you write a word.
- The best case is a single take that triggers two opposite emotions. Your fans think "finally someone said it" while the other side thinks "WTF". Both reactions spread the post.

### Reusable hook shapes

- Mythbuster: take something many people believe and show it is wrong.
- "If you're out of the loop": explain the thing everyone keeps hearing about but doesn't understand.
- David vs Goliath: a small, underdog person or team against a giant.
- "PSA" (public service announcement) or "let me do you a favor": a short warning or tip that helps the reader.
- "Mistakes I made" or "what I wish I knew 5 years ago": you speak from lessons you learned, not from a position of being better than the reader.

### Write simply

- Write at a 2nd to 8th grade reading level. Use no jargon. A phrase like "yield a positive ROI" carries no emotion.
- Here is a real example. A thread about the audio app Clubhouse went viral at a 2nd to 3rd grade reading level. A smarter founder wrote the same take at a 12th grade level, and it died.

### Timing and reading the numbers

- Timing matters. The Clubhouse take only worked at the peak of the hype. A month later, or 3 months earlier, it would have flopped.
- After you post, read two ratios. Likes divided by impressions (how many people saw it): 2 to 3% is a big hit. Reposts divided by likes: 20 to 30% means the post will spread, because people repost what they want to be seen agreeing with.

### Test small, then expand

- Make the first tweet earn the second, and the second earn the third. Also write the hook 20 different ways before you pick one.
- A single tweet that does better than normal can become a thread.
- The best tweet inside a thread can become its own post. One example is Elon Musk's line "if you need words of encouragement, don't do a startup".
- Most people didn't see what you posted 6 months ago, so you can reuse what worked.

### Frameworks

**Viral writing checklist**

1. Pick the emotion (LOL, WTF, finally someone said it, aww, wow).
2. Make tweet 1 earn tweet 2.
3. Write the hook 20 ways.
4. Write at a 2nd to 8th grade level.
5. Read likes per impression and reposts per like.
6. Turn winners into threads, and the best tweet of a thread into its own post.

### Examples

- **The Clubhouse thread:** A thread written at a 2nd to 3rd grade reading level went viral. A smarter founder's version of the same take, at a 12th grade level, died.
  <br>*Why it works:* Same take. The simpler words won.
- **Test small:** Elon Musk's line "if you need words of encouragement, don't do a startup" worked as one tweet inside a thread and then as its own post.
  <br>*Why it works:* The best tweet in a thread can stand on its own.

### Common mistakes

- Writing before deciding which emotion the reader should feel.
- Spending the same effort on every tweet instead of the first words and the first tweet.
- Using jargon and a high reading level.
- Judging a post by likes alone and ignoring reposts per like.

### Try it

Take one finished post and write the hook 20 different ways. Then check which emotion from the list the full post leaves behind.

## 5.14 How the algorithm decides (and what creators disagree on)

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** One useful way to think about it: the algorithm is a matchmaker. It tests your video on a small group of strangers and pushes it to more people if they enjoy it. This is a simplified model, not published platform rules, and other creators disagree on parts of it.

### A model: the algorithm as a matchmaker

- Platforms make money when people keep watching, so they try to match each video with the people who will enjoy it.
- Step 1: it describes your video. Computer vision reads the visuals. It also reads the audio or transcript, the caption and data about you. From this it builds a score for who should like the video.
- Step 2: it shows the video to a first test group (about 200 people in this model), mostly people who don't follow you. Strangers liking it is the real signal.
- Step 3: good results push it to about 2,000 people, then about 20,000, and so on. Mixed results mean it is tested on another small group. Bad results stop it. People call being stuck at around 200 views "200 view jail": it means the first test group didn't like it.
- What the platform reads: average watch time or the share of the video people finish, the engagement rate (likes plus comments plus shares, divided by views), and how much of someone's session was spent on your videos (which nobody outside the platform can see).

### 5 ways to get more comments

- Take a hard stance.
- Take the contrarian side.
- Push the framing further, for example "better than every pasta shop in the world".
- Talk about things people already have opinions on. Naming Nike gets more replies than saying "shoes".
- Make them feel something.

### Why the first seconds matter so much

- On short videos, viewers drop off fast, with a cliff in the first 2 seconds. Every second without a clear topic loses a large share of the audience.
- The attention timer on short videos is about 4 seconds. On YouTube it is about 1 to 2 minutes.

### What other creators say about the algorithm

- One creator says the Shorts algorithm is not random and has nothing to do with luck. It shows the best, most engaging content. Make engaging shorts with high retention, he says, and blowing up is almost guaranteed.
- The same creator says his first channel took about 4 months to reach 10,000 subscribers. The next channel reached 10,000 in 3 weeks, because of more experience.
- A fair question: why would Instagram or TikTok push a video that isn't captivating to 90% of viewers? Keywords and hashtags might add 10% more views, but they won't take you from 1,000 views to 100,000.
- The algorithm is always the hot topic. If you understand human nature and what captures, holds and creates attention, you don't need to chase the algorithm or study every new tactic. You can build a steady system and your own feel instead of jumping on every trend.
- Don't trust anyone who says one platform or format is better because "the algorithm changed". Test them all.
- One creator names three types of people who fail: people who post and post and then blame the algorithm, smart people whose posts are like art pieces that nobody buys (the market doesn't care), and people who grow a little but don't double down on what works.

### There is no single "meta"

- A meta is the style everyone says is winning right now. One creator argues there isn't one. People keep declaring "everything is moving to storytelling", "longer videos" or "thumbnails are dead".
- YouTube is too big for one style. Five to ten years ago long storytelling videos did well. In 5 to 10 years, short 2 to 3 minute videos without a story may do well.
- His claim is that even at the peak of MrBeast-style videos, only about 5 to 10% of trending videos used that style, and maybe 1 to 2% of all YouTube viewing. More storytelling and less hyper-fast editing is a trend, not the rule.
- On consistency: a regular posting schedule may not matter to the algorithm, but it matters for your own motivation.

### Replace "algorithm" with "audience"

- About 6 years ago, a friend who works on the YouTube algorithm told one creator: whenever you are about to say "algorithm", say "audience" instead, and you probably have the real answer.
- "The algorithm is punishing me" becomes "the audience isn't interested". "The algorithm didn't like my thumbnail change" becomes "my audience didn't click it".
- Blaming the algorithm is a cop out. Good YouTube strategy comes down to understanding real people and predicting what they will do.

### Frameworks

**The algorithm as a matchmaker**

1. It describes your video and builds a score for who should like it.
2. It shows the video to a first test group of mostly strangers (about 200).
3. Good results: pushed to about 2,000, then about 20,000.
4. Mixed results: tested on another small group.
5. Bad results: it stops ("200 view jail").

**5 ways to get more comments**

1. Take a hard stance.
2. Take the contrarian side.
3. Push the framing further.
4. Talk about what people already have opinions on.
5. Make them feel something.

### Examples

- **Specific gets more replies:** Naming Nike gets more replies than saying "shoes".
  <br>*Why it works:* People comment on things they already have a view on.
- **No single meta:** Even at the peak of MrBeast-style videos, only about 5 to 10% of trending videos used that style, one creator says.
  <br>*Why it works:* A style that is going around is a trend, not the rule.

### Common mistakes

- Treating the 200-person test as an official platform rule. It is a simplified model.
- Chasing every claim that "the algorithm changed".
- Believing a new style is now the only way.
- Relying on keywords and hashtags to replace a captivating video.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say posting time, hashtags and captions don't matter. Others found that posting time did. A third says keywords and hashtags might add about 10% more views at most. One creator says high retention almost guarantees you will blow up, while others say no single trick or meta exists. Test it yourself.

### Try it

Read the 5 ways to get more comments, then look at the last post you saw with lots of replies. Which of the 5 did it use?

## 5.15 The X algorithm: 16 rules

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** One creator, who grew from 400 to 26,000 followers, lists 16 rules for how X's algorithm works in 2026. They are his reading of the latest update, not official rules, so treat each one as "it appears that". The main idea: you grow by understanding what the algorithm wants people to do with your content.

### The big idea

- You don't grow by posting and replying more. You grow by understanding what the algorithm wants people to do with your content.
- It wants people to read, watch, reply, quote, share and follow. It is also getting better at telling real interest from empty engagement.
- All 16 rules below come from one creator's reading of the update.

### What to make (rules 1 to 7)

- 1\. Post original content. Original posts give you the best chance to reach people who don't follow you. Don't build your strategy on replies, reposts, or copied or recycled text. Posts should stand on their own.
- 2\. Make posts people want to share. Sharing is one of the strongest signals, especially when someone copies the link and sends it to a person.
- 3\. Make people want to reply and quote. Try opinions, observations, frameworks, questions and ideas people want to react to.
- 4\. Make videos worth watching. Start with simple, honest, self-made videos with no special effects. The first seconds matter more than ever.
- 5\. One great post beats six average posts. Your posts compete with each other in the same feed, so spend more time making one idea great. Use drafts on your phone.
- 6\. Talk to your audience: ask questions, reply, support people, build relationships and follow the people you interact with. The claim is that reply conversations between people who follow each other get much more weight.
- 7\. Make posts that earn followers. A post that makes someone follow you is a powerful signal. Some of your posts should give a clear reason to follow: be useful, interesting, educational or explanatory.

### What to avoid (rules 9 to 13)

- 9\. Stop writing clickbait. Attention that you keep is what counts, not clicks.
- 10\. Avoid content people want to hide. "Not interested" is one of the strongest negative signals. Controversial is fine. Irrelevant, annoying or misleading is not.
- 11\. Don't use engagement bait such as "like if you agree", "comment yes to receive it" or "tag 3 friends". It is penalized more than ever.
- 12\. Don't stuff in hashtags.
- 13\. Don't repeat identical or nearly identical text, because it looks like spam. Reuse an idea with a new angle, format or insight.

### Timing, account size and numbers (rules 8, 14, 15, 16)

- 8\. Small accounts (under 1,000 followers) appear to get extra discovery. It is the best time to experiment boldly.
- 14\. Don't post back to back. Posts compete for the same audience, so let each one earn its own reach first.
- 15\. Numbers: bookmarks appear to have little or no direct effect on ranking. Time spent on the post is the major metric. Sharing, replying, quoting and following still count.
- 16\. The first 48 hours matter most. Don't expect the algorithm to bring an old post back to life. Reply to the comments on your own posts within the first 30 minutes.

### Frameworks

**The 16 rules in four groups**

1. Make: original posts, posts worth sharing, posts people reply to, videos worth watching, one great post, posts that earn the follow.
2. Talk: engage with people who follow each other.
3. Avoid: clickbait, content people hide, engagement bait, too many hashtags, repeated text, posting back to back.
4. Know: small accounts get extra discovery, time spent is the major metric, and the first 48 hours decide.

### Examples

- **Engagement bait:** "Like if you agree", "comment yes to receive it" and "tag 3 friends" are said to be penalized more than ever.
  <br>*Why it works:* Ask for real replies with a real question instead.

### Common mistakes

- Building your whole strategy on replies and reposts.
- Posting six average posts instead of one great one.
- Using engagement bait or stuffing in hashtags.
- Waiting for the algorithm to revive a post after 48 hours.

### Where the sources disagree

One creator says bookmarks appear to have little or no direct effect on ranking. Many other creators still treat saves as a sign a post was valuable. Both can be true: a save can show value without being a ranking signal. Remember that all 16 rules are one creator's reading, not official statements.

### Try it

Pick one of your recent posts and check it against rules 2, 3 and 7: would someone send it to a friend, reply to it, or follow you because of it?

## 5.16 The viral trap: chase the right views

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Not all views are equal. A video that goes viral with the wrong crowd can stall everything that comes after it. Aim for views from people who could actually become your customers. This is sometimes called on-target virality.

### The wheel of topic slices

- Picture a wheel cut into slices, one per topic. People who just scroll sit in the middle. Buyers with a specific pain sit at the edges.
- A huge viral video about a broad topic is like a big ball that gets stuck in the middle. You get lots of views and nobody buys.
- A specific video, for example "mobility exercises for millennials", rolls down one slice to the people who actually want it and buy.

### The idea rings

- Use the example of marketing for restaurant owners. Think of four rings around the person you serve.
- Core: their exact problem in their exact niche (marketing for restaurants).
- Inner: their problem in a neighbouring niche (marketing for any local shop).
- Outer: their problem in any niche (business marketing).
- Max: anything about the broad topic (all marketing). Avoid this ring. A viral video here brings the wrong followers and confuses the algorithm.
- A good mix if you want to sell: 40% core, 40% inner, 20% outer (this is your "controlled chaos", where posts can surprise you and spread), and 0% max.

### Three more filters

- Walk-away value: does the post give one usable tip, even if the person never buys?
- The peer-share test: would a restaurant owner text this to another restaurant owner?
- Stay consistent for the same person. If you post about tech, then health, then politics, the algorithm mixes those audiences when it shows your next video, and it flops. Sometimes say no to ideas that seem viral but would attract the wrong audience.

### Topic discipline versus virality

- If you optimize for virality, you get pushed toward topics that don't lead to what your business needs. A video about ordering fast food might go viral, but those viewers may not care about your main topic.
- Keep your viewers' minutes on target. Narrow your topic choices so that more of the time people spend with you builds one association: you are the person for this topic.

### The follower trap

- One social media lead at an AI company, who says they generated billions of organic views, argues that not all views are equal and that most can harm long-term growth.
- Here is the picture (made up to illustrate). A brand new page with zero followers posts a video made for marketers and gets 50,000 views and 500 followers. Then it posts a funny video of a gorilla running a marathon and gets 5 million views and 5,000 followers.
- Now it posts its next product video. The algorithm shows it to the gorilla followers, who came for entertainment. They don't engage, so the algorithm stops pushing it.
- Some companies have become known for viral stunts instead of their product. Chasing distribution by any means necessary is a trap.
- Be strict and patient about the audience you gather while you grow from 0 to 1 million views a month.

### Frameworks

**The idea rings**

1. Core: the exact problem in the exact niche.
2. Inner: the same problem in a neighbouring niche.
3. Outer: the same problem in any niche.
4. Max: anything about the broad topic (avoid).
5. Mix: 40% core, 40% inner, 20% outer, 0% max.

**Three filters**

1. Walk-away value: one usable tip.
2. Peer-share test: would they send it to someone like them?
3. Consistency: the same few topics for the same person.

### Examples

- **The gorilla video (made up):** A page gets 50,000 views and 500 followers on a video for marketers, then 5 million views and 5,000 followers on a gorilla marathon video. Its next product video goes to the gorilla crowd and the algorithm stops pushing it.
  <br>*Why it works:* It shows how the wrong views can hurt your next post.
- **Restaurant owners:** Core is marketing for restaurants, inner is marketing for any local shop, outer is business marketing, max is all marketing.
  <br>*Why it works:* The same problem, widened one ring at a time.

### Common mistakes

- Making a max-ring video just because it could go viral.
- Switching topics every post, so the algorithm blends audiences.
- Chasing views on topics that don't lead to what your business needs.
- Treating distribution by any means necessary as a plan.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say to stop niching down: keep your ideal customer but cover every problem they have, not one narrow topic. The rings model keeps the same buyer too, but warns against the broad max ring and suggests a 40/40/20/0 mix. They agree on keeping the ideal customer. They differ on how far from the core to go.

### Try it

Write the four idea rings for one topic you teach. Then check which ring your last three posts fell into.

## 5.17 Doubling down and testing new things

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Brands grow on what is proven and lead on what is new. Use the 70/20/10 rule: 70% proven content, 20% small changes to what works, 10% brand new experiments. And when something wins, make more of it.

### The 70/20/10 rule

- 70% of your content is what you know works. Your audience likes it, it does at least as well as your usual results, and you have enough data to say it will keep working.
- 20% iterates on that 70%: small refinements like changing a hook, the format or the structure a little. Nothing crazy.
- 10% is experiments: trying something entirely new. Leaving this out is a big mistake. Most teams only do the 70%, more advanced teams add the 20%, and few try the 10%. It is the only way to find new formats, thumbnails and packaging.
- The line to remember: brands grow on the 70%, but brands lead on the 10%.
- How to run it: don't do "one experimental post out of every 10". Out of every 30 days, set aside 3 days for experiments. Batching is more efficient, and in that mindset you come up with 2 or 3 ideas at once. If you work with a team, tell them about the rule so they know they can spend 10% of their time on new things. It works for much more than content.

### Flops and data

- Well-known brands often stall at a ceiling because they are unwilling to try things that might get a tenth or a twentieth of their normal views. That is okay. Experiments are allowed to flop.
- Not every experiment works. The more you fail in experiments, the closer you get to a win.
- But 7 days in a row of poor results is not something to celebrate. It is something to look into. Keep tracking your data.
- One creator says that content hackathons (a short event where the whole team only brainstorms and tests) produced many new formats, scripting ways and YouTube packaging.
- Do a weekly recap of your numbers, like views and watch time. On YouTube, if the average view duration (how long people watch, on average) is going up, you are doing the right things. If it is going down, rethink how your script is built.
- Report your work with data: for example a video that did 2.5 or 3 times better than your average on TikTok or LinkedIn, plus engagement, average view duration, leads and leads that turned into customers.
- A/B test instead of arguing about taste. Show two versions and let the audience decide.

### Double down on winners

- A common failure is people who grow a little but don't double down on their best ideas. They forget what made them grow.
- A monthly method: look at your best content, save the links, and rewrite each piece from different angles. Make some short, some long, some lists. Try a bigger idea, a better hook, a clearer main problem, a more practical list.
- This lifts your baseline of engagement and persuasion over time. It isn't about writing whatever you feel like. It is about repeating the ideas your audience actually likes. Expect it to take 6 months to a year.
- Your best-performing angle also points to the product you should create. You need that angle to catch attention and sell.
- Start with whatever content excites you, listen to your customers, then double down (and eventually triple down) on a few formats that you see working. You don't need to do everything at once.
- When something pops, make 5 more versions. Don't think "I already did that one". Top creators repackage their winners instead of chasing new ideas.
- On a team, someone can look at the data, titles, packaging and thumbnails, where and why people become customers, and the sales team's data, to decide what to make next.

### Stay flexible

- Every format, angle and package goes stale. Most posts should use formats you know work, but a meaningful part should be experiments, because moving first is how you gain influence. Most creators react second or third. Set aside creative time to plan new angles.
- Habits of the top 1%: they study outliers obsessively, they consume as much as they create, they have strong opinions, they only talk about what they've done, they use data, they move fast, they think in years, and they are willing to look stupid.
- One creator says he pioneered several X formats, such as pairing an article with a quote post, or with a GIF (this is his own claim).

### Frameworks

**70/20/10**

1. 70% proven content.
2. 20% small changes to proven content.
3. 10% new experiments.
4. Run it as 3 days of experiments per 30 days.
5. Track your data, and investigate a week of flops.

**The monthly double down**

1. Review your best content every month.
2. Save the links.
3. Rewrite each from different angles: short, long, list, bigger idea, better hook, clearer problem, more practical.
4. Keep going for 6 months to a year.

### Examples

- **5 more versions:** When something pops, make 5 more versions of it. Don't think "I already did it".
  <br>*Why it works:* Top creators repackage their winners.
- **3 days out of 30:** Instead of one experimental post out of every 10, set aside 3 days out of every 30 for experiments and batch the ideas.
  <br>*Why it works:* Batching is more efficient.

### Common mistakes

- Only doing the 70% and never testing anything new.
- Forcing one new thing a week instead of batching experiments.
- Celebrating 7 days of flops instead of investigating them.
- Growing a little and forgetting what made you grow.
- Arguing about creative calls instead of A/B testing.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators give a number: about 10% experiments, or 3 days out of 30. Others just say a meaningful part with no number. Others say to focus on a few formats and double down instead of doing everything at once. They agree that you need both proven content and experiments. They differ on how much new to try.

### Try it

Sort your last 10 posts into three piles: proven, refined and new. Check whether the split roughly matches 70/20/10.

## 5.18 Making things worth spreading

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Don't ask who can promote your post. Ask what you can create that people want to react to, use, test or share. The same thinking works for posts, for products, and for moving early on new tools.

### Give people something to quote

- Plan how your post will spread by asking two questions: who would share it, and what would they say about it?
- "Excited to announce our new platform" gives people nothing to discuss.
- Show the concrete thing instead. Say you turned a messy inventory spreadsheet into a working app. Show what you started with, what the tool produced, and what you had to fix yourself. Now a business owner recognises their own problem, a developer examines the result, and someone tries it on their own spreadsheet.
- Test before you publish: what could a person who doesn't know you add to this post? If you struggle to answer, add something more specific.
- Disagreement brings attention, but think about what it leaves people believing. You want a conversation you can back up with evidence.

### Create things worth spreading

- Instead of "who can promote this?", ask what you can create that people want to react to, use, test or share.
- Ideas that work: a benchmark (a comparison with a clear scoreboard), a dataset, a teardown (taking something apart to show how it works), a strong take, a public experiment, or a product to try.
- Let people test your workflow, compare against your benchmark, join your experiment and add their own result.
- The more personally relevant it is to them, the less you have to push it.

### Packaging a product: find the angle first

- The biggest beginner mistake when building a first product is not looking for the right angle first. The angle is how you position the product so people see it as valuable. You wouldn't call it "Writing Course". You'd call it something that makes people want it.
- This used to need slow, boring market research. Now social media shows you exactly what people want, through what they engage with.
- Here is an example. If a YouTube video called "how to focus for 12 hours straight" goes viral, a product called "How to study for 12 hours straight" will likely sell well, especially if you link it in that video's description.
- An AI prompt you can use: "I'm building a product about [topic]. It helps people [transformation]. I want you to research top performing social posts across all platforms that could act as great angles for my product, then help me brainstorm what to name my product."
- Bonus: use those viral posts as inspiration for your own posts. Content built on a viral idea, with your product under it, is more likely to spread and bring sales.

### Use new tools early

- This is sometimes called AI video arbitrage. A new AI model comes out, you make something interesting or viral with it, and you know how to post it and get traffic. It lasts as long as AI video keeps improving.
- Treat every project as a test for the future and not a snapshot of now, because quality only goes up.
- Take the launch video for an OpenAI model as an example. A generic video would have gotten views, but pushing for something unique got hundreds of millions of impressions.

### Frameworks

**Things worth spreading**

1. A benchmark
2. A dataset
3. A teardown
4. A strong take
5. A public experiment
6. A product to try

**The quote test**

1. Who would share this?
2. What would they say?
3. What could a stranger add?
4. If that is hard to answer, add something more specific.

### Examples

- **The messy spreadsheet:** Show a messy inventory spreadsheet turning into a working app: what you started with, what the tool produced, and what you had to fix yourself.
  <br>*Why it works:* Business owners, developers and testers each have a way in.
- **12 hours:** A viral video called "how to focus for 12 hours straight" suggests a product called "How to study for 12 hours straight".
  <br>*Why it works:* The angle that went viral is also the angle that sells.

### Common mistakes

- Posting an announcement that gives people nothing to discuss.
- Chasing disagreement without thinking about what people end up believing.
- Naming a first product plainly, like "Writing Course".
- Treating every project as a snapshot of now when the tools keep improving.

### Try it

Take one post you plan to write and run the quote test: what could a stranger add to it? If you can't answer, add something more specific.

## 5.19 Home run ideas and the three stages of packaging

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A playbook for a viral video in any niche: put the effort of 10 normal videos into one big idea, package it so it stops the scroll, makes people curious and earns the click, make the video satisfying, then repeat enough times to give luck a chance.

### Singles, doubles and home runs

- Borrow the language of baseball. Most channels are built on singles (normal ideas, normal effort, solid but unlikely to break through) and doubles (more effort, maybe better than average, but not a real big swing).
- The big realisation is that the algorithm multiplies what you give it. You can make 10 singles, or you can put the effort of those 10 videos into one video. That one video is a home run.
- Results follow a power law: a much better idea with much better execution can get many times more views, not just a little more.

### What makes a home run

- Go broader. Cast the widest net in your niche. Pick an idea that everyone in the niche could click and enjoy: beginners and experts, die-hard fans and casual ones.
- Give it more time than feels reasonable. Two weeks to film, or travelling to film it, is fine.
- Multiply every part of the execution. If you usually make 2 thumbnails, make 10. If your usual budget is \$500, ask whether this video can justify \$2,000.
- Treat it as a loss leader (something you do at a loss to bring people in). It doesn't have to pay for itself directly. The goal is a video that lifts the whole channel, because breaking through to a new audience compounds.
- Not every video should be a home run. If you swing for the fences every time, you miss a lot. Go bigger once a month or once a quarter.
- It works in small niches too. One student in the permaculture niche (growing food in a way that works with nature) got 16 million views on a video, according to the program they were in.

### The three stages of packaging

- Stage 1, scroll stop. Viewers see a title and a thumbnail for a fraction of a second, so something has to stop them. Usually the thumbnail does it with a striking picture, an emotion or a few words. For example, a tiny flip-book once stopped one creator the first time they saw it.
- Stage 2, interest. The viewer has stopped and is now taking in the packaging, still in seconds. Interest is nearly always built with a curiosity gap: a question the packaging opens. "Wait, how is a flip-book that small possible? Does it actually work?"
- Stage 3, the click. The viewer decides whether to act on that interest. A lot of this depends on the value you promise and whether they believe you.
- All three happen in the blink of an eye, mostly on impulse. Splitting them lets you check your own packaging: what stops the scroll, what opens the gap, and what makes them click?

### Give luck more chances

- Going viral is about creating more chances for luck. The game isn't one try. It is repeated attempts: 5 videos, 10 videos, 50 videos.
- The full playbook in order: a home run idea, packaging that passes the three stages, a satisfying video (a later module covers how), then repeat.

### Frameworks

**The viral video playbook**

1. Pick a home run idea and give it the effort of 10 normal videos.
2. Package it through three stages: scroll stop, interest, click.
3. Make the video satisfying: deliver the promise early and sweat the details.
4. Repeat for 5, 10, 50 videos to give luck more chances.

### Examples

- **The tiny flip-book:** Scroll stop: a flip-book so small it looks impossible. Interest: "how is that possible, does it work?" Click: the viewer believes the video will show them.
  <br>*Why it works:* Each stage has one job, done in under a second.
- **Permaculture:** A student of a creator program got 16 million views on a permaculture video, a niche not known for huge views.
  <br>*Why it works:* It shows that home runs can happen in small niches too.

### Common mistakes

- Swinging for a home run on every video.
- Never taking a big swing at all.
- Packaging that needs 5 seconds to understand.
- Judging one attempt instead of 10 or 50.

### Where the sources disagree

Some creators say the idea and packaging make up half of a video's performance, which is their own estimate. Others say to focus on volume and keep posting. The answer is a mix: mostly normal videos, plus a big swing once a month or once a quarter.

### Try it

Take one idea you already planned and write how it would look as a home run: a broader topic, double the time, 10 thumbnail options. Then run its packaging through scroll stop, interest and click.

## Cheat sheet: Ideas, packaging and virality

The whole module on one page.

- The topic matters most: no editing can save a topic nobody cares about.
- Test every topic on three filters: do enough people want it, does it fit your audience, and do you care about it.
- A strong idea works for your fans, casual viewers and total strangers at the same time.
- An idea has three parts: topic, angle and format. Check all three before you script.
- Make an old topic feel new with a new frame, a new name, a recent update or your own finding.
- Copy the format, never the words.
- Judge a post against its own account's normal, and only look at recent ones.
- Take outliers apart, keep the parts that keep winning and change the rest.
- Kill bad ideas faster: find 20 to 30 and make only the best few.
- Choose ideas that have something clear to show in the first seconds.
- Thumbnail: one to three things to look at, a clear emotion, 3 to 5 words, and never repeat the title.
- Title: one clear promise about a big outcome that a stranger understands in 3 seconds.
- Pick the emotion before you write, and make your first tweet earn the second.
- Treat algorithm rules as claims and test them, because creators disagree on parts of them.
- Aim for views from people who could become customers, not just any views.
- Use the 70/20/10 rule: 70% proven, 20% small changes, 10% new experiments.
- Every month, make more versions of what won.
- Make something people want to react to, use, test or share.
