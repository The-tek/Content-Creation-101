*Write clear, specific, human words that make the right reader take the right action.*

## 11.1 What good copy actually is

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan's definition: good copywriting is getting the right person to take the right action. Every post is built for one reader, one idea and one takeaway, and good copy starts with good thinking.

### The definition

- Ryan: "good copywriting is getting the right person to take the right action." The action might be reading, replying, sharing, following or buying, but every post should be engineered to make someone do something.
- Ryan: "good copywriting is good thinking." Good copy is concise, easy to understand, thought-provoking and built on a strong idea. Weak thinking cannot be fixed with nice words.
- Ryan says writing changed his life: it taught him systems for thinking and "how to actually make people feel ideas".
- Ryan calls copywriting the missing skill: most brands on X are "doomed from the start", not because of their ideas, but because they cannot turn brilliant ideas into words people want to read.
- Matt Gray calls X "a copywriting Olympics": the people winning there are the best writers, so hold your posts to the bar of the best posts you have ever saved.
- Dan Koe: persuasive content is content that captures attention, holds it and delivers value on it.

### Write for one specific reader

- Ryan: picture your ideal reader before you post and get as detailed as possible. What are they struggling with right now? Where do they consume content (is X even the place they are)? What value do they want? What are they feeling right now?
- Ryan: the lower in the funnel a post sits (top, middle or bottom), the more detailed your picture of the reader needs to be.
- Sam's friend Ronnie: there is content that speaks to everyone and content that speaks to one person. Speak to one.

### One idea per post

- Ryan: "If you have three ideas, you have three posts." Multiple ideas generally confuse both the reader and the algorithm.
- Ryan (Oct 19 post): one specific idea per post, "if you need two, write two posts".

### Every post needs a takeaway

- Ryan: ask yourself "What does someone walk away with?" Every post needs a concrete point and a takeaway.
- Kallaway: every content minute should be non-obvious and tactically implementable. He calls the share of your content that does this your "signal".
- Ryan: nail these four fundamentals (a specific reader, one idea, a takeaway, clarity over cleverness) and you are already ahead of most people who post on X.

### Frameworks

**Ryan's 4 copy fundamentals** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh), X Copywriting Masterclass)

1. Write for a specific audience: their struggle, where they read, what value they want, what they feel right now
2. One idea per post: three ideas means three posts
3. Every post needs a takeaway: what does someone walk away with?
4. Clarity over cleverness: if a 12 year old can't read it, don't publish it

### Examples

- **Ryan, his own account:** Ryan says this skill is what took him to 1B+ lifetime impressions and grew his own account from zero, with +9,000 followers and about 6M impressions in his first month.
  <br>*Why it works:* These are Ryan's own claims. He uses them to argue that copywriting, not ideas, is the skill that decides growth.

### Common mistakes

- Packing two or three ideas into one post, which confuses the reader and the algorithm (Ryan)
- Posting without knowing who the post is for (Ryan)
- A post with no concrete point, so the reader walks away with nothing (Ryan)
- Thinking nice wording can save a weak idea: good copy is good thinking first (Ryan)

### Try it

Take your last three posts. For each one write: who exactly it was for (one sentence), the one idea, and what the reader walks away with. If any post had two ideas, split it into two drafts. If any had no takeaway, write the one line it was missing.

## 11.2 Clear over clever: smart ideas, simple words

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Every source in this course agrees: if people don't understand it, nothing else counts. Keep the ideas smart but the words, sentences and formatting simple enough for a child to read.

### The reading level rule

- Ryan: "If a 12 y/o can't read it, I'm not publishing." (In an October post he said 10 year old.) Your concepts don't need to be dumbed down, but your vocabulary, formatting and word choice do.
- Kallaway: simple words, ideally a sixth grade reading level.
- Sam: "speak in seventh grade English. Don't use big terms." Then people apply it, get results and credit you.
- Tim Denning: edit with three goals: fewer words, simpler, fifth grader language. "The best stories aren't the smartest. They're the stories people actually can understand."
- Shaan Puri (with Dickie Bush and Nicolas Cole of Ship 30 for 30): write at a 2nd to 8th grade reading level. His viral Clubhouse thread was written at a 2nd to 3rd grade level; a smarter founder's version of the same take was written at a 12th grade level and died.

### Why simple wins

- Ryan: good writing reduces friction for your reader. Make it as easy as possible to understand your point.
- Kallaway gives comprehension the biggest weight of his four content levers: 40%. Shorter words, simpler sentences, bite-sized chunks. If they don't get it, nothing else counts.
- Sam: it feels good to sound smart, but it doesn't help people. Content only a professor understands won't get views.
- Sam: a true expert turns complex topics into simple principles. The beginner understands it for the first time, and the expert understands it more.
- Ryan (Nov 4 list): mastering copywriting means explaining hard topics in few words.
- Paddy Galloway (on the curse of knowledge): simpler framings of things that seem obvious to experts expand your reach.
- Kallaway: complexity makes viewers check out. Simplify solutions all the way down so the most people can act. Don't confuse complex with valuable.

### Clarity is the non-negotiable

- Kallaway: in your messaging, clarity and consistency on your core contrarian beliefs are the only two non-optional things. Everything else is style.
- Caleb Ralston: in educational content you only control one thing, how clear the message is (how easy it is to understand the action steps). Put all your effort there.
- Caleb: give "radically clear steps" from the problem to the solution. Make it as obvious as possible. A download can help.
- Caleb (on briefing teams): "The enemy of creativity is confusion."
- Nicolas Cole on bios: "Clear, not clever."

### Say it twice

- Kallaway: say a hard point twice, once like you'd say it to a colleague, then again with a simple metaphor like you'd say it to a child ("600 mph = a plane driving on the road").
- Kallaway's "atomic shareability": shrink the idea so someone can repeat it after one read or watch. Paul Revere's "the British are coming" is a whole story in four words.
- Kallaway's story ladder has a "translator" level: a comprehension pass where you add a visual for every complex point or restate it as a simple metaphor.

### Frameworks

**The comprehension pass** (Kallaway)

1. Read the draft as a beginner would
2. Mark every complex point
3. Give each one a visual, or say it a second time as a simple metaphor
4. Shrink the core idea until someone could repeat it after one read

### Examples

- **Shaan Puri, Ship 30 for 30 interview:** His Clubhouse thread, written at a 2nd to 3rd grade level, went viral. A smart founder posted the same take written at a 12th grade level and it died. He also points to phrases like "yield a positive ROI" as jargon with zero emotion.
  <br>*Why it works:* Same idea, different reading level, different result. Simple words carry emotion; jargon carries none.
- **Paul Revere, via Kallaway:** "The British are coming."
  <br>*Why it works:* Four words that anyone can repeat. Kallaway uses it to show atomic shareability.

### Common mistakes

- Writing to sound smart instead of to be understood (Sam)
- Thinking complex means valuable (Kallaway)
- Using jargon your reader doesn't know (Kallaway, Ship 30)
- Assuming something is too obvious to explain, the curse of knowledge (Paddy Galloway)

### Where the sources disagree

The sources agree on simple words but name different reading levels: Shaan Puri says 2nd to 8th grade, Tim Denning 5th grade, Kallaway 6th grade, Sam 7th grade, Ryan a 10 to 12 year old. All of them mean the same thing: far simpler than most people write.

### Try it

Take one paragraph you wrote recently. Paste it into any free reading level checker. Rewrite it until it scores at 6th grade or below without losing the idea. Then pick the hardest point in it and add a one-line metaphor a child would get.

## 11.3 Kallaway's sentence clarity toolkit

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Kallaway treats confusion as one of the four things that kill a hook. His fix is plain craft: fewer words, active voice, better word order, no jargon, and a test that checks if a line can be read two ways.

### Confusion kills

- Kallaway's four hook killers are Delay, Confusion, Irrelevance and Disinterest. Confusion means the topic arrives on time, but the words or phrasing are unclear, so the viewer understands half and can't judge if it's for them.
- His fix: become a better writer. Use fewer words, but enough that the viewer can't misunderstand.
- Simple words, ideally a sixth grade reading level.
- Direct active voice, not passive: "the dog jumped", not "the jump of the dog".

### Word order matters

- Slightly different words and order raise the comprehension hit rate. Nothing new was added, it was only reordered and simplified.

### The misinterpretation test

- Read the one or two key sentences in isolation and ask: is it possible for the reader to understand this the wrong way? Is there more than one way these sentences could be read?
- If yes, rewrite to remove the other paths until only one reading is left.
- Kallaway's "super clear" rule for hooks: only one possible reading, no wordplay.
- Mino Lee: revise until it's the clearest, most concise version, and read it out loud five times.

### Jargon and names

- Avoid terms the viewer doesn't know unless you can show them. Kallaway's example of a failure was using "generative world model" as a hook term.
- Prefer names that can't be misread. For his floor plans video he suggests "future of home design" as first text over "life-size floor plans".
- Compression, not speed: Kallaway says 8 words beat 28 because the brain can only hold so much at once.

### Using AI for clarity

- Note what the prompt asks for: the same meaning, only clearer. The idea stays yours.

### Frameworks

**Four hook killers (the confusion fix)** (Kallaway)

1. Delay: the topic arrives too late, so move it to line one
2. Confusion: the words are hard to parse, so use fewer, simpler words, active voice and better order
3. Irrelevance: the reader isn't sure it's for them, so say 'you' and name their pain
4. Disinterest: no curiosity, so add contrast between what they believe and what you say

**Misinterpretation test** (Kallaway)

1. Isolate the key line
2. Ask if it can be read more than one way
3. Rewrite until only one reading remains

### Examples

- **Kallaway, Hooks masterclass:** "...the online money they made is most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice like they did" became "...their secret for earning money online was their insane journaling practice."
  <br>*Why it works:* Same facts, new order and simpler words. The reader gets it on the first pass.
- **Kallaway, active voice:** "The dog jumped" instead of "the jump of the dog".
  <br>*Why it works:* Active voice puts a clear subject doing a clear action, which is faster to read.

### Common mistakes

- Cutting so many words that the line can be misunderstood (Kallaway: fewer words, but enough)
- Wordplay and clever double meanings in key lines (Kallaway)
- Leading with jargon the reader has never seen (Kallaway)
- Never reading the line out loud before publishing (Mino Lee)

### Try it

Write the first line of your next post. Run the misinterpretation test: show it to someone with no context and ask what it means. Then rewrite it three ways: once shorter, once in active voice, once with the words reordered so the main point comes first. Read all versions out loud and keep the clearest.

## 11.4 Every word earns its place

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan's word choice rule: if deleting a word doesn't change the meaning, delete it. Cut filler and soft words, pick simple words and strong verbs, and swap broad words for emotional, specific ones.

### Ryan's word choice rules

- Every word needs to earn its place in your copy.
- Cut filler: really, very, just, actually.
- Use simple words: utilise becomes use, facilitate becomes help, commence becomes start.
- Use strong verbs: "grew really quickly" becomes "exploded".
- Write in active voice: "Mistakes were made" becomes "Most creators make this mistake".
- Delete: if deleting a word doesn't change the meaning, delete it.
- In his hook process: cut any words that say the same thing twice or add no value.

### Cut soft words, be concrete (Codie Sanchez)

- Cut soft words like "kind of" and "I think".
- Cut "very", "really" and "honestly".

### Emotional, specific words (Mino Lee)

- Mino Lee (average 1.2M views a video) says one or two words can decide 5k versus 100k views.
- Swap vague words for emotional, specific ones: "alone" over "by yourself", "burnt out", "going broke", "dying business", "passive income machine", "AI agents", "10K a month".
- Avoid broad words like "get better" or "improve".
- Stack several keywords in one line (his example stacks insecure, Asian, nerds and losers in one hook).
- After you upgrade a word, remove what became redundant: "AI agents" makes "with AI" unnecessary.

### Strong nouns and verbs in titles

- Paddy Galloway: use well-known entities and strong verbs in titles. His example "How the UN is holding back the Sahara Desert" uses "the UN" and "holding back".
- Shaan Puri (Ship 30): jargon like "yield a positive ROI" has zero emotion.

### Frameworks

**The delete test** (Ryan (@ryangrowthh))

1. Go through the draft one word at a time
2. Ask: if I delete this, does the meaning change?
3. If not, delete it
4. Then swap long words for short ones and weak verbs for strong ones

### Examples

- **Ryan, X Copywriting Masterclass:** "Mistakes were made" becomes "Most creators make this mistake". "Grew really quickly" becomes "exploded".
  <br>*Why it works:* Active voice names who did it; a strong verb replaces a weak verb plus two filler words.
- **Codie Sanchez, psychology of selling:** "There by Friday" instead of "fast delivery".
  <br>*Why it works:* A concrete picture the buyer can see beats an abstract promise.

### Common mistakes

- Filler words like really, very, just, actually (Ryan, Codie)
- Hedging with "kind of" and "I think" (Codie)
- Broad words like "improve" or "get better" (Mino Lee)
- Fancy words where a short one works: utilise, facilitate, commence (Ryan)
- Saying the same thing twice in one line (Ryan, Mino Lee)

### Try it

Take a 150 word draft. Circle every filler and soft word (really, very, just, actually, kind of, I think, honestly) and delete them. Then run Ryan's delete test on every remaining word. Finally replace one vague word with an emotional, specific one. Compare the word count before and after.

## 11.5 Be specific: numbers, names and proof

*7 min read*

> **The big idea:** Vague lines give the reader nothing to picture or predict. Numbers, names, timeframes and real proof make a line believable and make people want the rest.

### Vague versus specific

- Ryan: "I grew fast" is weak. "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month" is better. Numbers, names and timeframes beat vague promises.
- Ryan: use numbers, results and specifics.
- Kallaway: "something crazy happened", "you're not going to believe it" and "things were about to change" are empty teasers. Give enough specific detail that the reader can guess: "within 30 minutes, she tells me something that completely changes my strategy for the next 6 months."
- Kallaway: specific urgency sounds like "within a week", "the call was in 2 hours", "48 hours".
- Kallaway: specific proof sounds like "from 2 to 15,000 followers in 6 weeks", not "this will help you grow faster".
- Paddy Galloway: specific, provocative claims beat generic ones: "this tool is now illegal in 7 and a half states", "99% of woodworkers get this wrong".

### Specificity sells

- Shann Holmberg: say specific things instead of vague ones.
- Hussain Ibarra: specificity sells.
- 0xfJuan's test: if a stranger couldn't add anything to your line, it's generic. Include something more specific.
- Matt Gray found in his own audit that specific numbers in hooks did "10x better" (his claim).
- Kallaway's text hook rules: specific numbers like "22 days" beat "a few weeks"; "\$97,000 in 13 days" beats a round claim.

### Specific and believable outcomes

- Dan Koe: make outcomes specific and believable within the timeframe: "build your first website in 30 days", "lose your first 10 pounds".
- Codie Sanchez: scarcity must be said specifically ("4 slots, 2 are gone"), never "limited spots".

### Specific in praise and feedback too

- Caleb Ralston: when praising a team member to executives, name the person, the situation, the deadline and the result (his "Sarah" example).
- Caleb: positive "keep doing" notes should be specific too ("I love this cutaway").

### Frameworks

**Specific proof line** (Kallaway and Ryan)

1. A number with its unit (followers, dollars, days)
2. A before and after (from 2 to 15,000)
3. A timeframe (in 6 weeks)
4. A name where it helps (a person, a company, a tool)

### Examples

- **Kallaway, Addictive storytelling:** "Something crazy happened" versus "within 30 minutes, she tells me something that completely changes my strategy for the next 6 months."
  <br>*Why it works:* The specific version lets the reader guess what's coming, which is what creates curiosity. The vague one gives them nothing to predict.
- **Ryan, Hooks 101:** "I grew fast" versus "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month."
  <br>*Why it works:* Numbers and a timeframe make the claim believable and concrete.
- **Paddy Galloway:** "This tool is now illegal in 7 and a half states."
  <br>*Why it works:* The odd, exact number makes it feel real and sparks curiosity.

### Common mistakes

- Empty teasers like "you won't believe this" (Kallaway)
- Round, vague promises like "grow faster" (Kallaway, Ryan)
- Outcomes that are too big for the timeframe to be believable (Dan Koe)
- "Limited spots" instead of an exact number (Codie)

### Try it

Find three vague lines in your recent writing (look for words like 'fast', 'a lot', 'huge', 'something'). Rewrite each one with a number, a timeframe or a name. Only use numbers that are true. Then run 0xfJuan's test: could a stranger have written the old line? Could they write the new one?

## 11.6 Use the words your audience already uses

*7 min read*

## 11.7 Open, close and shape a post

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan's rules for the shape of a post: get to the point fast, one thought per line, short paragraphs, and a closing line that never trails off. Kallaway adds the rhythm rules and the 'you before I' rule.

### How to open a post (Ryan)

- Ryan: cut the filler, get to the point fast, and show proof and credibility as early as you can.
- Kallaway: in hooks, use 'you' and 'your' instead of 'I' and 'me', so the viewer feels it is for them. His own note: his power word example 'If I had to grow from 0 to 100K...' uses 'I' with the viewer replicating it, and a creator's own result can work as proof when the creator matches the viewer.
- Ryan (Nov 4 list): think 'you' before 'I'. Always make it about the reader.

### How to close a post (Ryan)

- Close with the takeaway in 1 or 2 lines, a question, or a tease of what comes next.
- Ryan: 'Never trail off.' The last line is the second most important line of the post (after the first).
- Ryan: every post needs a takeaway. Ask 'what does someone walk away with?'

### Rhythm and readability (Ryan)

- Use line breaks. Walls of text get skipped.
- Mix short and long sentences.
- One thought per line. Ryan: 'If a sentence has two ands and a comma, it's probably two sentences.'
- Paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines. On a phone, 4 lines look like an essay.
- Make it scannable with numbers, lists and arrows.
- Use transition lines that pull people down, like 'Let me explain...'.
- Preview the post on your phone before you publish.

### Sentence rhythm for hooks versus body (Kallaway)

- Hooks: staccato, short sentences for maximum clarity and value per word.
- Body: vary sentence length for rhythm. Expand to medium and longer sentences.

### Frameworks

**Ryan's post shape** (Ryan)

1. Open: cut filler, state the point, show proof early
2. Middle: one thought per line, paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines, mix short and long sentences
3. Pull: add transition lines like 'Let me explain...'
4. Close: takeaway in 1 or 2 lines, a question, or a tease of what's next
5. Check: preview on your phone

### Examples

- **Ryan's two-ands rule:** A sentence with two 'and's and a comma is probably two sentences, so split it into two lines.
  <br>*Why it works:* Ryan uses this to keep one thought per line.
- **Kallaway, you versus I:** Hooks lean on 'you / your' so the viewer feels the line is for them.
  <br>*Why it works:* Kallaway says creator-proof with 'I' can still work when the creator matches the viewer.

### Common mistakes

- Opening with filler before the point
- Walls of text and 4-line paragraphs
- Trailing off with no takeaway at the end
- Writing about 'I' when the post should be about the reader

### Try it

Take one finished post. Split every sentence that has two 'and's, cap paragraphs at 3 lines, rewrite the last line as a takeaway, a question or a tease, then preview it on your phone.

## 11.8 Contrast, tension and honest tone

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Kallaway's phrasing toolkit builds contrast with small connecting words and benefit-first lines, then keeps it honest: commit to the story, whisper instead of shouting, show instead of command, and always deliver the payoff.

### Contrast phrasing toolkit (Kallaway)

- Interjection words that create contrast: but, however, yet, although, therefore, on the other hand, 'but get this', 'but here's the thing'.
- 'Everyone thinks X, but actually it's Y.'
- Stated contrast (A and B both said) versus implied contrast (only B said).
- Lead with the benefit or pain: 'If you want better sleep...', 'If you struggle with acne...'.

### Phrasing devices from other creators

- Mino Lee: a closing line like 'Take notes and thank me later' adds pull (he calls it a super hook).
- Mino Lee templates: 'Here's how to [stop painful thing]' and 'If you're [painful situation], here's the ultimate guide to [result]'.
- heyDominik: 'This [thing] should not [expected outcome]...' with trailing dots to open a loop.
- heyDominik: contradict a common belief in one short line, like 'Lean at 45 and I eat carbs every day'.

### Tone and commitment (Kallaway, Illusion of novelty)

- Commit to the story. Do not hedge or downplay. 'Of course this is just circadian biology, known for decades' kills it.
- Be the gossip whisperer ('Okay, so not that many people know about this yet...'), not the town crier ('Huge news. This changes everything.').
- No fake urgency.

### Show, do not command (Kallaway, Power of suggestion)

- Do not tell people what to believe or do ('you should believe you can grow faster', 'go rewrite your scripts now'). Show the story, data and steps and let them conclude.
- Commands raise the guard and feel like homework.

### The payoff must be real

- Kallaway: a great setup with no compelling payoff makes people churn and makes you 'a fraud'.
- pmitu (16 rules): headlines and hooks should be strong but accurate, and the content must deliver the promise.

### Story sentences (Humm, Hoyos)

- Humm: name the location briefly, use action verbs, quote raw thoughts, show emotion through the body, use exact juicy dialogue. Avoid professional-sounding thoughts and stiff dialogue.
- Hoyos (TED 60-second story): keep it short and concise, under 60 seconds.

### Frameworks

**Contrast line recipes** (Kallaway)

1. Everyone thinks X, but actually it's Y
2. If you want X, don't do Y, do Z
3. X used to be the gold standard, but now Y makes X obsolete
4. You've been told X your whole life. Turns out X is wrong
5. Lead with the benefit or pain, then add the contrast

### Examples

- **Whisperer versus town crier:** Whisperer: 'Okay, so not that many people know about this yet...' Town crier: 'Huge news. This changes everything.'
  <br>*Why it works:* Kallaway prefers the whisperer. Calm, committed and honest.
- **Bad commands:** 'You should believe you can grow faster' and 'go rewrite your scripts now'.
  <br>*Why it works:* Kallaway: show the story and steps instead, and let people conclude.

### Common mistakes

- Hedging or downplaying your own story
- Shouting 'huge news' with fake urgency
- Telling people what to believe or do instead of showing
- Writing a hook the content cannot pay off

### Where the sources disagree

Provocation limits: Koe says stand firm and pick a side, you will get hate. pmitu says controversial is fine but irrelevant, annoying or misleading is not. 0xfJuan adds: pick a take you can defend with evidence.

### Try it

Write one hook with the 'Everyone thinks X, but actually it's Y' recipe, then check it: is there a real payoff in the body, and is any line a command that should be a shown story?

## 11.9 Persuasive copy: Koe's six elements and headlines

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Dan Koe says persuasive content captures, holds and delivers value on attention, and gives six boxes to check. Other creators add rules for headlines, bullets, product titles and promises.

### The six elements of persuasive content (Dan Koe)

- Koe: persuasive means it captures, holds and delivers value on attention. Check at least 3 or 4 of the boxes in every piece at the start. With skill you make your own rules. Save the list somewhere safe.
- 1\. Hook: will the first sentence grab attention? Words, structure and visual design of the post all count.
- 2\. A big problem: imply or state a problem people relate to. Not too high level.
- 3\. A clear solution: an actionable way to overcome it, preferably a unique way from personal experience.
- 4\. A big benefit: imply or state a desirable benefit they'll get.
- 5\. Confidence or polarization: stand firm, pick a side, be willing to change. You will get hate.
- 6\. A novel perspective or big idea: an explanation that isn't normal, which gives a dopamine hit and advances understanding.

### Articulation (Koe)

- Train articulation with high-performing structures as training wheels (the anomaly exercise).
- When you can articulate an idea engagingly and want to write about it, everything becomes a source of ideas.

### Headlines, bullets and promises

- Hussain Ibarra: good headlines sell the outcome, not the product.
- Hussain: bullets are what they will learn plus the benefit.
- Koe: title products to spark desire, not describe the category. 'Writing Course' is his bad example.
- Koe: present ideas as options. List benefits, show low risk, make a compelling argument and let the reader choose.

### Copy craft from the articles

- Shann Holmberg: rewrite AI openings. The generic default is wrong.
- EXM7777: rewrite the landing page headline yourself even if AI writes the page.
- EXM7777: write ad stories the way you would tell them to a friend first.
- EXM7777: the first line of a sales email should be about them, tied to something that just happened at their company.
- beechinour: a script nobody would say out loud feels off.

### Frameworks

**Koe's six elements checklist** (Dan Koe)

1. Hook
2. A big problem
3. A clear solution
4. A big benefit
5. Confidence or polarization
6. A novel perspective or big idea

### Examples

- **Koe, bad product title:** 'Writing Course' only describes the category.
  <br>*Why it works:* Koe: title it to spark desire instead.
- **Koe, believable outcomes:** 'Build your first website in 30 days' and 'lose your first 10 pounds'.
  <br>*Why it works:* Specific and believable inside the timeframe.

### Common mistakes

- Describing the product category instead of the outcome
- Stating a huge vague promise nobody believes
- Checking none of the six boxes
- Letting AI write the headline and not rewriting it

### Try it

Take one draft post and tick Koe's six boxes. If fewer than 3 or 4 are ticked, add a problem, a clear solution or a benefit before posting.

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet says content is a conversation, not a presentation. Talk to one person, lower your tone, use outlines not scripts, and treat authenticity as a skill that compounds.

### A conversation, not a presentation (Sam)

- People watch and scroll alone, on a couch or at a computer, not in a group around one phone. Talk to one person.
- Top creators say 'you', never 'you guys', 'hey everyone', 'you all' or 'y'all'. Group words turn it into a performance. His example: Obama's 2008 victory speech says 'you'.
- His friend Ronnie: some content speaks to everyone, some speaks to one person. Speak to one.
- Lower your tone. 'Content creators think they need to MrBeast themselves', and people see through it. Talk to the camera like to a friend: lower your energy, do not scream, do not rush, pause when you need to.
- Educators do not need MrBeast energy. People are done with 'putting on a front' (Yap Method).

### No upspeak, slow down, use pauses (Storyteller)

- Upspeak is ending sentences going up. It sounds unsure. End sentences low and it sounds like authority.
- Slow down and let pauses happen. Nerves make you 'word vomit' value. Pauses give people time to take it in and pull them in.

### Outlines, not scripts

- Sam calls his 'scripts' but they are outlines ('calling them scripts is a disservice'): notes off to the side, never read word for word.
- Write it yourself: scripts by hand, AI only to widen options.

### The off the dome method (Sam, Brand Training)

- 1\. Write the outline, ideally the same week so it is fresh.
- 2\. Remember it: read it before you shoot, like actors learn their lines.
- 3\. Riff: let yourself go on small tangents. That is where authentic delivery comes from.
- 4\. Repeat: if you are bad at delivering something, you just have not said it enough times. Dan has said his points hundreds of times to his team and on stage. 'No one's a natural.' Your authenticity compounds.

### Authenticity is a skill, and yapping builds it

- Being on camera is not natural at first. Do it enough and talking to the camera feels like talking to a friend (50 Hacks #27, #9).
- 'Ignore your comments': the less you care what people say, the more you make what you believe, and that is when it sounds real.
- Yap Method: the biggest benefit of yapping was not the 50K followers, it was getting better at explaining ideas in meetings and on camera. 'Those reps you cannot replace.' Hard at first, different over 3 to 12 months.
- Sam calls himself 'pretty bland and super stoic' on camera and still grows, because he is convinced of what he says.

### Frameworks

**Off the dome method** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Write the outline the same week
2. Remember it before you shoot
3. Riff on small tangents
4. Repeat until it comes out naturally

### Examples

- **Trail, not rail:** A train stays on the rail. A hiker wanders to the edges of the trail but always comes back to the middle.
  <br>*Why it works:* Sam's picture for following an outline loosely.
- **Obama, 2008:** The victory speech says 'you'.
  <br>*Why it works:* Sam's example of speaking to one person.

### Common mistakes

- Saying 'you guys' or 'hey everyone'
- Reading a script word for word
- Ending sentences with upspeak
- Copying MrBeast energy when you are an educator
- Rushing instead of pausing

### Where the sources disagree

Comments: Sam says 'ignore your comments' so you make what you really believe. He notes this is in tension with replying to comments, which other sources in this course recommend.

### Try it

Write a 5-bullet outline for one video, read it once, put it away, and film it in one take. Repeat 3 times and compare.

## 11.13 Explain simply, and keep AI sounding human

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam and Ryan agree: people can tell when content is not authentic. Use AI as a tool, not the writer, and teach it to avoid AI tells.

### Clear over clever when explaining (Sam)

- Ryan (Nov 4 list): master copywriting means explaining hard topics in few words. Start with one skill (writing or presenting), learn both later.
- Use the words people actually use (Sam's self-articulated words, see the earlier lesson).

### Human stands out (Ryan)

- Ryan: 'AI slop is everywhere, so human stands out.'
- Ryan: minimal use of AI until you are good. Consume good content to build taste.
- His X Writer prompt ends with 'avoid anything that sounds like AI', and he still writes most copy by hand.

### Teach AI to speak human (Sam)

- Audiences can tell when you are not authentic. Do not paste a ChatGPT viral script into a teleprompter.
- 'Make me a viral video' gives you nothing. Break down what you mean: ask for novel, non-obvious ideas, avoid em dashes, and use contractions ('what's' and 'they'll', not 'what is' and 'they all').
- Sam's idea of using AI: only to widen options, writing the script yourself by hand.

### AI tells and checks (articles)

- XCreators (Grok Bot guide): have AI check 'This doesn't sound like you' in drafts.
- domschats and the openings study: 'not written by AI' as an explicit trust line.
- Kallaway: his clarity prompt asks AI for the same meaning, only clearer, at a sixth grade level. The idea stays yours.

### Frameworks

**Sam's human AI prompt rules** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Break down what you mean instead of 'make it viral'
2. Ask for novel, non-obvious ideas
3. Tell it to avoid em dashes
4. Tell it to use contractions
5. Rewrite the opening yourself

### Examples

- **Contractions:** 'What's' and 'they'll', not 'what is' and 'they all'.
  <br>*Why it works:* Sam's example of making AI sound spoken.

### Common mistakes

- Pasting an AI script straight into a teleprompter
- Prompting 'make me a viral video' with nothing else
- Keeping the generic AI opening
- Using big terms to sound smart

### Where the sources disagree

How much AI: Ryan says minimal use until you are good and still writes most copy by hand. Sam is fine using AI to widen options, but not to write the script. Neither suggests the AI writes the final words.

## Cheat sheet: Writing and copywriting

The whole module on one page.

- Good copy gets the right person to take the right action (Ryan).
- Write for one specific reader, with one idea and one takeaway per post.
- Clarity over cleverness: smart ideas, simple words (12 year old test, Ryan).
- Active voice, fewer words, no jargon (Kallaway).
- Test every key line: can it be read two ways? Rewrite until it cannot.
- Delete any word that does not change the meaning.
- Swap vague words for emotional, specific ones (Mino Lee).
- Use numbers, names, timeframes and proof, not 'a few weeks'.
- Use the exact words your audience says (Sam, Codie).
- Name your concepts so people can remember, repeat and reference them.
- Open fast with the point and proof. Close with the takeaway, a question or a tease.
- One thought per line, paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines, preview on your phone.
- Think 'you' before 'I'.
- Build contrast with 'but', 'however' and 'everyone thinks X, but actually Y'.
- Commit to the story and whisper, do not shout. The payoff must be real.
- Show, do not command.
- Check 3 or 4 of Koe's six elements in every piece.
- One soft CTA matching the topic. No engagement bait.
- Write like you talk. Voice is the part nobody can copy.
- Speak to one person, use an outline not a script, and keep AI from writing the final words.
