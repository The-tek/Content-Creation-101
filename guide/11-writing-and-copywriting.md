# Module 11: Writing and copywriting

Write clear, specific, human words that get the right reader to take the right action

Good copywriting gets the right person to take the right action. You will learn to write for one reader with one idea and one takeaway (the one useful thing the reader leaves with), to use simple, specific words your audience already uses, and to open, close and shape a post. You will also learn six checks for persuasive posts, soft CTAs (the line that tells people what to do next) and how to keep your own voice, on camera and with AI

## Lesson 11.1: What good copy actually is

### Big idea

Copywriting means writing words that get the right person to do the right thing. Each post needs one reader, one idea and one takeaway (the one useful thing the reader leaves with)

### How it works

#### Good copy gets someone to act

Good copywriting gets the right person to take the right action. The action can be reading to the end, replying, sharing, following or buying

Before you post, ask what you want the reader to do

It starts with good thinking. If the idea is weak, nicer words won't save it

A strong idea in plain words does better than a weak idea in fancy ones

#### Write for one specific reader

Before you post, picture the one person you are writing for. Ask what they struggle with right now, where they read, what they want and how they feel today

A post for everyone feels like it is for nobody. The closer a post is to a sale, the more exact the reader should be

#### One idea per post

If you have three ideas, you have three posts. Two ideas in one post confuse the reader

Say you write about how to write hooks and also about the best time to post. Make it two posts

#### Every post needs a takeaway

A takeaway is one clear thing the reader can use or remember. Before you post, ask what the reader walks away with

Aim for posts where every line is something the reader didn't know and can use today. Get these basics right (a specific reader, one idea, a takeaway, plain words) and your writing is already better than most

### Mistakes

- putting two or three ideas in one post
- posting without knowing who the post is for
- writing a post with no clear point
- hoping nice wording will fix a weak idea

### Try it

Take your last 3 posts. For each one, write down who it was for, what the one idea was and what the reader walks away with

If a post had two ideas, split it into two drafts

## Lesson 11.2: Clear over clever: smart ideas, simple words

### Big idea

If people don't understand a post, nothing else about it works. Keep your ideas smart, but write the words and sentences so simply that a child could follow

### How it works

#### Write at a simple reading level

A good test: if a 12 year old can't read it, don't publish it. Your ideas don't have to be simple, but your words and sentences should be

Aim for about a 6th grade reading level. Free reading level checkers online will score your text

The best posts aren't the smartest ones. They are the ones people can understand

#### Why simple words work better

The less effort it takes to get your point, the more people finish the post and share it. If people don't get it, a great hook and great timing won't help

A real expert turns a hard topic into a few simple rules. A beginner finally understands it, and the expert understands it even better

Once you know something well, it seems too obvious to explain. But the simple version of an obvious idea is often what reaches the most people

#### Being clear comes first

Whatever style you choose, the reader must always know what you believe and what to do next. Style is extra

Being clear is required

In a post that teaches, give very clear steps from the problem to the solution, so the path is obvious. The same goes for your bio: make it clear, not clever

#### Say hard points twice

When a point is hard, say it twice. First say it the way you would to a coworker

Then say it again with a simple comparison a child would get

Make the main idea small enough that someone can repeat it after one read

### Examples

Jargon and a simple version:

Jargon: "yield a positive ROI". Simple: "get back more money than you spent"

Why it works: The simple version is the one a beginner understands on the first read

### Mistakes

- writing to sound smart instead of to be understood
- thinking complicated means valuable
- using jargon (special words only experts know)
- skipping an explanation because it feels too obvious to you

### Try it

Take one paragraph you wrote recently and paste it into a free reading level checker. Rewrite it until it scores 6th grade or lower, and keep the idea

Then add a one-line comparison a child would understand for its hardest point

## Lesson 11.3: Make every sentence clear

### Big idea

If a reader can't tell what your first line means, they leave. Clear sentences come from a few habits: fewer words, active voice (someone does something), the main point first and a test for lines that can be read two ways

### How it works

#### Why confusion costs you readers

A confusing first line names the topic, but the words are hard to follow. The reader gets half of it and can't tell if it's for them

The fix is better writing. Use fewer and simpler words, but keep enough that nobody can misread you

Use active voice, where someone does something. "The dog jumped" is clear. "The jump of the dog" is slower to read

#### Put the main point first

The same facts in a different order can be much easier to understand. Put the main point first and the detail after

Short lines are easier to read, because the brain can only hold a little at once. A line of 8 words is usually clearer than one of 28

#### The two-meanings test

Take your one or two most important sentences and read each on its own. Ask: could someone understand this the wrong way?

1. If yes, rewrite it until only one reading is left
2. Skip wordplay and double meanings in key lines
3. Read it out loud. If you stumble, your reader will too

#### Jargon and names

Don't use a term your reader doesn't know, unless you show what it means. Name things in a way nobody can misread

You can ask an AI tool to make a line clearer. Tell it to keep the same meaning and only make it simpler

### Examples

A first line, rewritten:

Before: "The online money they made is most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice like they did." After: "Their secret for earning money online was an intense journaling practice."

Why it works: Same facts. The new version has simpler words and a better order, so you get it on the first read

### Mistakes

- cutting so many words that the line can be misread
- using wordplay with a double meaning in a key line
- opening with jargon the reader has never seen
- publishing without reading the line out loud

### Try it

Write the first line of your next post. Show it to someone with no context and ask what it means. Then rewrite it shorter, in active voice and with the main point at the start, and keep the clearest

## Lesson 11.4: Every word earns its place

### Big idea

One rule cleans up most writing: if taking out a word doesn't change the meaning, take it out. Then use short words and strong verbs, and swap broad words for specific ones

### How it works

#### The delete test

Go through your draft one word at a time. Ask: if I delete this word, does the meaning change?

If not, delete it

Cut filler words like really, very, just and actually. Cut soft phrases like "kind of" and "I think", because they make you sound unsure

If a line says the same thing twice, keep the better half

#### Short words and strong verbs

Use simple words: say "use" instead of "employ" and "start" instead of "commence"

One strong verb can replace three weak words. "Grew really quickly" becomes "exploded"

Use active voice. "Mistakes were made" hides who did it

"Most creators make this mistake" says it

#### Pick specific words people can feel

One or two well chosen words can change how many people stop and read. Swap vague words for specific ones: "alone" instead of "by yourself", "going broke" instead of "money trouble", "10K a month" instead of "a good income"

Avoid broad words like "get better" or "improve". They don't give the reader a picture

Once you upgrade a word, remove what became extra. If you write "AI agents", you no longer need "with AI"

### Examples

A picture instead of a promise:

"There by Friday" instead of "fast delivery"

Why it works: The buyer can picture a day on the calendar. They can't picture "fast"

### Mistakes

- filler words like really, very, just, actually
- soft phrases like "kind of" and "I think"
- broad words like "improve" or "get better"
- fancy words where a short one works

### Try it

Take a draft of about 150 words. Delete every filler word and soft phrase, then run the delete test on the rest

Replace one vague word with a specific one and compare the word count before and after

## Lesson 11.5: Be specific: numbers, names and proof

### Big idea

Vague lines give the reader nothing to picture. Numbers, names, timeframes and real proof make a line believable and make people want to read on

### How it works

#### Vague or specific

"I grew fast" is weak. "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month" is better, because the number and the timeframe make it believable

Use numbers, results and details wherever you can, and only use ones that are true

A test: if a stranger could have written your line, it's generic. Add something only you could say

#### Empty teasers

Teasers like "something crazy happened" or "you won't believe this" give the reader nothing. Give enough detail that they can guess what's coming

Say "within 30 minutes, she tells me something that completely changes my strategy for the next 6 months" instead

#### What specific proof looks like

- A number with its unit: followers, dollars, days
- A before and after, with a timeframe: "from 2 to 15,000 followers in 6 weeks"
- A name where it helps: a person, a company or a tool

#### Believable results and exact spots

Make a promised result believable for its timeframe: "build your first website in 30 days". A promise that is too big for the timeframe makes people stop trusting you

Say exactly how many spots are left. "4 slots, 2 are gone" is believable

"Limited spots" is not

### Examples

A vague claim and a specific one:

"I grew fast" versus "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month"

Why it works: A number and a timeframe make the claim clear and believable

### Mistakes

- empty teasers like "you won't believe this"
- round, vague promises like "grow faster"
- promises too big to believe in the timeframe
- "limited spots" instead of an exact number

### Try it

Find 3 vague lines in your recent writing (look for words like fast, a lot, huge, something). Rewrite each one with a number, a timeframe or a name, using only true facts

## Lesson 11.6: Use the words your audience already uses

### Big idea

The best words are the ones your readers would say themselves. Collect their exact phrases, write the way people talk to a friend and give your ideas short names others can repeat

### How it works

#### Use the words your reader would use

Say things the way your reader would say them. "Make more money" reaches many more people than "make more revenue"

Start with the result people want, like more money or more free time, and talk about how to get it later

#### Collect your audience's phrases

Write the way people talk to a friend. "Stop wasting my whole day on my phone" feels real, while "develop better habits" sounds like a brochure

Keep a problem bank: copy problems from comments, replies and DMs word for word. "I don't have the money and I'm scared I'll look stupid asking" is better than "capital constraints"

Use those exact phrases in your hooks and posts. Repeating someone's own words builds trust

#### Power words: borrow them, then change them

A power word is a strong phrase that already works in top content, like "scientifically impossible to skip"

Don't copy it exactly. Change it a little, like "psychologically impossible to resist", so it becomes yours

#### Name your ideas so people can repeat them

Give your main ideas short names that are easy to remember and easy to repeat. When a name sticks, other people spread your idea for you

Give your steps a name too, so they read like a system. A system is easier to sell than a pile of loose tips

Only name things you really teach

### Examples

Talk like a friend:

"Stop wasting my whole day on my phone" instead of "develop better habits"

Why it works: It's what people say out loud to a friend, so it feels like their own thought

### Mistakes

- changing your audience's pain into expert terms like "capital constraints"
- copying a power word phrase exactly instead of changing it a little
- naming things you don't actually teach

### Try it

Spend 10 minutes reading comments under 3 top posts in your niche. Copy 10 phrases people use to describe their problem, word for word

Then rewrite one of your hooks using one of those exact phrases

## Lesson 11.7: Open, close and shape a post

### Big idea

A good post gets to the point fast, puts one thought on each line, keeps paragraphs short and ends with a clear last line. Write about "you" before "I"

### How it works

#### How to open a post

Skip the warm-up and get to the point. Show your proof early so the reader trusts you

Use "you" and "your" more than "I" and "me". "You can grow your account" pulls people in more than "I grew my account"

An "I" line can still work, like "If I had to grow from 0 to 100K...", because the reader pictures themselves doing the same

#### How to close a post

End with the takeaway in 1 or 2 lines, a question or a hint of what comes next

Don't let the post fade away. The last line is the second most important line, after the first

#### Make it easy on the eyes

People skip big blocks of text, so put one thought on each line. If a sentence has two "and"s and a comma, it is probably two sentences

Keep paragraphs to 1 to 3 lines. On a phone, 4 lines look like an essay

Mix short and long sentences so the post has a good rhythm. Preview the post on your phone before you publish

### Examples

The two-ands rule:

Say you write: "I tried posting every day and I got more followers and I also learned what my audience wants." Split it into three short lines

Why it works: Each line now holds one thought and is easier to read on a phone

### Mistakes

- starting with a warm-up before the point
- big blocks of text and paragraphs of 4 lines
- fading away at the end with no takeaway
- writing about "I" when the post should be about the reader

### Try it

Take one finished post. Split every sentence that has two "and"s, keep paragraphs to 3 lines or fewer and rewrite the last line as a takeaway, a question or a hint of what's next

Then preview it on your phone

## Lesson 11.8: Contrast, tension and an honest tone

### Big idea

Small words like "but" create contrast, and contrast makes people curious. Keep it honest: stay calm, show the story and steps instead of giving orders, and always deliver what you promised

### How it works

#### Contrast: the "but" move

Contrast means putting two opposite things next to each other. It makes people curious how both can be true

Words that create it: but, however, yet, although

A simple pattern: "Everyone thinks X, but actually it's Y"

You can also start with the benefit or pain: "If you struggle with acne...". The right readers stop right away

#### Stay calm and stand behind your story

Believe in your story. Don't make it small with lines like "of course this is just basic biology", because that kills the curiosity you just built

Be the friend who lowers their voice to share a secret: "Okay, so not that many people know about this yet...". Don't shout "Huge news

This changes everything."

Don't invent urgency. If nothing is happening now, don't say it is

#### Show people, don't give orders

Don't tell people what to believe or do. Show the story, the data and the steps, and let them reach their own conclusion

Orders put people on guard and feel like homework

#### The payoff must be real

A great opening with a weak ending makes people leave, and they trust you less. They feel tricked

Make hooks strong but true, and make sure the post really gives what the hook promised

#### Tell stories with detail

Name the place in a few words, use action verbs, quote the real thoughts you had and show emotion through the body, like "my hands were shaking"

Use the exact words people said. Avoid stiff, professional-sounding lines

### Examples

A calm voice and a shouting voice:

Calm: "Okay, so not that many people know about this yet..." Shouting: "Huge news. This changes everything."

Why it works: The calm version feels honest and sure of itself. The loud one feels like an ad

### Mistakes

- making your own story smaller than it is
- shouting "huge news" with fake urgency
- telling people what to believe or do instead of showing them
- writing a hook the post can't live up to

### Try it

Write one hook with the "Everyone thinks X, but actually it's Y" pattern. Then check: does the post really deliver on it, and is any line an order that should be a story?

## Lesson 11.9: Persuasive copy: six checks and better headlines

### Big idea

Persuasive content grabs attention, holds it and gives value for it. Six simple checks help you build that, and a few rules help with headlines and promises

### How it works

#### The six checks

While you are learning, tick at least 3 or 4 of these six boxes in every piece:

1. Hook: will the first sentence grab attention?
2. A big problem: say or hint at a problem your reader knows well
3. A clear solution: a way to solve it that people can act on
4. A big benefit: what they get if it works
5. Confidence: pick a side, and be ready to change your mind if you are proven wrong
6. A fresh view: an explanation that gives a small "oh, I never saw it like that" moment

#### Practise putting ideas into words

Copy the structure of posts that already did well, like training wheels on a bike, and fill it with your own ideas

Once you can explain an idea in a way people enjoy, everything you see becomes a source of post ideas

#### Headlines and promises

A good headline sells the result, not the product. Bullet lists should say what the reader will learn and what they get from it

Give products names that make people want them. "Writing Course" only says what it is

Present your offer as an option: list the benefits, show that the risk is low and let the reader choose

#### If AI writes your copy

The default AI opening sounds like everyone else's, so rewrite it. Write the headline yourself, even if AI writes the rest

Read your copy out loud. If nobody would say a line out loud, fix it

### Examples

A weak product name:

"Writing Course" only describes the type of product

Why it works: A name that points at the result, like "Write a post people actually finish", would make people want it more (made-up example)

### Mistakes

- describing the product instead of the result
- making a huge, vague promise nobody believes
- ticking none of the six checks
- letting AI write the headline and not rewriting it

### Try it

Take one draft post and tick off the six checks. If you ticked fewer than 3 or 4, add a problem, a clear solution or a benefit before you post

## Lesson 11.10: CTAs and persuasion in every format

### Big idea

Persuasion isn't only for posts. Use it in replies, DMs, emails and videos, and write like a friend. A CTA should be soft, clear and tied to your content, and never a trick to get likes

### How it works

#### Persuade everywhere you write

Persuasion applies to posts, replies, quote posts, DMs, emails and videos. Write DMs like a friend would, not like a robot from a big company

In replies and quote posts, don't repeat the original post. Add your own experience or a new angle

#### Keep your CTA soft and single

A soft CTA is a casual mention of what you offer, with a clear result, no pressure and no countdown. In a video, use one main CTA that points to a free resource matching the topic

Be clear on what you sell and what the person should do. Mention your offer plainly and once

#### CTAs that work in long articles

- a light one early on, like "a like and repost go a long way"
- an ask at the end to follow and repost
- a keyword ask like "DM me LAUNCH", paired with a clear offer
- an open invitation like "drop any questions below"

#### Skip engagement bait

Engagement bait is a trick to get likes and replies, like "like if you agree", "comment yes" or "tag 3 friends". It brings the wrong audience and feels cheap

### Examples

A soft CTA:

"If you want to work with me on this, fill out this quick questionnaire and I'll reach out."

Why it works: It is friendly, has one clear step and no pressure. Place it in the middle and at the end of a newsletter

### Mistakes

- engagement bait like "comment yes"
- several different CTAs in one video
- a CTA that doesn't match the topic
- DMs that sound like a robot from a big company

### Try it

Choose the one CTA for your next piece. Make sure it matches the topic, then write it as one friendly line with a clear result and no countdown

## Lesson 11.11: Voice: write like you talk

### Big idea

Anyone can copy your format and your hooks, but your voice is much harder to copy. Write like you talk, share real opinions and tighten your words after the first rough draft

### How it works

#### Write the way you talk

The best way to write is the way you talk in real life. With so much AI writing around, a real voice stands out more than ever

A voice is made of a few things: opinions, the same tone from post to post, personality, your own experiences and your quirks. Short forms like "ASAP" and slang are fine if that is how you talk

#### Be yourself so you can keep it up

Don't play a character you aren't. You will get tired of it, and readers can tell

If you don't know your style yet, copy the style of someone you like for a while, then change it step by step until it becomes yours

Write things you actually believe, because your content shows who you are

#### Write loose first, then tighten

Write the first draft without worrying about quality or length. Then cut it down into shorter, punchier lines

When you edit, aim for fewer words, simpler words and the language of a 10 year old

#### Be bold, within limits

If you try to please everyone, your writing turns boring. Imagine writing an email to a close friend instead of picturing people who dislike you

A strong opinion is fine, but being irrelevant, annoying or misleading is not. Only take a stand you can back up with evidence

#### Taste

Taste is knowing what is good and what is not, and you can't fake it. You build it by reading and watching lots of top content, creating your own, failing and trying again

Keep a swipe file, a folder of posts and ads you admire. Re-create what worked in your own words, and never copy

### Mistakes

- playing a character you can't keep up
- copying someone's voice instead of learning from their moves
- saying things only to upset people
- polishing before the first rough draft exists

### Try it

Read your last post aloud. Mark every line you would never say to a friend and rewrite it in your own words

Then make it shorter by cutting

## Lesson 11.12: Speak to one person: how to talk on camera

### Big idea

Content is a conversation, not a presentation. Talk to one person, use a calm voice and an outline instead of a script, and practise, because sounding natural on camera is a skill you build

### How it works

#### A conversation, not a presentation

People watch alone, on a couch or at a computer. So talk to one person and say "you", not "you guys", "hey everyone" or "y'all". Words for a group turn it into a performance

Use a calm voice, like you would with a friend: no screaming, no rushing, and pause when you need to. Viewers can see through fake high energy

#### Slow down, end low, use pauses

Upspeak is when your voice goes up at the end of a sentence. It sounds unsure. End sentences on a lower note and you sound like you know what you are talking about

When you're nervous, you pour out information too fast. Slow down and let pauses happen, so people have time to take it in

#### Use an outline, not a script

An outline is a few notes you glance at and never read word for word. Reading word for word makes people sound stiff

Write the outline yourself, by hand if you can. Use AI only to give you more options to choose from

#### The no-script method

1. Write the outline in the same week you film, so it is fresh
2. Read it before you shoot, like actors learning lines
3. Talk freely and let yourself go on small side topics. That is where natural delivery comes from
4. Repeat. If you're bad at delivering something, you probably haven't said it enough times

#### Natural delivery is a skill

Being on camera feels strange at first. Do it enough and it feels like talking to a friend

Try to ignore your comments while you create. The less you worry about what people say, the more you say what you believe, and that is when you sound real

### Examples

One person, not a crowd:

Say "if you're stuck on your first post" instead of "hey guys, if any of you are stuck"

Why it works: It feels like you are talking to one person

### Mistakes

- saying "you guys" or "hey everyone"
- reading a script word for word
- ending sentences with your voice going up
- rushing instead of pausing

### Try it

Write an outline of 5 bullets for one video, read it once, put it away and film it in one take. Do this 3 times and compare the takes

## Lesson 11.13: Explain simply, and keep AI sounding human

### Big idea

People can tell when content isn't real. Use AI as a helper and not as the writer, and tell it what to avoid so it doesn't sound like AI

### How it works

#### Explain hard things in a few clear words

A good writer can explain a hard topic in a few words. Use the words your audience actually uses

#### Human writing stands out

AI writing is everywhere now, so writing that sounds human stands out

Use AI as little as you can until you're a good writer. Build your taste first by reading and watching a lot of good content

#### Teach AI to sound human

"Make me a viral video" gives you nothing useful. Be specific about what you want

1. Ask for new ideas that are not the obvious ones
2. Ban em dashes (the long dash that AI loves)
3. Ask for short forms like "what's" and "they'll" instead of "what is" and "they will"
4. Rewrite the opening yourself

The safest way to use AI: let it give you more options, and write the final words yourself

#### Check for signs that AI wrote it

Ask AI to check your draft with the question: "Where does this not sound like me?"

To make a line clearer, ask AI to say the same thing at a 6th grade level. The idea stays yours

### Examples

Short forms:

"What's" and "they'll" instead of "what is" and "they will"

Why it works: These short forms are how people speak, so they make AI text sound spoken

### Mistakes

- pasting an AI script straight into a teleprompter (a screen that shows you your words while you film)
- asking AI "make me a viral video" and nothing else
- keeping the generic AI opening
- using big words to sound smart

### Try it

Paste a draft into your AI tool with these rules (new ideas, no em dashes, short forms like "what's"). Then rewrite the opening yourself and read it aloud
