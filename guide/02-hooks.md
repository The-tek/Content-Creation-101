## 2.1 What a hook does and why it decides everything

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A hook has one job: help the viewer decide to stop and keep watching or reading. Kallaway says it only needs two things, topic clarity and on-target curiosity.

### The one job of a hook

- Most of this module follows Kallaway's 62-minute masterclass "How to create hooks better than 99% of people". His own credentials as he states them (his claims): 2 million followers, "billions of views", content is all he does all day, and he reviews hundreds of business owners' videos every week. The masterclass runs in this order: his three-step formula plus five fast tips, the six hook formats, the four hook components and the key visual, the six power words, and the four biggest hook mistakes.
- Kallaway: a hook has one job, to help the viewer decide to opt in and keep watching. Stop the scroll, then get them to stay.
- It only needs to give two things. Topic clarity: it is crystal clear what the video is about. On-target curiosity: the viewer believes it is for them, that it will give them value, and they are curious what comes next. Kallaway: do both and "I guarantee the viewer will be hooked."
- Kallaway's highway picture: the viewer is driving at 70 mph. The hook must make them see something, stop, turn around and come back the other way.
- A hook is usually 2 to 4 lines, not one punchy line (Kallaway). Topic clarity often sits in sentence 1 and the contrast in the next 1 or 2. When both fit in one line, those are the easiest hooks to reuse.
- Sam Gaudet: a hook is just the first few words. The first words out of your mouth, the first words of an email or of a tweet.
- Mino Lee calls the hook the first 3 seconds of a video. Logan Smith calls it the first 5 seconds of a short and "the most important part of your content".

### Why hooks carry so much weight

- Kallaway's claim: becoming a master at hooks is the single biggest lever for performance. "Hooks are the 80 of the 80/20 in the content flow."
- Mino Lee's claim: the hook is about 80% of why a video gets 1,000 views instead of 100,000, not 50/50 with the rest. If you are stuck under 1,000 views and you are tweaking music, camera, keywords or hashtags, you are looking at the wrong things.
- heyDominik's claim: the single biggest reason reels and short videos don't perform is bad hooks. They crush retention, make people swipe before they know what is going on "and make the algorithm hate you".
- Ryan (ryangrowthh): a post has 3 parts, hook, body (the promise) and CTA, and the "hook does 80% of the heavy lifting". He says top creators "obsess over hooks/packaging more than the content itself" and tells people to put more effort into the hook than the post body.
- Ryan also says most people spend 95% of their time on the content and 5% on packaging, when it should be closer to 50/50.

### The retention graph: why the first seconds matter

- Mino Lee's core principle is the retention graph: the share of viewers still watching at 5, 10, 15 and 20 seconds.
- If your video only gets interesting at second 10, you may already be down to about 10% of viewers. Even if the curve flattens after that, the platform sees that 90% left because the start was dull.
- Mino: "The rest of your video does not matter at all if you cannot capture their attention within those first 5 seconds."
- Kallaway: short-form retention is exponential decay. The first two seconds drop "like a cliff". Every second you go without saying what the video is about, a large share of viewers leave.

### The viewer is on autopilot

- heyDominik: people swipe on autopilot, with muscle memory, until something hits their brain and it goes "hmm, that's interesting". It happens so fast that you must build hooks that hit those triggers as fast as possible, for your target audience.
- heyDominik's claim from his own research: many viewers swipe past, the brain processes it late, and they swipe BACK. He says this seems to be a strong positive signal to the algorithm.
- Kallaway's interrupt theory (from a separate hooks video): the feed is a highway and the thumb is on autopilot. A hook must cause a "freeze point". The neuroscience term is an orienting response, the involuntary "wait, what was that?" you would have if you saw a wolf in your backyard.
- Science check: the orienting response is a real, well-studied reflex (Sokolov, 1960s).

### Kallaway's 4 S's: what a hook must answer

- To freeze someone, the hook answers 2 questions: what is this about (subject) and what do I get if I keep watching (stakes).
- Subject: crystal clear who or what it is about.
- Stakes: a reason to care. For education content that is the outcome they want: views, clients, money, time.
- Speed: subject and stakes in as few words as possible. Kallaway calls it "compression, not velocity": 8 words beat 28, because the brain only holds so much.
- Super clear: only one possible reading, no wordplay.

### Attention spans: a myth worth knowing

- The famous "8-second goldfish attention span" stat traces back to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report that credited a website called Statistic Brain. A BBC journalist found no research behind it.
- Attention researchers (for example Michael Posner) say there is no evidence human attention has changed since the late 1800s.
- The research bank's reading: people don't have short attention spans, they have short patience for boring content. What changed is competition: endless options, so people leave faster. That is why every expert in this module says the hook decides.

### Frameworks

**The two jobs of a hook** (Kallaway)

1. Topic clarity: the viewer knows exactly what this is about, so the right people opt in and the wrong ones leave
2. On-target curiosity: the viewer believes it is for them, will give them value, and wants to know what comes next

**The 4 S's (interrupt theory)** (Kallaway)

1. Subject: crystal clear who or what
2. Stakes: the outcome they get if they keep watching
3. Speed: subject and stakes in as few words as possible
4. Super clear: only one possible reading

### Examples

- **Mino Lee, the 10-second problem:** A video that only gets interesting at second 10 may have lost about 90% of viewers by then. Even if the rest holds well, the platform has already seen most people leave.
  <br>*Why it works:* The platform judges the start, so the start must earn the rest.
- **heyDominik, the swipe back:** Viewers swipe past a video, their brain catches up a moment later, and they swipe back to it.
  <br>*Why it works:* His claim: this looks like a strong positive signal to the algorithm, and it only happens when something in the first frame hits a trigger.

### Common mistakes

- Blaming music, camera, keywords or hashtags when the real problem is the first seconds (Mino Lee).
- Relying on gimmick hook advice ("use this zoom effect", "jump up and down") without understanding why videos work. Mino Lee rejects this.
- Thinking a hook must be one punchy line. Kallaway says it is usually 2 to 4 lines.
- Spending almost all your time on the body and almost none on the packaging (Ryan).

### Where the sources disagree

How much the hook matters. Kallaway ("the 80 of the 80/20"), Mino Lee (about 80% of the result) and Ryan ("80% of the heavy lifting") put the hook at the top. Sam Gaudet's "pyramid of importance" puts topic above hooks: all else equal, a better topic wins, and hooks and formats sit lower down. Caleb Ralston says the biggest brands don't blow up because of titles, thumbnails and hooks but because of a fundamentally different (contrarian) way of seeing their niche, and that none of the "steal this hook" advice matters without a strong foundation.

### Try it

Open your last 5 posts or videos. For each one, write down only the first line (or first 3 seconds). Next to it, answer Kallaway's two questions in a few words: what is this about, and why would my ideal viewer care? Mark any hook where you can't answer both. Those are your first rewrites.

## 2.2 Kallaway's master formula: lean, stun, snap back

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Kallaway's mega tip is a three-step hook formula: a context lean, a scroll stop interjection and a contrarian snapback. It builds a curiosity loop where every line makes the next one feel necessary.

### The curiosity loop underneath it

- Kallaway calls the curiosity loop one of the base commandments of storytelling: line 1 must make the viewer unable to imagine doing anything but watching line 2, then line 3. He calls it "the domino effect".
- The deeper the loop, the more curious the viewer, and the more hooked they are.
- His question: how do you do that in just three or four sentences? His answer is a formula he says works every time, with three steps.

### Step 1: the context lean (first line or two)

- Job one is topic clarity: be super clear what the video is about, so the viewer can self-select in or out.
- Kallaway does not want to trick non-target viewers: if he is talking about investing and you don't care about investing, he wants you gone. If you do care, he wants to signal it as early as possible.
- Job two is getting them leaning in. The more they lean in, the more curious they are.
- His four easiest ways to get someone leaning in: establish common ground, reference a benefit or pain point they have, use a metaphor to simplify a complex idea, or tell them something so interesting it blows their mind.
- The viewer must feel connected, as if what you are talking about relates to them in some way.

### Step 2: the scroll stop interjection (one line)

- A single line that acts like a stun gun. They were leaning in, and now they stop as if they "ran into a window".
- Best done with a contrasting word. Kallaway's favourite is "but". Others: however, yet, although, therefore, on the other hand.
- It is only a setup line for the haymaker in step 3. To accept the haymaker, the viewer has to be stunned first.
- At this point the viewer wonders what could possibly come next, and that wondering replaces any distracting thought that could send them somewhere else.

### Step 3: the contrarian snapback (the haymaker)

- A sentence that goes the opposite way of the initial lean. It stays on topic, but it snaps the viewer onto a different path.
- The bigger the shock and the bigger the snap, the better the effect. Kallaway calls it playing "the uno reverse card".
- The underlying pattern: "If you want to achieve X, don't do Y, do Z." If the viewer wants X, they will listen.
- The catch: Z must actually be compelling. If you play the game with nothing at the end of the rainbow, people churn "and you're a fraud."

### Where it works

- Kallaway says that if you analyse the best performing content of any creator in any medium, a lot of it follows this formula.
- It is not only for big news or entertainment content. It works for pure B2B lead generation too (see the real estate example).
- It works in short form, long form, any type of content, in conversation and in life. He calls it "base psychology 101".
- Implementing it in every different video and use case is what takes reps and practice. He says that part is not easy.

### Frameworks

**Context lean, scroll stop interjection, contrarian snapback** (Kallaway)

1. Context lean: say what it is about (topic clarity) and get them leaning in through common ground, a benefit or pain point, a metaphor, or a mind-blowing fact
2. Scroll stop interjection: one stun line, usually built on a contrast word like but, however, yet, although
3. Contrarian snapback: a line that goes the opposite way of the lean, still on topic, and snaps them onto a new path

### Examples

- **Kallaway, Vegas Sphere video (8 million views, his figure):** Context lean: "The tech in the Vegas sphere is insane. Biggest screen ever built. 20 times bigger than an IMAX." Interjection: "But get this, the screen is actually the least impressive part of the whole thing." Snapback: "because the most impressive part is the audio. This is going to blow your mind."
  <br>*Why it works:* The lean uses the blow-your-mind route and references IMAX, something viewers already know. The stun says the huge screen they can see is not impressive. The snapback switches to the audio, so they must stay to learn how audio beats the biggest screen ever built.
- **Kallaway, a B2B real estate version:** "There are three massive mistakes people are making with their mortgage. The average person pays an extra \$12,000 per year. Now, most people think it's because of high interest rates. But it turns out it has nothing to do with that. Because the biggest waste of money is actually coming from..."
  <br>*Why it works:* Kallaway says he doesn't know the real answer, he is showing the shape. His claim: a real estate agent making content in this format would drive a lot of leads and build authority.
- **Kallaway, the hook of the masterclass itself:** Lean: "Today we're talking about hooks. If you want your videos to perform better, you have to make better hooks." Interjection: "But here's the thing. I'm not going to give you a list of 25 proven viral hooks cuz that's not what you need." Snapback: "What you need is to understand the psychology behind why those hooks worked and then the tactics for how to use that psychology in your own stuff."
  <br>*Why it works:* He says "if you're still watching, that proves it worked". The lean gives topic, common ground and a benefit. The stun plays on a belief viewers already hold, that lists of "40 best proven hooks" aren't the answer. The snapback uses the word psychology, which most people suspect leads to deeper, secret information.

### Common mistakes

- Building a great setup with no real payoff. Kallaway: people churn and you look like a fraud.
- Skipping topic clarity to be clever, so the viewer can't self-select in.
- A snapback that goes off topic. It must go the opposite way of the lean while staying on the same subject.
- Expecting the formula to be easy. Kallaway says applying it to every use case takes reps.

### Try it

Pick a topic you know well. Write one line that says what it is about and why your viewer should care (the lean). Then write one line starting with "But" that challenges what they just assumed. Then write one line that sends them in the opposite direction, still on topic. Read all three aloud. If the third line has no real answer behind it, change the topic or find the real Z.

## 2.3 Five fast tips: visuals, benefits, culture hopping, speed, staccato

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** On top of his master formula, Kallaway gives five quick ways to improve any hook: a visual hook, leading with a benefit or pain point, culture hopping, compressing speed to value, and short staccato sentences.

### Tip 2: visual hooks

- Kallaway's claim: visual hooks are "probably 100 times more powerful" than spoken hooks, because people read faster than they hear, and faster than they can take in a whole video scene. (In his storytelling video he calls visual hooks "10x more effective". Both are his own claims.)
- Put 3 to 5 words of title text on screen, in a big bold font, that build the context lean. If you can say it in 1 to 2 sentences, distil it to 3 to 5 words. His claim: just adding title text makes videos do "so much better".
- Pick the words with care. For his life-size floor plans video (about 15 million views) he led with "future of home design" rather than "life-size floor plans", because the second is just the name of the thing and risked being misunderstood. He added arrows too.
- The visual itself: the most compelling visual you can get, with "just enough" motion. Like a deer in the woods that notices movement out of the corner of its eye.
- Too much motion overwhelms the viewer and they miss the context. Too little bores them. An example of just enough: a girl in a get-ready-with-me video putting her hair up, leaning forward and back.
- His opinion on the Colin and Samir Ray-Ban Meta video (he says 60 million views, later 50 million): it went viral because the first clip had the perfect amount of rapid motion plus Samir in the mirror.
- Text on screen plus motion plus spoken word together is far stronger than spoken word alone.

### Tip 3: lead with the benefit or pain point

- Kallaway: people like hearing about what they already care about, and they are open to new perspectives if those unlock a benefit or solve a pain.
- Average: "You should be taking magnesium because it's one of 21 core building block minerals." It needs prior knowledge and gives no benefit.
- Better: "If you want better sleep, you need to be taking magnesium." Even someone against supplements will likely hear you out, because the wish to fix bad sleep is so strong.
- When writing a hook, always ask what the benefit or pain solve is for the target viewer, and work it in if you can.
- Dan Koe says the same from the writing side: to reach people who aren't trying to learn, open with some version of "if you want" (a desire) or "if you don't want" (a pain). His example: "If you hate the thought of building someone else's dreams for the rest of your life, start a personal brand."
- Kallaway (content psychology playbook): go ONE STEP AWAY from the real pain so you don't trip the viewer's BS detector. His videos about "making money" flop, his videos about "growing faster" do well, because viewers make the leap to money themselves. He also says to "twist the knife" (name the frustration bluntly) and talk to an audience of one: one of his hooks used "you" 9 times and "I" 0 times.

### Tip 4: culture hopping (cult hopping)

- Kallaway: hearing familiar things creates comfort in the subconscious. Hearing lots of unfamiliar things makes people feel dumb, and they leave. Like sitting in an advanced physics lecture with no physics background.
- So wrap the unknown idea in something known: a brand, celebrity, movement or cultural reference, used as a metaphor or comparison.
- His example: for complex tax planning, talk about how Taylor Swift's financial adviser would plan her estate around the Eras Tour earnings. Or just name her among the celebrities when you describe the elite wealth level you serve.
- You don't need the whole piece to be about the celebrity. It lets you "draft off the credibility and the popularity" of something that already exists while you are still small.
- In his speedrun video he calls a related move "speed to relatability": showing a face or brand viewers already know.

### Tip 5: compress speed to value

- Kallaway: imagine a countdown timer before your content "explodes" and nobody pays attention. About 4 seconds for short form, maybe 1 to 2 minutes for YouTube. This applies to both education and entertainment.
- Give a first hit of value in the hook or right after it. Don't bury your best stuff. Anything after they click off "might as well be a black screen".
- You are not going for full completion (super fans watch the whole thing). You are trying to "eke out an extra 30 seconds at a time".
- If the first hit of value is unique and helpful, viewers usually stay long enough for the second hit.
- The value loop mirrors the curiosity loop: context, immediate value, context, immediate value. Putting value inside the hook itself is what he calls masterful.
- In his speedrun video he adds: aim for "2 hits of dopamine before they leave" (the visual, then the value tease), with value signalled within about 2.5 seconds.

### Tip 6: staccato hook sentences

- Staccato is a short note in music.
- Over a whole video you should vary sentence length to create rhythm. But in the hook, compress to short sentences.
- Short sentences force maximum clarity and raise the "density of value per word" at the moment time is most precious.
- Go short first, then medium and longer as the video goes on.

### Frameworks

**Five fast hook tips (tips 2 to 6 of the masterclass)** (Kallaway)

1. Visual hook: 3 to 5 bold words on screen plus a visual with just enough motion
2. Lead with the benefit or pain point the viewer already has
3. Culture hopping: wrap the unknown in something known (brand, celebrity, movement)
4. Compress speed to value: a first hit of value in or right after the hook
5. Staccato: short sentences in the hook, longer ones later

### Examples

- **Kallaway, magnesium hook:** "You should be taking magnesium because it's one of 21 core building block minerals" becomes "If you want better sleep, you need to be taking magnesium."
  <br>*Why it works:* The second leads with a benefit the viewer badly wants, so even skeptics wait to hear the rest.
- **Kallaway, Taylor Swift and tax planning:** A tax planner frames complex estate planning around how Taylor Swift's adviser would handle her Eras Tour earnings.
  <br>*Why it works:* A known name makes an unfamiliar topic feel comfortable, and borrows popularity while the creator is still small.
- **Kallaway, life-size floor plans text hook:** On-screen text "future of home design" first, then "life-size floor plans" with arrows.
  <br>*Why it works:* He avoided leading with the bare name of the thing because it could be misunderstood.

### Common mistakes

- Relying on the spoken hook alone with no text and no motion.
- Too much motion in the opening shot, so the viewer misses the context.
- Hooks that need prior knowledge and name no benefit (the magnesium "21 minerals" version).
- Piling up unfamiliar terms so the viewer feels dumb and leaves.
- Saving the best value for later. Kallaway: after they leave, it might as well be a black screen.
- Long, winding sentences in the first seconds.

### Try it

Take one hook you have written. Rewrite it four ways: (1) start it with "If you want..." or "If you struggle with...", (2) add one known brand, person or event as a comparison, (3) cut every sentence to under 10 words, (4) boil it down to 3 to 5 words you could put on screen. Pick the version a stranger would understand fastest.

## 2.4 The six hook formats

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** From analysing thousands of top hooks, Kallaway found six formats that keep coming up in every niche: fortune teller, experimenter, teacher, magician, investigator and contrarian. Any idea can be written in all six.

### Why formats help

- Kallaway says he analysed "thousands of the top hooks" and these six kept coming up in every niche.
- If you don't know how to approach a hook, pick one of the six and use it.
- Each format is a different way of building contrast. Some hide the contrast in the framing, the contrarian says it outright.

### 1\. The fortune teller (present vs future)

- Positions the present against the future and builds curiosity about how the future might change.
- Examples: "That new microwave is going to change cooking forever." "That little blue light is going to impact traffic moving forward." "We're witnessing one of the largest breakthroughs in marketing." "This is the future of animation."
- Why it works, per Kallaway: the human brain is designed to constantly think about the future.
- Steps: (a) establish the current reality, (b) work out how the future might change based on the subject, (c) frame a question or statement teasing that new future.
- Good for breaking news, product innovations, and anyone building authority on knowing the future of a topic.

### 2\. The experimenter (peer to peer demo)

- Shows how something works with a demo or experiment, angled peer to peer: one friend showing another what they found.
- The contrast: I used to do it this way, I found a method, let me show you how I do it now.
- Colin and Samir: "Does anything look weird about this shot to you? I actually don't have a camera in my hands. I'm recording with these glasses on my face. These are the Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses and they're about to change the way that we create." Kallaway loves it because the glasses camera is itself the subject, shown live in the hook.
- Another: "This right here is the brand new ChatGPT agent that literally controls your browser... this is an 11 step process. And we're going to see if it does it."
- Many MrBeast videos fit: human experiments you watch in real time. So do the people building elaborate outdoor structures or log cabins by hand.
- Steps: (a) establish the base pain point, (b) show you solved it or tried a new method or tool with an experiment, (c) walk through why the new way is different and solves the pain better.
- Good for product demos, new frameworks and B2B tool showcases.

### 3\. The teacher (teacher to student lesson)

- Like the experimenter, but teacher to student, and framed as a lesson rather than a live experiment. "I just saw somebody who solved X with Y method, let me explain how."
- The contrast: you're failing at this, I found a new way, here's how so you start winning.
- Examples: "Three things you can learn from Aritzia." "Here's how I got this shot." "I made this ecom brand a million dollars in 12 hours using something called the product drop method."
- Either the creator tried something and pulled out the lessons, or studied someone else who solved it and teaches it back.
- Steps: (a) establish the base pain point, (b) show you have a solve through learnings or a method, (c) walk through why the steps worked.
- Good for building authority in a category so you can sell that expertise.

### 4\. The magician (the stun gun)

- Different from the other five: it can be used before, or combined with, any of them.
- Uses visuals and/or language ("Hey, look at that") to force attention to one thing. Usually outlandish, or rhythmic and visually popping.
- Examples: "What's up, MTV crew"; "Yo, check this out. So, President Trump just ordered the resurgence of a 1980s plan for nuclear war in space. Let me explain." Visual stun tactics like the snap click Karl Shakur uses.
- Kallaway's favourite: the neon sign guy who starts as Trump in a wig, makes a joke in the voice, throws the wig off and goes straight into neon signs.
- Kallaway has opened his last 30 or so videos with "Check this out really quick", a signature pattern people recognise by ear.
- The visual pacifier: a passive activity that holds just enough subconscious attention so the viewer focuses on the main thing. Alix Earle doing her makeup, a guy stacking cups, split-screen GTA gameplay videos.
- Steps: find a visual, sound or signature that is atypical, tied to your videos, and stops the scroll. Many creators stack it: magician first, then one of the other five.

### 5\. The investigator (the secret)

- Contrast against an unknown secret or a finding from your research: today you don't know, I show you, now you know.
- Very common. Kallaway says almost all his own short videos fit it (unearthing tech or AI things about the future).
- Examples: "So this is one of the sneakier marketing campaigns I've seen. [A rapper's] brand sells out in seconds. And what's crazy is that it's not just because he's a rapper." "Check this out. This is a secret Japanese city built at the base of Mount Fuji. But it's not just any city."
- Steps: tee up that there is a secret they don't know, and frame it against their current reality of not knowing.
- It doesn't have to be cheeky. Straightforward ones still grab.
- Good for people at the leading edge of their field, or showing deep research. (He once calls it "the secret keeper".)

### 6\. The contrarian (said outright)

- The easiest one to see where the contrast sits: the creator says outright what they believe that differs from conventional wisdom ("you're doing your branding wrong, you should be doing it this way").
- Examples: "I think this is probably the most important thing that you need to be doing if you run a brand that you are probably not doing. And this is having one-on-one conversations with your customers." "You have no creative ideas because your space sucks to live in." "This guy is a multi-millionaire from his clothing brand. And the weird thing is their best sellers are almost never in stock."
- Steps: work out what you believe that most people don't, and say it explicitly.

### Any idea fits all six

- Kallaway's "secret": you don't have to figure out which of the six works, because every idea can be shaped into any of them.
- So the real question becomes how to choose. His answer: start from the key visual and pick the format that creates the most contrast with it (next lesson).

### Frameworks

**The six hook formats** (Kallaway)

1. Fortune teller: present vs future, tease how the future changes
2. Experimenter: show a demo peer to peer, old way vs new way
3. Teacher: frame a lesson teacher to student, you're failing vs here's the method
4. Magician: a visual or verbal stun, stackable with any other format
5. Investigator: you don't know the secret, now you do
6. Contrarian: say outright what you believe that most people don't

### Examples

- **Kallaway, one backpack review in all six formats:** Fortune teller: "This backpack is going to completely change the way millennials travel." Experimenter: "I just took this backpack 7,000 miles across the world, but it has three major design flaws." Teacher: "If you're traveling internationally this summer, this is the best way to pack your backpack." Magician: a rapid match cut of many backpacks with the voice-over "Which of these backpacks is the best for a guy in their 20s." Investigator: "I can't believe this company isn't marketing this one flaw in their bag." Contrarian: "Everybody loves this bag, but if you use it every day, it is so overhyped."
  <br>*Why it works:* Proof that the format is a choice, not something fixed by the topic.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking your topic only allows one format. Kallaway shows one idea in all six.
- Treating the magician as a full hook. It is a stun that works best stacked on one of the other five.

### Try it

Take one idea you want to post about. Write six one-line hooks for it, one per format, like Kallaway's backpack example. Then circle the one with the biggest gap between what people expect and what you show.

## 2.5 The four parts of a hook and the key visual

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Every hook has four parts: spoken, visual, text and audio. Kallaway says the gap between 500 and 500,000 views is getting all four to say the same thing, and that the visual matters most, so you should build the hook starting from the key visual.

### Four components and max alignment

- Kallaway: every hook has four parts. The spoken hook (what you say), the visual hook (what you show), the text hook (the words on screen) and the audio hook (music and sound effects).
- His big secret: "The difference between 500 and 500,000 views is unlocking max alignment between those four things."
- If you say one thing and the visual is even slightly different, that is misalignment, and the hook gets weaker.
- The reason is comprehension: how well the viewer understands what they see and hear. You can't hook someone unless they fully understand and are interested.
- Misalignment leads to confusion, and confusion leads to comprehension loss.
- In another hooks video Kallaway calls the same idea the triple hook: text hook (words on screen that are not the captions), spoken hook and visual hook, plus a fourth, the first caption line above the fold. His number one mistake with business owners: they polish the spoken hook but the three don't match. His test: watch the visual alone, can you guess the text? And the other way round.

### The visual, audio, visual sandwich

- Kallaway's "subconscious heat map" of how all viewers take in a video.
- First they take in the visual and the text hook (reading the text if it is obvious and well placed).
- Then their ears catch up and they hear the spoken hook.
- Then they look back at the visual to confirm what they heard.
- His reason (his claim): our eyes process 10 to 100 times more information per second than our ears. He calls it the speed of light vs speed of sound.
- His conclusion: the most important part of a hook's performance is the visual, not what you say. He calls it the key visual.

### The 5-step hook writing process

- Step 1: when you have the idea, first work out what visuals you can slot into the first 3 to 5 seconds. The better the visual, the more you can lean on it and set up contrast back to it. No good visual? Ask whether you should make the video at all. If yes, manufacture the key visual with motion graphics or stock footage.
- Step 2: list the most interesting fact sets or angles of the story. The one with the biggest contrast decides which of the six formats you use: the format that creates the most contrast and aligns with the visual.
- Step 3: write the spoken hook. 2 to 4 lines: context lean, contrast (the interjection), contrarian snapback.
- Step 4: decide what text goes on screen to support the first visual, and the re-check the viewer does after hearing the speech.
- Step 5: watch it back and gut check. If the viewer goes visual, speech, visual, are they very clear what the video is about, and does it match where the rest of the video goes? If not, stop and rework. "That is how important it is."

### Five kinds of visual hooks

- From Kallaway's interrupt theory video: graphic or text overlay, pattern interrupt (switching visuals), subject motion (moving in or out of frame), a visual effect or transition, and visual selection (picking a striking shot).
- The easiest place to start: move your body, move the visual, or move text on it.
- Don't overdo it. Too much reads as gimmicky.

### Frameworks

**Hook writing from the key visual** (Kallaway)

1. Find the key visual for the first 3 to 5 seconds (or build one, or drop the idea)
2. List the most interesting angles and pick the one with the biggest contrast, which picks the format
3. Write a 2 to 4 line spoken hook: lean, interjection, snapback
4. Add on-screen text that supports the visual and the viewer's re-check
5. Watch it back visual, speech, visual: is it 100% clear and does it match the rest? If not, rework

**Four hook components** (Kallaway)

1. Spoken: what you say
2. Visual: what you show
3. Text: the words on screen
4. Audio: music and sound effects
5. All four must say the same thing (max alignment)

### Examples

- **Good: Kallaway's life-size floor plans (15 million views, his figure):** Watched without sound, the eye goes first to sliding text: "the future of home design". The opening split-screen clip (a girl with her arms going up) was only a visual stun gun. The real key visual, which he built the whole video around, was the next full-screen shot of people walking on projected floor plans in a warehouse, with the text "life-size floor plans" and an arrow. Script: "Check this out" (magician), "These are life-size floor plans. You can literally walk through your exact home design before you build it." (context lean that describes exactly what you see), then "I think this is the future of how people are going to design their homes." (fortune teller snapback).
  <br>*Why it works:* Embedded contrast: most people had never seen this, so its existence alone was contrast. No "but" was needed. He chose fortune teller over investigator ("a secret about how people design homes") because it was easier to explain and more shocking. Speech, text and visual all matched.
- **Bad: Kallaway's generative world models (about 100,000 views or less):** Text "future of storytelling" over rooms magically appearing. Spoken: "This is the future of storytelling. It's called a generative world model. It lets you turn a single image into a photorealistic cinematic world."
  <br>*Why it works:* "Future of storytelling" is abstract and the rooms don't show it, so comprehension loss starts before the sound. Nobody knows what a generative world model or a photorealistic world is, and he had no visuals that showed those terms. Viewers saw cool visuals but didn't take in his words. Looking back he says he should have gone explicit ("These rooms are the future of commercials" with footage of people filming commercials in them), or, since the B-roll was hard to explain, canned the idea. "There's always more ideas that you could make."

### Common mistakes

- Polishing the spoken hook while the visual, text and audio say something slightly different (Kallaway's number one mistake with business owners).
- Abstract on-screen words that the visual doesn't show ("future of storytelling").
- Using terms the viewer doesn't know and has no visual for.
- Writing the words first and hunting for visuals after. Kallaway starts from the key visual.
- Making a video with no good visual instead of building one or dropping the idea.
- Overdoing visual effects until the hook feels gimmicky.

### Try it

Take a video idea (or a recent video). Before writing a word, list 3 visuals you could show in the first 3 seconds. Pick the strongest. Now write a 2 to 3 line hook that describes exactly what that visual shows, and 3 to 5 words of on-screen text. Watch the visual with the text and no sound: could a stranger guess the topic? If not, change the visual or drop the idea.

## 2.6 The six power words: the copywriting inside a hook

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Kallaway breaks the best hooks into six word slots: four core (subject, action, objective, contrast) and two optional (proof, time). Mark them in hooks that worked, then fill them for your own topic.

### The four core words

- Kallaway's teaching hook: "If I had to grow from zero to 100K subs on YouTube, here's how I would do it."
- 1\. Subject clarity word: who the sentence is about. Subject then predicate, as you learned in first grade. In the example: "I". Options: I, we, you, or a proper noun.
- 2\. Action word: the action the subject takes to get the outcome. In the example: "grow". A verb or adverb.
- 3\. Objective or end state word: the result the action gets. In the example: "0 to 100K subs on YouTube". The more shocking and non-obvious, the better, but there must be one.
- 4\. Contrast word: compares the new outcome with the base state, which intensifies the curiosity from A to B. In the example, "0 to 100K" (base state 0, end state 100K).
- How to find the contrast: what does the viewer expect today (the base state), what new reality does the objective open up, and how do you put the two side by side in words?

### The two optional words

- They ratchet up curiosity where they fit.
- 5\. Proof word: why the viewer should care about your view. Rarely needed in entertainment, often needed in education. Add one word, "again": "If I had to grow from zero to 100k subs on YouTube again, here's how I would do it." Proof can be implied (the viewer sees your subscriber count) or stated.
- 6\. Time word: a time limit that maps urgency or speed to effectiveness. "...in 5 months, here's how I would do it" or "...here's how I would do it in the next 90 days." Months, days, minutes, seconds.
- Proof and time are optional partly because they can go in lines 2 and 3.
- The words can be single words or phrases. Kallaway says to think of them as chunks you stack.
- One phrase often does two jobs (objective and contrast together), especially when written tightly.

### Power words you mine and remix

- From Kallaway's interrupt theory video: power words are 1 to 3 word bundles that are "heat-seeking missiles for attention" (that phrase is itself one).
- His two best videos both used "scientifically impossible to skip".
- Lazy creators copy the phrase. Good ones remix it: "psychologically impossible to resist", "neurochemically impossible to skip".
- Level 1 is copying hook formats. Level 2 is mining power words from top performers and twisting them. The research bank notes this matches Mino Lee's keyword bank (lesson 11).
- His practice (from his six power words video): "copy work". Write down 10 to 30 top hooks a week by hand, mark the 6 slots, and swap in your own topic.

### Frameworks

**The six power words** (Kallaway)

1. Subject: I, we, you, or a proper noun
2. Action: the verb the subject takes
3. Objective / end state: the result, as shocking and non-obvious as possible
4. Contrast: base state vs end state (A to B)
5. Proof (optional): why trust this view, e.g. "again"
6. Time (optional): a time limit, e.g. "in 5 months"

### Examples

- **Kallaway, life-size floor plans (15M):** "These are called life-size floor plans. You can literally walk through your exact home design before you build it."
  <br>*Why it works:* Subject: "life-size floor plans" and really "you". Action: "can literally walk through". Objective: walking your home design before building. Contrast: "before you build it" (normally you only see it after). The same phrase is objective and contrast. No proof. Debatable time word: "before you build it".
- **Ava (personal brand launch), the borrowed shock hook:** She says "This is a shock value hook", then plays an already-viral clip: "This is why you should always wash your bread before eating it" (Kallaway says about 66 million views).
  <br>*Why it works:* Her real hook is the validated video placed on top of hers, a savvy move per Kallaway. Subject: you. Action: "should always wash your bread". Objective: washed bread, implied because saying it would be redundant. Contrast: washed vs not washed. No proof or time in line 1, but line 2 could add proof ("I'm a professional baker that's been doing this for 30 years").
- **Hormozi (6.3 million views):** "If you find a girl who believes in your dreams more than you do, who makes you want to be a better man, who's willing to work alongside you to get there and is grateful for whatever you have right now today, no matter where you're at, just marry her."
  <br>*Why it works:* Subject: you (mostly men). Actions: "find a girl" and "marry her", split apart. Objectives: the list. Contrast: "more than you do" (you believe X, she believes X plus more). Time: "right now today". No proof, it doesn't fit.
- **Cat GPT (2.9 million views, a big outlier):** "If we were smarter, we would listen to 13-year-olds more."
  <br>*Why it works:* Subject: "we" (the royal we). Objective: "smarter". Action: "would listen", which comes after the objective. Contrast: "to 13-year-olds more", because after "if we were smarter we would listen" you expect older, wiser people. She says the opposite.

### Common mistakes

- A hook with no objective or end state. Kallaway says there must be one.
- Copying a viral power phrase word for word instead of remixing it.
- Forcing proof or time into line 1 when they fit better in lines 2 and 3, or when they don't fit at all (entertainment rarely needs proof).

### Try it

Copy work: find 10 hooks from top-performing posts or videos in your format. Write each by hand. Underline the subject, action, objective and contrast, and circle any proof or time word. Then write one hook of your own on your topic using all four core words, and a second version that adds proof or time.

## 2.7 Hook mistakes 1 and 2: delay and confusion

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Kallaway's four biggest hook mistakes come in order: delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest. Fix them in that order, because each assumes the ones before are fixed. The first two are about getting the topic across fast and clearly.

### The four horsemen

- Kallaway reviews dozens to hundreds of videos from business owners every week. His list of mistakes comes from those reviews.
- Bad hooks fail to deliver topic clarity and on-target curiosity because of one or more of these four: delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest.
- Fix all four and you are good. Order matters: each one assumes the earlier ones are already fixed.

### Mistake 1: delay (the easiest to fix)

- The context on what the video is about comes too late.
- Picture a 13-line script where line 3 is the first time the topic shows up. Lines 1 and 2 are fluff. Cut them and move line 3 up, so the topic lands in the first 1 to 2 seconds.
- Kallaway calls the idea "speed to value": how fast the viewer gets to clarity or value.
- Bad: "Guys, this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen. And when you see it, you're never going to believe it." It sounds like suspense but gives zero context. "A crazy thing you saw could be literally anything", so the viewer can't decide whether to keep watching.
- Short-form retention is exponential decay and the first two seconds drop like a cliff. Every second without saying what the video is about, a large share of viewers leave.
- Vague, open-ended hooks seem to work a lot on TikTok. Kallaway's claim: the people who stay do so because of how the creator looks, the emotion on their face, or the text hook, not the spoken words. They can work, just far less often.
- Fix: the first sentence gives the needed context in as few words as possible. Good: "Here are three simple ways to improve your gut health" or "If you have gut issues, these three remedies will help you immediately."

### Others on delay and missing context

- heyDominik calls missing context the most common hook mistake. Example: a cover reading "Things I don't do being lean over 40", then the video starts in a kitchen with "I definitely don't cut out carbs." The scrolling viewer doesn't know why they should care.
- His analogy: a stranger in a hardware store walks up and says "I never pull out", then explains, halfway through a project, that he means screws. There's a fine line between grabbing attention and confusing people.
- His fix: add context as on-screen text at the top of the first frame, and make that text a curiosity gap at the same time.
- heyDominik: "Who can relate?" is a weak hook because the viewer doesn't know what the video is about.
- Logan Smith's HPC system says the same: the hook must introduce the topic (while leaving questions open).
- Kallaway (storytelling mistakes video) calls the school order "jumbling the W's": where and when, then who and what, then how and why. Online, lead with WHAT and WHY ("The future of entertainment is about to look very different"), then WHO and HOW, with where and when as colour. "You don't lead with the icing, you lead with the cake."

### Mistake 2: confusion (comprehension loss)

- Delay is gone, but the words or phrasing are unclear. The viewer understands half and can't judge if it's for them.
- Bad (deliberately exaggerated): "These guys built a \$30 million empire and the online money they made is most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice like they did."
- Better: "These guys built a \$30 million empire and their secret for earning money online was their insane journaling practice." Slightly different words and order raise the comprehension hit rate.
- Kallaway's tactics are about becoming a better writer: fewer words, but enough that they can't misunderstand. Simpler words, ideally a sixth grade reading level. Direct active voice, not passive ("the dog jumped" vs "the jump of the dog").
- Mino Lee agrees: use simple, easy words, because hard-to-understand hooks lose viewers.

### Two clarity tools

- Kallaway's AI prompt: "I've written a hook for a short form video about X topic. I need help increasing the clarity and the framing of the sentences I used. I want the meaning to be the exact same, but can you rewrite this in a sixth grade reading level so that there's no misunderstanding from the viewer?"
- The misinterpretation test: read only the 1 to 2 hook sentences on their own and ask, "Is it possible for the viewer to misunderstand what I'm saying in the wrong way? Is there more than one way these sentences could be interpreted?" If yes, rewrite to remove the other paths until only the one you want is left.

### Frameworks

**The four hook mistakes (fix in order)** (Kallaway)

1. Delay: the topic comes too late
2. Confusion: the words are unclear
3. Irrelevance: they don't feel it's for them
4. Disinterest: they're not curious enough

### Examples

- **Kallaway, delay fix:** "Guys, this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen..." becomes "Here are three simple ways to improve your gut health."
  <br>*Why it works:* The second gives the topic in the first words, so the right viewers can opt in at once.
- **Kallaway, confusion fix:** "...the online money they made is most difficult to earn if you don't develop a journaling practice like they did" becomes "...their secret for earning money online was their insane journaling practice."
  <br>*Why it works:* Same idea, simpler order, fewer ways to misread it.
- **heyDominik, the lean-over-40 video:** A cover "Things I don't do being lean over 40" followed by "I definitely don't cut out carbs" in a kitchen, with no context for the scrolling viewer.
  <br>*Why it works:* The fix is context as on-screen text at the top of the first frame, written as a curiosity gap.

### Common mistakes

- Opening with fake suspense ("the craziest thing I've ever seen") that gives no topic.
- One or two lines of warm-up before the topic.
- "Who can relate?" openers (heyDominik).
- Long, passive, jargon-heavy sentences.
- Never testing whether the hook can be read two ways.

Then read the new first two sentences alone and ask Kallaway's question: could this be understood in more than one way? Rewrite until the answer is no, at a sixth grade reading level.

## 2.8 Hook mistakes 3 and 4: irrelevance and disinterest

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Once the topic is fast and clear, the hook must feel made for the viewer (fix irrelevance with "you" and a real pain) and must make them curious (fix disinterest with contrast: what they believe now against what you show them).

### Mistake 3: irrelevance

- Kallaway: the viewer knows the topic but isn't confident that what follows will be valuable for them.
- Fix 1: say "you / your" instead of "I / me". "I've struggled with skin problems my whole life" makes the viewer ask whether they see themselves in the creator. If they don't, they value the solution less and leave. "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life..." leaves no question it's for them, so they hold on longer.
- Fix 2: frame around expected value. Entertainment solves boredom, education solves a specific problem, and that solution is the value. Agitate a pain point they already have, so you can tee up a solution for them.
- "These are three common trends in skincare" is a nice to have. "If you struggle with acne, try these three things" is a need to have.
- Kallaway (dopamine ladder video): if your hooks aren't hooking, the question that popped into the viewer's mind is either not relevant to them or not interesting enough. His makeup example: an attractive person with a moving red and orange graphic gets him to pause (stimulation), but "Here's how you do your makeup for the holidays" pops questions he doesn't care about, so he leaves. Stimulation won, captivation lost.

### Mistake 4: disinterest (the one people fixate on)

- The hook is clear and relevant, but not curious enough.
- The fix is a curiosity loop. Kallaway says everybody talks about curiosity loops but nobody explains them: the viewer sees something, asks a question in their head, gets context that answers it but sparks a new question, more context, a new question, and so on through the whole video.
- The hook's job is to open the first loop and start the spiral: the first question they can't look away from.
- How, every time: put contrast or comparison in the hook. Contrast is the distance between the viewer's current common belief and your contrarian or alternative view.
- Example: "This one trick will clear up all your acne in 45 minutes without any side effects." The baseline in their head (wait 6 months, take Accutane, side effects) vs your offer (45 minutes, no side effects). That distance is contrast.
- Why it works, per Kallaway: framing A (known) against B (unknown) brings back the "sticky pain point" of A not solving their problem. The comparison re-agitates the pain, so they get curious because they want it solved.
- Simplified: A is what they already believe. B is an alternative that solves their pain faster, better or cheaper.
- Kallaway calls contrast with words "the secret to driving curiosity whenever you want" and "the secret to storytelling at a macro level".

### Stated vs implied contrast

- Stated contrast: say both A and B. "Most people solve their acne with Accutane, but I have an herbal remedy that does it three times faster." Blunt and impossible to miss.
- Implied contrast: say only B, because people already know A. "If you want to solve your acne, this herbal supplement is eight times more effective." Your take against "the field of all other options". Subtler, "a little bit more savvy".

### More ways the sources build curiosity

- Kallaway's two questions while structuring a hook: (1) What is the most curiosity-inducing question I could trigger about this topic? (2) How well does it map to what my ideal viewer cares about? Then pick the words and visuals that make it pop.
- Two ways to plant the question: ask it rhetorically, or imply it with details so they put it in their own head. Either way, as fast as possible.
- Kallaway's pro tips: contrast (compare a known thing to an unknown thing) or a shocking or weird premise they've never seen. Unknown things pop curiosity best, because curious people want to know what they've never seen.
- Anticipation hooks (Kallaway): the visual of someone about to be hit by a slowly approaching car pops "is that person going to get hit?", and the slow approach builds anticipation in real time.
- heyDominik: "challenges a common belief" is one of the strongest hooks. Every hook needs a setup that makes your audience care and plants a question they want answered. His test: "what is the question you want to pop in people's heads?"
- heyDominik: open loops and don't close them as long as possible. When you close one, open another.
- Logan Smith: a good hook introduces the topic while leaving lots of questions. "I want to be the first person to have a snowball fight in the desert" (70M+ views) raises how, where will he get snow, how did he get to a desert.
- Jenny Hoyos: start with a question, because people stay to the end to find the answer, or with something shocking. Her examples: how much ice cream can you get for a dollar? What's the cheapest thing in an airport? Is it faster to go inside or the drive-through? Is fast food really as fast as they say? (45M+ views).
- Kallaway (illusion of novelty): a hook of 1 to 2 lines covering (a) the new angle and (b) the outcome the viewer wants. Frames he shares but asks people not to copy word for word: "X new thing just changed the way you get Y result." "You've probably heard of X topic. Well, Y just happened that changed the way you do X to get Z result."

### What the science says about curiosity

- Loewenstein 1994 (Psychological Bulletin): curiosity is a feeling of deprivation from a gap in what you know. It needs SOME knowledge first. A gap that is too big or too small doesn't pull. This is why "this one detail about X" beats "a mystery": viewers must know enough to feel the missing piece.
- Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath 2014 (Neuron): when people were highly curious, they remembered the answer better, and even remembered unrelated faces shown while they waited. Reward areas lit up. Caveat: "dopamine" is how the press described it; the study measured brain areas linked to dopamine.
- Schultz, Dayan and Montague 1997 (Science): dopamine neurons signal prediction errors and fire at the cue that predicts a reward. This gives a real basis to anticipation. Caveat: it is monkey research about learning, so don't say "dopamine makes people addicted to your video".
- Robertson et al. 2023 (Nature Human Behaviour), about 105,000 headline variations on Upworthy: for an average headline, each extra negative word raised click-through by 2.3%. Berger and Milkman 2012 (about 7,000 New York Times articles): high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) get shared, sadness makes sharing less likely. So a sad hook can get the click, but awe or a bit of anger gets the share.

### Frameworks

**Contrast: A vs B** (Kallaway)

1. A: what the viewer already believes or does now
2. B: your alternative that solves their pain faster, better or cheaper
3. Stated: say both A and B
4. Implied: say only B and let them supply A

### Examples

- **Kallaway, "you" instead of "I":** "I've struggled with skin problems my whole life" becomes "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life..."
  <br>*Why it works:* The viewer no longer has to decide whether they are like the creator. It is plainly about them.
- **Kallaway, nice to have vs need to have:** "These are three common trends in skincare" vs "If you struggle with acne, try these three things."
  <br>*Why it works:* Trends are optional. Fixing acne is a pain they already feel.
- **heyDominik, the lean at 45 rewrite:** "Lean at 45 and I eat carbs every day."
  <br>*Why it works:* Short and snappy, and it contradicts the common belief that lean people cut carbs.

### Common mistakes

- Opening with "I" so the viewer has to wonder if they are like you.
- Framing around nice-to-know facts instead of a pain the viewer has.
- Stimulating the eye but popping a question the viewer doesn't care about (the holiday makeup example).
- A curiosity gap that is too big (pure mystery) or too small (obvious), per Loewenstein.
- Contrast without a real B behind it.

### Try it

Write down one common belief in your field (A). Write your alternative that solves the same pain faster, better or cheaper (B). Now write two hooks: one stated ("Most people X, but Y") and one implied (only B). Both must start with "you" or "if you". Read both and ask which question pops into your head first.

## 2.9 Text hooks and the first frame

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** Eyes hunt for words on screen before they take in the scene, so the text hook and the first frame do much of the work. Kallaway gives rules for writing text hooks, and heyDominik shows how to build a first frame that plants a question without giving away the answer.

### Kallaway's rules for text hooks

- Kallaway (interrupt theory video): text hooks matter most. Eyes hunt for on-screen text and catch power words faster than they process the image.
- Use the subject's name (a proper noun or term pulls the eye) plus one of: a pain reminder, a dream outcome, or a "something just changed" (e.g. "Claude just changed social media forever").
- Use specific numbers: "22 days" beats "a few weeks", "\$97,000 in 13 days".
- Max 2 lines, broken where the phrase naturally breaks ("ChatGPT just pioneered / the future of work").
- No punctuation except quotes or brackets, and no "and / but / therefore". It is not a sentence.
- Place it about a third down from the top, never over the face, and leave it on screen about 3 seconds. People read, look at the visual, read again, then decide.
- In the masterclass: 3 to 5 bold words that build the context lean (lesson 3).

### The first frame is a visual hook

- heyDominik: the first frame itself is a visual hook. Add a text hook on top.
- Create contrast inside the frame to deepen the curiosity gap. For the "lean at 45 and I eat carbs every day" hook, put a big bowl of unhealthy carbs in the bottom half of the frame. Viewers think "really? how does she do it?"
- Carousels also need a hook in some form. A carousel of just pictures (or versions of the same picture) has none. Carousels beat single pictures, but still need a hook.
- Mino Lee: text hooks count too, because "hooks is just writing". A non-talking video with the on-screen text "2015, insecure cuz the only Asians on screen were nerds and losers" stacks keywords (insecure, Asian, nerds, losers) next to a picture of him younger. The words stack to hit hard.

### Don't give away the answer in the frame

- heyDominik's coffee creator example: on-screen text "vanilla blueberry iced latte" gives the answer away, and someone not in the mood for it scrolls.
- Better: "This coffee should not taste good..." with trailing dots to open the loop.
- Then go further: the blueberries were still visible, so the viewer could half-solve it and had no reason to stay. Blur the blueberries and reveal only at the end.
- If the viewer can solve the puzzle from the first frame, there is no reason to keep watching. Hide or blur the clue.

### First frame craft (heyDominik's fixes for reels)

- Respect the safe zones: text that overlaps the comment box or app buttons looks bad. Captions can usually be much smaller than you think.
- Removing a too-big caption makes the frame look cleaner at once.
- Framing: leave clean space for text. In one kitchen shot the person, kitchen and clutter left no usable space. The fallback is a background box behind the text (Instagram's own text background does this).
- Vary font weight (for example medium for some words) to make text easier on the eye.
- Colour: try options. On the coffee frame red looked "off and cheapish", and an unexpected colour worked better. Small touches help, like dots ("...") to open the loop, or an outer glow.
- Declutter: in the coffee video the latte didn't stand out, and shadows and the wall gave no depth. A clean shot with the product as the focus is "night and day". A phone under about 4 years old plus a window (or one light) is enough.
- He used an AI image tool (ChatGPT) to mock up a better first frame before filming, with a prompt like: real screenshot, nice natural lighting, kitchen, big bowl of unhealthy carbs in the bottom half, scroll-stopping, 9:16.

### Other hook space people forget

- Kallaway (packaging tips): on short form, packaging is the first caption line, the thumbnail and the first 3 to 5 second hook. The first caption line is extra hook space, so write it like a title.
- Short-form thumbnails still matter (Kallaway): they show in DM shares, they decide which videos people binge on your profile, and they make the profile look good.
- Kallaway's Diary of a CEO breakdown: a big text hook on screen ("THE TRUTH ABOUT ADDICTION") lets viewers self-select in.
- heyDominik: people who find you through a reel land on your profile, which must pass the "this is exactly what I'm looking for" test. A profile with great photos but no hooks next to weak reels "instantly takes away all the credibility".
- Sam Gaudet counts on-screen graphics as a visual hook among the extras that add "15-20%" to a talking video, but ranks it the least important of those extras.

### Frameworks

**Text hook rules** (Kallaway)

1. Name the subject (a proper noun or term)
2. Add a pain reminder, a dream outcome, or "something just changed"
3. Use specific numbers
4. Max 2 lines, broken at natural phrase breaks
5. No sentence punctuation or joining words
6. Place a third down from the top, off the face, for about 3 seconds

### Examples

- **heyDominik, the coffee video:** "vanilla blueberry iced latte" (answer given away, berries visible) becomes "This coffee should not taste good..." with the blueberries blurred until the end.
  <br>*Why it works:* The viewer gets a question instead of an answer, and can't solve it from the frame.
- **heyDominik, the carbs bowl:** Hook "Lean at 45 and I eat carbs every day" with a big bowl of unhealthy carbs in the bottom half of the frame.
  <br>*Why it works:* The picture adds contrast to the words and plants "how does she do it?"

### Common mistakes

- Putting the answer in the text hook or leaving the clue visible.
- Text over faces, over the app's buttons, or too big.
- Full sentences with punctuation as on-screen text.
- Vague numbers ("a few weeks") instead of specific ones.
- Cluttered frames where the subject doesn't stand out.
- Carousels with no hook at all.

### Try it

Take one video or image post. Write a text hook using Kallaway's rules: name the subject, add a pain, dream outcome or "just changed", include one specific number, max 2 lines, no punctuation. Then look at your first frame: is any clue to the answer visible? Is there one thing in the frame that adds contrast to the words? Fix one of the two.

## 2.10 After the hook: keep the promise, time the payoff

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** A hook that wins the stop but breaks its promise loses the viewer seconds later. The sources agree the next lines must confirm the hook and build trust, and they warn against both clickbait and paying off the answer too early.

### The lock-in zone

- Kallaway's term for the 2 to 4 sentences after the hook. He says it is arguably more important than the hook itself.
- Two things happen there. First, confirm the hook's promise: no bait and switch. Second, the viewer runs a rapid trust check: "is this person going to waste my time?"
- Four levers for that trust check: how you look; packaging quality (set, light, sound and visible care signal trust); relatability (people trust people like them: Gen Z trusts Gen Z on AI, an accountant should look like an accountant); and the strongest one, proof.
- Proof works as a "trust anchor" right after the hook: a study, a case, a screenshot.
- Kallaway (packaging tips): the intro must "confirm the click" by repeating the title's promise in the first seconds.

### No clickbait lies

- Mino Lee: does the hook match the video idea, or are you rage-baiting? If you get people to second 3 and the video is something else, the hook lied. "Clickbait isn't actually that effective." If you make a claim, the next lines must back it up truthfully.
- pmitu: stop writing clickbait. Getting the click isn't the goal, keeping attention is. If the headline promises and the content doesn't deliver, people leave and the algorithm punishes you. "Write hooks that are strong, but accurate."
- Shaan Puri (with Ship 30 for 30): "there's no such thing as clickbait headlines, only clickbait content". If you pay off the promise, nobody minds the hook. "Want to know a secret?" followed by "work hard" is a broken promise.
- Sam Gaudet's claim: a mismatch between the packaging promise (title, thumbnail) and the hook is the biggest cause of drop-off today. "This isn't 2016 anymore", you can't clickbait people into staying. What breaks it: a "how to make money" title followed by a story about doing a puzzle with your grandma.
- Logan Smith: the progression must meet the expectations the intro set. If you promise a snowball fight in the desert, show the trip to get the snow, don't pull ice out of a cooler.
- Kallaway: the payoff (the Z in "don't do Y, do Z") must be real, or people churn and see you as a fraud (lesson 2).

### Don't pay off too early

- Logan Smith: a short opens "MrBeast shared the key secret to his success on YouTube", then reveals the secret ("make 100 videos and improve something every time") 5 seconds in. No anticipation is left, so viewers scroll. The secret should come at the end. Delivery matters as much as the idea.
- heyDominik: reveal only at the end (the blurred blueberries in lesson 9).
- Jenny Hoyos: start with a question and build toward the answer. Make the answer feel uncertain until the last second.
- heyDominik: keep loops open as long as possible, and open a new one when you close one.

### But give value fast

- Kallaway: compress speed to value. Give a first hit of value in the hook or right after it, because anything after people leave "might as well be a black screen".
- His value loop: context, immediate value, context, immediate value. If the first hit is unique and helpful, viewers usually stay for the second.
- pmitu (video): getting a tap isn't enough if they leave right away. Start with the strongest part, remove long intros, get straight to the point.
- XCreators: grab attention in the first three seconds and hold it for the full 60.

### Frameworks

**The lock-in zone** (Kallaway)

1. Confirm the hook's promise in the next 2 to 4 sentences
2. Pass the trust check: look, packaging quality, relatability
3. Drop a trust anchor (proof) right after the hook

### Examples

- **Logan Smith, the MrBeast secret short:** "MrBeast shared the key secret to his success on YouTube", then the secret 5 seconds in.
  <br>*Why it works:* Paying off at second 5 left nothing to wait for, so viewers scrolled. The reveal belonged at the end.
- **Shaan Puri, the broken promise:** "Want to know a secret?" followed by "work hard".
  <br>*Why it works:* The hook isn't the problem. The content that fails to pay it off is.

### Common mistakes

- A hook that promises one thing and a video that delivers another.
- Making a claim and not backing it up in the next lines (Mino Lee).
- Revealing the main answer seconds after the hook (Logan Smith).
- Long intros between the hook and the first piece of value.
- No proof in the lock-in zone, so the viewer's trust check fails.

### Where the sources disagree

When to give the answer. Kallaway says front-load value: give a first hit of value in the hook or right after it, then keep alternating context and value. Logan Smith, heyDominik and Jenny Hoyos say hold the main reveal until the end, because paying off early kills anticipation. Kallaway's own value loop keeps opening new loops after each hit of value, and the others agree you can close a loop as long as you open the next one.

### Try it

Take a hook you like and write the next 3 sentences. Sentence 1 must restate the hook's promise in different words. Sentence 2 must give one piece of proof (a number, a result, a screenshot you could show). Sentence 3 must give a first small piece of value while leaving the main answer for later. Cut anything that is warm-up.

## 2.11 Copy, understand, maximize: building hook and keyword banks

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Mino Lee's way to get good at hooks fast: copy hooks from proven videos in your format, understand which emotional keywords made them work, then maximize your own hook with a checklist and many rewrites.

### Step 1: copy viral hooks

- Mino Lee: "I don't care what anybody says about being original." You won't learn to go viral if you haven't copied and studied why others do well.
- The goal is to speedrun your "viral sense", your understanding of why what works works. Blaming luck or "the algorithm doesn't like me" denies you the learning.
- Study on TikTok first: he says its search and For You algorithm are more refined than Instagram's, and what works on TikTok generally works on Instagram because both measure engagement and watch time.
- Only study videos with 50K+ likes. "Train your data set with the best data": think of your brain as a model trained on quality data points. 10K+ is okay, but make sure every hook you study went very viral.
- Only study videos in your format (talking head, vlog, music montage, how-to, list, explaining vs vlogging) and in your niche or related niches.
- Format matters more than niche because format is more universal. A college day-in-the-life creator can study 9-to-5 day-in-the-life videos. He studies dating and fitness talking videos for his business talking content. Don't study cooking montages to make persuasive talking videos.
- Write every hook down: that is your hook bank. Then mash hooks together with a list of ideas. His example: "If you are tired of throwing events nobody pulls up to, here's the ultimate guide to throwing bangers with thousands of people. Take notes and thank me later." He calls it "stealing like an artist".
- Logan Smith agrees: "never be afraid to imitate other creators". Learning from successful channels is a key part of improving.

### Step 2: understand why it went viral (keywords)

- Copying alone won't make a viral video. You also need to understand and maximize.
- Ask which keywords made it go viral. Here a keyword does NOT mean SEO. It means words that trigger emotion in your viewer. No single word makes or breaks it; it's the combination.
- Level-one thinking: "he's walking, so I'll walk in my videos". Level two: knowing which words triggered the emotion.
- Examples he breaks down: "You scared of talking to women? I hate to break it to you, but it doesn't matter if you get buff..." (keywords: scared of talking to women, I hate to break it to you, then common objections). "I passed the last year of law school with high honors despite being an absolute academic assault rifle and procrastinating literally every exam" (law school, high honors, academic assault rifle). In the social media niche: "200 view jail", "algo pull".
- Build a keyword bank for your niche. His productivity examples: dopamine detox, productivity hacks, morning routine, 6 a.m., 5 a.m., "lock in" (in his experience it lands better than "mental health" in that niche), doom scrolling, zero motivation, scrolling.
- Avoid broad words like "get better" or "improve". Words that work can depend on the age group. You can also search a word on YouTube and study the titles that use it.
- Relatable wording means how people actually talk. "Here's how to break your [bad] habits. Clean your environment." beats "here's how to develop better habits / become consistent", because people say the first to friends. Relatable doesn't mean "I'm a relatable human". It means wording that hits emotionally, like talking to a friend and not holding back. Casual, but specific.
- His template case (his claim: 0 to 100,000 followers in one month on a second account): one hook did well, "Here's how to stop waking up in the morning, looking in the mirror, and hating what you see. Stop using your phone." He reused it as "Here's how to [blank]" with niche keywords injected.
- His opinion: "Day 1 of road to 10K a month" or "Day 17 of road to millionaire" is not a good hook, even though it copies one that went viral once, because it doesn't play into the core principles.

### Step 3: maximize (his checklist)

- Clarity: is this the clearest, most concise wording? Rewrite and revise. His claim: the difference between 5,000 and 100,000 views (or 100K and 1M) can be one or two words at the start.
- Keywords that add specificity.
- Specificity and numbers ("10K a month", "\$2,000 a day", "67K per week"; the XK-per-day pattern shows up again and again in titles). A personal result with a number is even better.
- Alignment: does it match the video, or is it rage bait?
- Who is your ideal viewer?
- The purple cow effect: novelty stands out. When driving you don't notice most cows and cars, but you'd notice a purple cow far away.
- Visuals: can they make the hook easier to understand or more captivating?
- Read it aloud five times. Does it feel right and make sense, or is it still "iffy"?

### Other creators on swipe files

- Codie Sanchez: build a swipe file of the top 100 posts in your niche and save the hook, the first 30 seconds and the top comments. Steal the hook, never the content, and pour your own expertise in.
- Kallaway (idea mining): the hook (spoken, text and visual) is one of 7 "Lego bricks" in every idea. Break outliers into bricks, "hold 2, remix 5", and look in the same content type, because a tweet's hook and structure won't carry over to a video. He keeps a "long-term patterns" doc of hooks, structures and formats that keep showing up for weeks.
- Dan Koe: every month rewrite your best posts from new angles, and one of those angles is simply a better hook.
- Caleb Ralston: in his 70/20/10 rule, the 20% is iterating on what already works, and changing the hook is one of those iterations.
- Mino Lee's warning: learning hooks can make you overthink and stop posting ("I won't post for a week until I figure out a good hook"). Constant learning without posting is "edging your learning". Focus on daily iteration with feedback.

### Frameworks

**Copy, Understand, Maximize** (Mino Lee)

1. Copy: write down hooks only from very viral videos (50K+ likes) in your format, into a hook bank
2. Understand: find the emotional keywords that made each one work, into a keyword bank
3. Maximize: run your hook through clarity, keywords, numbers, alignment, ideal viewer, novelty, visuals, and read it aloud five times

### Examples

- **Mino Lee, a live rewrite chain:** "If you're tired of running your business 24/7 by yourself" (wordy, no keywords) → "alone" instead of "by yourself" → add "10K a month" → "burnt out running your business 24/7 alone" → "going broke running a dying business alone" → he searches YouTube for "passive income" titles (\$2,000 a day, 67K per week) → "the ultimate guide to building a passive income machine, fully automated with AI" → swap to "AI agents", a hot phrase, which makes "with AI" redundant → final: "This is the ultimate guide to start using AI agents to build a passive income machine. Take notes and thank me later."
  <br>*Why it works:* One word at a time, each swap adds emotion or specificity. He says the follow-up line must back the claim, and only if true ("Thousands of kids just like you are using AI agents to make 10K per month but you're just too lazy to set it up"). He calls "Take notes and thank me later" a small "super hook".
- **Mino Lee, the reusable template:** "Here's how to stop waking up in the morning, looking in the mirror, and hating what you see. Stop using your phone." Reused as "Here's how to [blank]" with niche keywords.
  <br>*Why it works:* One proven hook shape, refilled with the emotional words of the niche.

### Common mistakes

- Studying hooks from average videos, or from a different format.
- Copying the surface ("he walks, so I'll walk") instead of the emotional keywords.
- Broad, flat words like "improve" and "get better".
- Copying a hook shape that went viral once but breaks the core principles ("Day 17 of road to millionaire").
- Studying hooks so much you stop posting (Mino's "edging your learning").

### Where the sources disagree

How much to copy. Mino Lee says he doesn't care what anybody says about being original, copy hooks from viral videos to train your viral sense, and Logan Smith says never be afraid to imitate. Ryan says his number one advice is to create new, original concepts, formats, angles and hooks, take inspiration but "tread very carefully when copying others", and be careful chasing views through pure trend-jacking. Codie Sanchez and Kallaway sit between: steal the hook or format, never the content.

### Try it

Find 5 videos or posts in your exact format with very high engagement. Write each hook in a list (your hook bank). Underline the words that carry emotion and copy them to a second list (your keyword bank). Then take one of your own hooks and rewrite it 5 times, changing one or two words each time using your keyword bank. Read the last version aloud five times.

## 2.12 Sam Gaudet's hook toolkit: proof, promise, plan

*10 min read*

> **The big idea:** Sam Gaudet (Dan Martell's creative director) boils every hook down to proof, promise and plan. He expands it into a full long-form opening, a 3 C's short-form hook, and a set of small moves like the stranger test, self-articulation and signaling.

### Proof, promise, plan ("the triple P")

- After studying Dan Martell's best long and short videos, Sam boils every hook down to 3 parts. Any order, but all 3. They answer the 3 questions every viewer has.
- Proof: why should I listen to you? (You are qualified to talk about it.)
- Promise: what is this about, what do I get? It must match the promise in the title and thumbnail. His claim: a mismatch between the packaging promise and the hook is the biggest cause of drop-off today.
- Plan: how will you get me there? (In his "7 Strategies" video, the 7 strategies, shown on screen at the start.)
- He uses it at the start of every one of his videos.

### The long-form hook: reassure, elevate, open a loop

- His expanded version for YouTube. You have the first 30 seconds. "This isn't 2016 anymore", you can't clickbait people into staying.
- Reassure, promise: a clear promise that matches the title and thumbnail. In the real Dan script: "the six phases to scale your business from 0 to million dollar in 12 months".
- Reassure, proof: answer "why you?" fast. Dan's: from 80-hour weeks to under 5 hours a week, 8 figures a year.
- Reassure, handle objections: kill "this won't work for me". Dan's: "the same six phases I took over 10,000 of my coaching clients through".
- Elevate, scale the promise: extras, free bonuses, teases. Dan's: a free template to find your phase.
- Elevate, urgency: make it a now thing, time-based or tied to something new like an AI release. Dan's: "with the rise of AI, building a business is the only viable way to make a million dollars in 2026".
- Open a loop, road map: show the steps on screen, blurred, so people want to see them. Dan's: the 6 phases listed, blurred.
- Open a loop, unique mechanism: something "familiar but unknown" with a name. Dan's: "the 10-80-10 rule".
- What breaks it: a "how to make money" title, then a story about doing a puzzle with your grandma. People leave.

### The short-form hook: the 3 C's

- Every short-form hook gives context (what this is about), a contrarian take, and opens a curiosity loop so people stay to the end.
- His example (his claim: over 200,000 views): "trial reels aren't dead, they're just misunderstood". Context: it is about trial reels. Contrarian: at the time people said they were dead. Open loop: "misunderstood" makes you ask what you don't understand.
- In his hook-explain-illustrate-teach (HEIT) structure for short videos, the hook is context, contrarian take and open loop, plus proof, promise and plan.

### Write for the stranger

- The stranger test: most people who see you don't know you. The first 3 seconds of every video must interest a stranger with no context who doesn't know who you are.
- Start with a problem that hits everyone. Dan's meetings video opens on the claim that meetings are the biggest time waster in any company.
- Reintroduce yourself, every time: because most viewers are strangers, at the start of every video Sam says who he is, what he's done and why you should listen.

### Small moves that sharpen a hook

- Self-articulation: say what people think but can't put into words. The best compliment he gets: "you literally read my mind".
- His coaching example: a business owner opened with "if you want to build a bigger team, you have to do X". Sam stopped him: most owners don't want a bigger team, they want more money and more impact. Lead with that outcome. Hiring can be the method inside the video, never the opener.
- Labeling vs signaling: labeling names the person ("if you're in your 20s", "if you're a seven-figure business owner"), and it works. Signaling works better: phrases only that person would understand. His example: "build a personal brand for your business" makes every business owner lean in.
- Old way vs new way: "you can't clickbait people into watching YouTube anymore, it's not 2016". It tells people the platform changed and gives weight to what comes next. He uses it all the time.
- Avoid CUB: anything you write (titles, posts, threads) should not be Confusing, Unbelievable or Boring.
- Named things hook people: a unique mechanism with a name (like "the 10-80-10 rule").
- Jenny Hoyos makes a related point, "you first, me second": "here's how you should train for your next marathon, because this is what I'm doing" beats "I'm training for a marathon". She says Dan Martell does it too: the tip, how you apply it, then his story. She also says to start almost at the climax, with no context ("immediate start"), and calls selfish content (talking about yourself, not their gain) the number one mistake.

### Frameworks

**Proof, promise, plan** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Proof: why should I listen to you?
2. Promise: what is this about and what do I get (matching the title and thumbnail)?
3. Plan: how will you get me there?

**Long-form opening: reassure, elevate, open a loop** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Reassure: promise, proof, handle objections
2. Elevate: scale the promise with extras, add real urgency
3. Open a loop: a blurred road map and a named unique mechanism

**The 3 C's (short form)** (Sam Gaudet)

1. Context: what this is about
2. Contrarian take: what most people get wrong
3. Curiosity loop: a question that keeps them to the end

### Examples

- **Sam Gaudet, his own video openings (his view counts, openings paraphrased):** Feels Like Cheating (28k): a way to get so many followers it feels like cheating, and it's not talent, luck or gear, it's 10 simple principles. Disgustingly Good (147k): I know a thing or two about content, the fastest growing brand in business plus my own 0 to 75K in 9 months, how to get so good it feels disgusting. Trial Reels (9.2k): trial reels aren't dead, you just don't understand how they work now. 6 Months (4.1k): you're 6 months away from blowing up, the only thing holding you back is starting. Cult Brand (3.2k): followers don't matter anymore if you want a cult-like brand. 50 Hacks (4k): built the fastest growing personal brand in business, my own 0 to 50K, 50 hacks to build yours fast.
  <br>*Why it works:* The pattern across his videos: a claim about the topic, a proof line in the first 15 seconds, then the promise, then a numbered road map. Other moves: removing excuses ("not talent, luck or gear"), a time frame, contrarian callouts.

### Common mistakes

- A hook whose promise doesn't match the title or thumbnail (Sam's biggest cause of drop-off).
- Leading with the method ("build a bigger team") instead of the outcome people actually want (more money, more impact).
- Assuming viewers know who you are.
- Hooks that are confusing, unbelievable or boring (CUB).
- Opening with yourself instead of the viewer's gain (Jenny Hoyos).

### Try it

Write a 3-sentence opening for your next piece using proof, promise and plan: one line on why you're qualified, one line on what they'll get, one line on how ("3 steps", "5 mistakes"). Then check it with the stranger test: would someone who has never seen you care in the first 3 seconds? Swap any label ("if you're a founder") for a signal only that person would recognise.

## 2.13 Ryan's hook system for X posts

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Ryan (ryangrowthh) teaches hooks for written posts on X: 8 hook types, 5 things every great hook taps into, a set of rules, where the hook sits in each format, and a process of writing the hook last in many versions.

### The job of the first line

- Ryan: "The only job of your first line is to get someone to read your second line." The main goal is a curiosity gap that makes people feel spoken to.
- From his videos: "on X curiosity gaps are everything". You need people to think "I need to watch this".
- Titles: big promise, clear outcome, no confusion. "If someone even has to think about what your title means, you've lost them."
- For video, the hook is also the visuals and the speaker: clean background, editing, and text on screen that repeats the hook.
- In almost every video he plans "a hook that only I can say": built from his own result, which nobody else can copy.
- He says being great at hooks is "very hard to find" in a head of content.

### 8 hook types

- From Distribution 101 (5 types): bold claim, callout, curiosity gap, clear outcome, contrarian. A good hook does at least one.
- From his copywriting masterclass (3 more): story ("It took me 8 years to realize this..."), number or list ("7 copywriting rules I wish I knew 6 years ago"), proof ("This post got 2M views. I analyzed why").
- The best hooks combine several types.

### The 5 things every great hook taps into

- Self-interest: outcomes, money, time saved.
- Curiosity.
- Novelty.
- Emotion: surprise, fear, FOMO, excitement.
- Identity: "this is about people like me".
- The strongest hooks stack 2 or 3. His example: a founder callout (identity) plus "this one change doubled my reach" (self-interest plus curiosity).

### His hook rules

- Lead with the most interesting thing and cut the warm-up.
- Be specific: numbers, names and timeframes. "I grew fast" vs "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month".
- Make it about the reader ("what's in it for me?").
- Keep it short.
- Create tension: open a loop the reader needs closed (a question, a surprise, a contradiction).
- If your best line is at the bottom, move it to the top.

### Where the hook sits in each format

- Short post: the hook IS the post. Line one must stand alone.
- Thread: the hook plus a promise of what's coming ("Here are 7 lessons:").
- Article: the title, subtitle and first paragraph are each their own hook.
- Video: the first 1 to 2 seconds, movement plus text on screen plus spoken hook.
- Quote post: your take adds tension or value, not just "this".
- Image: the headline on the image, one idea, readable on a phone.

### His hook process

- Write the post first and the hook last.
- Write 5 to 10 versions using different hook types. "Write 3 hooks minimum."
- Cut the weakest right away.
- Rework until each line rolls into the next, and cut words that repeat or add nothing.
- "Put more effort into hooks than the post body itself."
- Patterns in his own hooks: the proof number sits in line 2; he asks for the bookmark in the hook ("bookmark this:"); brackets like "Full crash course", "(actually)", "In Depth", "(Dense Guide)"; the word "underrated"; a prediction aimed at the reader ("someone reading this is going to blow up"); his article pitched as a prompt for an AI; a meme reaction quote on top of his own article.
- More of his openers: a plan hook ("If I had 90 days to make \$10k/mo from a personal brand on X, this is what I'd do (step-by-step):"), a clear money outcome with the content in an image ("All you need in 2026 to make \$10k/mo as a creator on X:"), a proof screenshot with the result hidden in brackets ("(first 30d of posting btw)"), and "the smart ones will...", which flatters the reader into acting.

### Frameworks

**Ryan's 8 hook types** (Ryan (ryangrowthh))

1. Bold claim
2. Callout
3. Curiosity gap
4. Clear outcome
5. Contrarian
6. Story
7. Number / list
8. Proof

**The 5 things a hook taps into** (Ryan (ryangrowthh))

1. Self-interest
2. Curiosity
3. Novelty
4. Emotion
5. Identity
6. Stack 2 or 3 in one hook

"Distribution is THE only moat left. The next wave of millionaires will be those who mass-distribute their brand... bookmark this:" (170k, 3,029). "Grok Bot is basically AGI for X content. It helped me generate ~2M views in my first 2 weeks. Here are 10 cool use cases:" (146k, 3,479). "~14 hours ago, X updated the algorithm (again)." (138k). "I debated if I should've even posted this." (110k). "Most people think building a personal brand takes 6+ months. It doesn't, and I proved it by building one in 9 days." (32k).
  <br>*Why it works:* Clear outcome plus "full" plus chapters showing the value upfront; bold claim plus money plus a bookmark ask; bold claim with proof in line 2 and a numbered list; timely news with an exact time; curiosity that feels like a secret; common belief, then proof.
- **Ryan, his newer hooks:** "The X algorithm, explained. Someone reading this is going to blow up." (423k views, 8,158 bookmarks, his most bookmarked post). "Explaining how the X algorithm (actually) works. Once you understand this, you can reverse-engineer virality." (393k). "The next wave of millionaires will be 20-26 y/o cracked marketers who grew up on the internet." (21k). "I'm begging you:" plus a 10-line list (32k).
  <br>*Why it works:* A short label plus a prediction aimed at the reader; "(actually)" promises the real version plus a clear payoff; bold claim plus identity; a begging opener, then pure tips.

### Common mistakes

- Warming up before the most interesting line.
- Vague claims ("I grew fast") with no numbers, names or timeframes.
- Titles that make the reader think about what they mean.
- Quote posts that only say "this".
- Writing only one hook. Ryan says 3 minimum, ideally 5 to 10.

## 2.14 Hooks in writing: first lines for posts, threads and articles

*9 min read*

> **The big idea:** Writers teach the same core as video creators, in text form: the first line exists to get the next one read, it must be written and rewritten by a human, and the strongest article openings follow a handful of proven shapes.

### Shaan Puri, Dickie Bush and Nicolas Cole (Viral Writing 101)

- Tweet 1's only job is to get tweet 2 read. Most drop-off in a thread happens in the first 2 to 3 tweets; after that people finish.
- Pick the emotion before you write. At the top of his doc Shaan writes the target: LOL, WTF, "finally someone said it", aww, wow. "If they feel nothing at the end, it's going nowhere." Best of all is one take that triggers 2 opposite emotions (fans think "finally someone said it", the other side thinks "WTF").
- Reusable hook shapes: the mythbuster ("everyone thinks X, I think the opposite, here's how it all goes down"); "if you're out of the loop" (explain the thing everyone hears about but doesn't get); David vs Goliath; "PSA / let me do you a favor"; "mistakes I made / what I wish I knew 5 years ago" (speak from lessons, not superiority).
- Adding "I wish this weren't true" up front made Shaan's Clubhouse take hit harder.
- Write at a 2nd to 8th grade reading level. His viral Clubhouse thread was 2nd to 3rd grade; a smarter founder's version of the same take was 12th grade and died. No jargon: "yield a positive ROI" has zero emotion.
- Write the hook 20 times (even out loud), and spend 30 to 40% of your time on the first and last words.
- Timing: the Clubhouse take only worked at peak hype. A month later or 3 months earlier it would have flopped.

### Dan Koe on first sentences

- The hook is the first of six elements of persuasive content. Ask whether your first sentence will grab attention.
- It goes beyond the words: the structure and visual design of the post can grab attention too, even on X.
- For cold audiences, open with a desire ("if you want") or a pain ("if you don't want"). His article opener in that pattern: "If you have multiple interests, you probably have no idea what to do with your life."
- Starting with your own story is how you make a validated topic unique. He opened his personal brand video with his failure story for exactly that reason.

### Don't let AI write the first line

- Shann Holmberg: the first line is the only line everyone reads. The AI's default hook is almost always wrong and safe, and "safe doesnt stop the scroll". Rewrite it yourself.
- His editorial rule: rewrite the first two sentences of an AI draft even if the rest is solid, because the AI opens with something generic.
- His split: 80% AI (research, drafts, structure), 20% taste, and the hook is part of the human 20%.

### Matt Gray: study and audit hooks

- X is "a copywriting Olympics". Build a swipe file of the top 20 posts you couldn't help saving. His example is Naval's "how to get rich without getting lucky": a one-line hook, then short, easy lines.
- Audit your top 20% vs bottom 20% of posts (topics, hooks, words). His claim: that is how he learned specific numbers in hooks did "10x better".

### Opening shapes from top X articles

- A study of top X articles found these first-line shapes.
- Threat numbers first: real public numbers that scare the reader before the promise (one article opened on Amazon, Dell and Oracle layoffs).
- A hard truth about the reader: "Most people are forgettable." (Tim Denning); "Most creators will never make a full-time living from it." (Hussain).
- Proof before teaching: "I've generated 3B organic views..." (domschats); "I grew my X account from 400 to 26,000" (pmitu).
- The crowd is doing it wrong: "Right now 90% of people using AI are doing the same thing" (Shann).
- Who it's for plus a TL;DR on top (Ryan). Name the reader in the first line or with a "this IS for you if" list.
- A concrete pain scene: "Your launch gets 'congrats' from friends, a few likes from other founders, and then the conversation stops." (0xfJuan).

### First lines of emails and outreach

- Sam Gaudet: a hook is the first words of anything, including an email or a tweet.
- EXM7777 (cold outreach): first lines get read when you write about something that just happened at the company (they raised money, posted a job for the role you'd replace, hired a new head of marketing, their website hasn't changed in ages). Find the reason, note it, write a first line about them.

### Frameworks

**Shaan Puri's hook shapes** (Shaan Puri (with Ship 30 for 30))

1. Mythbuster: everyone thinks X, I think the opposite
2. If you're out of the loop: explain what everyone hears about but doesn't get
3. David vs Goliath
4. PSA / let me do you a favor
5. Mistakes I made / what I wish I knew 5 years ago

### Examples

- **Shaan Puri, the Clubhouse thread:** A take on Clubhouse at peak hype, written at a 2nd to 3rd grade reading level, opened with "I wish this weren't true".
  <br>*Why it works:* Simple words, a clear emotion, and perfect timing. A smarter founder's 12th grade version of the same take died.
- **Naval, via Matt Gray:** "How to get rich (without getting lucky)"
  <br>*Why it works:* A one-line hook followed by short, easy lines. Matt Gray holds his posts to that bar.

### Common mistakes

- Keeping the AI's default first line (Shann Holmberg).
- Jargon and high reading levels that carry no emotion (Shaan Puri).
- Writing the hook once instead of 20 times.
- Not knowing what emotion the reader should feel.
- Speaking from superiority instead of lessons.

### Try it

Pick a thread or article you want to write. Before writing it, choose the emotion you want (LOL, WTF, finally someone said it, aww, wow). Then write the first line 20 times, using at least three shapes: a mythbuster, a hard truth about the reader, and proof before teaching. Check the best one for reading level: could a 10-year-old read it?

## 2.15 Hooks across platforms, long form and testing

*8 min read*

> **The big idea:** The hook adapts to the platform: a long YouTube intro, a clip cut from a podcast, a UGC ad and a repost each need their own opening. The sources also show how to test and review hooks once they are live.

### Long-form openings

- Kallaway: the countdown before attention "explodes" is about 4 seconds in short form and maybe 1 to 2 minutes on YouTube.
- Kallaway (packaging tips): on YouTube, packaging is the title, thumbnail and first 30 to 60 seconds, and the intro must confirm the click by repeating the title's promise.
- Paddy Galloway's MrBeast breakdown: open fast (a visual change about every 1.1 seconds in the first 20 seconds), establish scale (zoom out to reveal the whole island, 100 people in a line), and reach the first challenge within about 17 seconds.
- Sam Gaudet's long-form opening is reassure, elevate, open a loop within the first 30 seconds (lesson 12).

### Clips, recordings and signature openers

- Kallaway's Diary of a CEO breakdown: the hook must be native to shorts, fast, punchy and straight to value. If the real moment has no punchy opening, re-record a hook afterwards in the same clothes.
- Oren (orenmeetsworld): re-record the intro at the END of a session when you're warmed up. His "strong take, then education" format uses the take only as the hook ("there are way too many Coachella influencers"), then teaches.
- Kallaway (speedrun video): a soft series has no numbers but every video feels like the same show, for example Orin's "Product vs Brand" always opens the same way. Kallaway's own "Check this out really quick" opener is the same idea (lesson 4).

### Ads, UGC and reposts

- EXM7777: for UGC, make the hook clip first and the rest after, because the hook's voice becomes the voice for every other clip.
- EXM7777: one clean video, then a different hook and caption per app. The same video can do great on Instagram and flop on TikTok, so track how many people stay past the first second and how much they watch, not just views.
- EXM7777 on ad research: ask a specific question in the Meta Ad Library ("which hooks are the best story ads in my niche using this month?"). For winning ads note the hook and the order of beats (hook, problem, how it works, proof, call to action). Copy the skeleton, never the script.
- Ernesto's theme-page tactic: a borrowed viral-style hook clip at the start (a Zack D Films clip as bait), then a snap cut into your own already-viral video. His claim: 10M+ views from videos that had already gone viral once.
- Kallaway's breakdown of Ava's video (lesson 6) shows the same move: her real hook was an already-validated viral clip placed on top of hers.

### Reviewing hooks after you post

- Codie Sanchez: read every post with the 3 S's. Start: did they stop? Stay: did it pay off fast? Signal: did they do anything? After each post write 3 notes: why they started, where they left, which line got a reaction. "If you can't name it, you can't repeat it."
- XCreators: break down saved inspiration videos with a template: why it worked, the hook (how it grabs in the first seconds), the pacing, the sound, and how to make it yours.
- XCreators: feed AI videos you find very engaging plus your own top performers, teach it what makes them work, and let it score scripts and flag likely drop-off points: "Hook is too slow." "You start teaching too early." They say content lives or dies on hooks, pacing, cadence and open loops.
- Matt Gray: audit your top 20% vs bottom 20% of posts by hook (lesson 14).
- Caleb Ralston's 70/20/10: 70% content you know works, 20% iterations on it such as a new hook, 10% new experiments.

### Frameworks

**The 3 S's for reading a post** (Codie Sanchez)

1. Start: did they stop?
2. Stay: did it pay off fast?
3. Signal: did they do anything?
4. Write 3 notes: why they started, where they left, which line got a reaction

**MrBeast intro pattern** (Paddy Galloway (breakdown))

1. Open fast: a visual change about every 1.1 seconds
2. Establish scale
3. Reach the first challenge within about 17 seconds

### Examples

- **Kallaway on Diary of a CEO clips:** When a strong podcast moment has no punchy opening, the host re-records a hook afterwards in the same clothes, and the clip carries a big text hook like "THE TRUTH ABOUT ADDICTION".
  <br>*Why it works:* The hook must be native to shorts even when the source is a long conversation.
- **EXM7777, one video, many hooks:** The same clean video goes to several apps, each with its own hook and caption, judged by how many stay past the first second.
  <br>*Why it works:* A video can win on one app and flop on another, so the hook is what you test per platform.

### Common mistakes

- Using a slow conversational opening as the hook of a short clip.
- The same hook and caption on every app with no testing.
- Copying a winning ad's script instead of its skeleton (EXM7777).
- Judging a post only by views, without noting where people stopped and left (Codie Sanchez).

### Try it

Take your last 5 posts or videos. For each, write Codie Sanchez's 3 notes: why people started, where they left, which line got a reaction. Then pick the weakest one and write 3 new hooks for it (lesson 4 formats or lesson 13 types). Repost or re-cut it with the best new hook when it fits your plan.

<a id="cheat-sheet"></a>

## Cheat sheet: Hooks

The whole module on one page.

- Say what it is about in the first sentence, in as few words as possible.
- Write the hook in 2 to 4 lines: context lean, "but", contrarian snapback.
- Only snap back to a Z you can really deliver.
- Lead with the benefit or the pain, not the name of the thing.
- Say "you" and "your", not "I" and "me".
- Frame around a need to have, not a nice to have.
- Build curiosity with contrast: what they believe now against your alternative.
- Start from the key visual, then pick the format that creates the most contrast with it.
- Make spoken, visual, text and audio say the same thing.
- 3 to 5 bold words on screen, a third down, off the face, about 3 seconds.
- Use short staccato sentences in the hook, longer ones later.
- Check every hook for subject, action, objective and contrast; add proof or time if they fit.
- Read the hook alone: if it can be understood two ways, rewrite it.
- Never give away the answer in the first frame.
- Confirm the promise and show proof in the next 2 to 4 sentences.
- Copy hooks only from very viral posts in your own format, and steal the hook, never the content.
- Write the hook last, in 5 to 20 versions, and read the best one aloud.
- Never keep the AI's default first line.
- After posting, note why people started, where they left, and which line got a reaction.

<details><summary>Kallaway: the two things every hook must give</summary>

Topic clarity (what it is about) and on-target curiosity (it is for me and I want what comes next).

</details>

<details><summary>Kallaway's master formula</summary>

Context lean, scroll stop interjection, contrarian snapback. Lean them in, stun them with "but", snap them the opposite way on the same topic.

</details>

<details><summary>The pattern under the master formula</summary>

"If you want to achieve X, don't do Y, do Z." The catch: Z must really be good.

</details>

<details><summary>Four ways to get a viewer leaning in</summary>

Common ground, a benefit or pain point, a metaphor that simplifies, or something mind-blowing (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>Culture hopping</summary>

Wrap an unknown idea in something known (a brand, celebrity, movement) so the viewer feels comfortable instead of dumb (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>Compress speed to value</summary>

About 4 seconds in short form, 1 to 2 minutes on YouTube. Give a first hit of value in or right after the hook (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>The six hook formats</summary>

Fortune teller, experimenter, teacher, magician, investigator, contrarian. Any idea fits all six (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>Visual pacifier</summary>

A passive on-screen activity (makeup, stacking cups, gameplay) that holds just enough attention so the viewer stays on the main point (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>The four components of a hook</summary>

Spoken, visual, text, audio. Max alignment between them is "the difference between 500 and 500,000 views" (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>The visual, audio, visual sandwich</summary>

Viewers see the visual and text, then hear the words, then look back at the visual to confirm. So start from the key visual (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>The six power words</summary>

Core: subject, action, objective, contrast. Optional: proof ("again"), time ("in 5 months") (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>The four hook mistakes, in order</summary>

Delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest. Fix them in that order (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>Kallaway's misinterpretation test</summary>

Read the hook alone: could it be understood in more than one way? If yes, rewrite.

</details>

<details><summary>Stated vs implied contrast</summary>

Stated says both A and B ("most use Accutane, this is 3x faster"). Implied says only B and lets the viewer supply A.

</details>

<details><summary>The lock-in zone</summary>

The 2 to 4 sentences after the hook: confirm the promise and pass the trust check, ideally with proof as a trust anchor (Kallaway).

</details>

<details><summary>Mino Lee's 3 steps</summary>

Copy (hooks from 50K+ like videos in your format), Understand (the emotional keywords), Maximize (checklist and rewrites).

</details>

<details><summary>A keyword, in Mino Lee's sense</summary>

Not SEO. A word that triggers emotion in your viewer, said the way people talk to friends.

</details>

<details><summary>Sam Gaudet's proof, promise, plan</summary>

Why listen to you, what do I get (matching the title), how will you get me there.

</details>

<details><summary>Sam Gaudet's 3 C's for short form</summary>

Context, contrarian take, curiosity loop. "Trial reels aren't dead, they're just misunderstood."

</details>

<details><summary>Labeling vs signaling (Sam Gaudet)</summary>

Labeling names the viewer ("if you're in your 20s"). Signaling uses phrases only they would understand, and works better.

</details>

<details><summary>Ryan's first-line rule</summary>

"The only job of your first line is to get someone to read your second line."

</details>

<details><summary>Ryan's 5 things a hook taps into</summary>

Self-interest, curiosity, novelty, emotion, identity. The strongest hooks stack 2 or 3.

</details>

<details><summary>Shaan Puri on clickbait</summary>

"There's no such thing as clickbait headlines, only clickbait content." Pay off the promise and nobody minds the hook.

</details>

<details><summary>Loewenstein's curiosity gap</summary>

Curiosity needs some knowledge first. A gap that is too big or too small doesn't pull (1994 study).

</details>
